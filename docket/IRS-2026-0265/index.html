What This Document Does

  • The IRS and Treasury propose to delete an existing tax-reporting rule that treats certain partnership related-party basis adjustment transactions as "transactions of interest."
  • If finalized, the proposal would remove the reporting obligations tied to that rule for transaction participants and material advisors.
  • The agencies also say they intend to let people treat the repeal as effective back to January 14, 2025, so the rule could be treated as if it never took effect.
  • The NPRM's main justification is burden reduction. IRS estimates the current rule would otherwise generate about 10,000 reportable basis adjustments per year and about $163 million in annual filing burden across Forms 8886 and 8918.

Why It Matters

  • This is a deregulatory move with real practical effect. It would eliminate a disclosure tool the IRS had only recently finalized in January 2025.
  • The agency gives a concrete paperwork-savings estimate, which is useful, but the record provided here is still fairly thin. There is no separate supporting attachment or visible methodology appendix showing how the 10,000-transaction estimate was built.
  • The proposal also makes a stronger move than simple repeal: it says the rule may be treated as though it never took effect. That raises transition and record questions for anyone who already complied, prepared to comply, or relied on Notice 2025-23.
  • Public comments in the cached docket split in familiar ways. Business-oriented commenters support repeal as a correction of an overbroad, burdensome reporting regime. Several individual commenters oppose repeal because they see the rule as a basic anti-abuse disclosure tool. The NPRM itself does not do much to reconcile those competing views with evidence.

Who Is Affected

  • Partnerships and partners involved in basis-adjustment transactions covered by the existing rule.
  • Material advisors, including law and accounting firms, that would otherwise file Form 8918 disclosures.
  • Small businesses and family-owned enterprises that may face compliance costs if the reporting rule remains in place.
  • Tax administration and enforcement staff, because repeal would reduce information the IRS might otherwise use to spot potentially abusive basis-shifting structures.

Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

  • The $163 million burden-reduction estimate may be hard to test from the public record. Why it matters: The headline case for repeal depends on assumptions about how many transactions would be reported, how many parties each transaction affects, and what each filing costs. Question to ask: What tax-return years, filters, and sensitivity ranges did IRS use to estimate 10,000 affected basis adjustments and the average participant/advisor counts?

  • The proposal says burden likely exceeds benefits, but it does not say much about the benefits side. Why it matters: Repealing a reporting rule is easier to evaluate when the agency explains what compliance costs bought in return, such as enforcement leads or deterrence. Question to ask: What information or compliance benefits did the agency expect from the original transaction-of-interest rule, and why are those benefits now judged smaller than the burden?

  • The NPRM does not seriously analyze narrower alternatives to full repeal. Why it matters: The criticism described in the notice could support a narrower fix, such as higher thresholds, tighter scope, or prospective-only relief, instead of wiping out the rule entirely. Question to ask: Why did Treasury and IRS not analyze targeted revisions that would preserve reporting for the highest-risk transactions while easing burden on ordinary-course deals?

  • Retroactive treatment deserves more explanation. Why it matters: The proposal says affected parties may treat the rule as if it never took effect, which can change expectations for records, prior filings, and enforcement treatment. Question to ask: How will the final rule handle parties that already complied, prepared disclosures, or changed behavior in response to the January 14, 2025 final rule and Notice 2025-23?

Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Major claim: compliance burden likely exceeds benefits
  • Underlying assumption: The agency has enough information about the compliance costs and enough confidence that the disclosure regime produces limited enforcement or deterrence value.
  • Failure scenario: If disclosures materially help detect abusive basis-shifting transactions, then burden alone does not show net benefit from repeal.
  • Procedural implication: Commenters can ask IRS to quantify enforcement benefits, deterrence value, and alternative narrower revisions before concluding repeal is superior.
Major claim: 10,000 basis adjustments would be reported
  • Underlying assumption: The underlying tax-return analysis is representative and the translation from threshold-crossing transactions to actual disclosures is reliable.
  • Failure scenario: If the underlying population is smaller, duplicates across participants are miscounted, or many transactions would not satisfy related-party and "transaction of interest" conditions, the burden estimate may be overstated.
  • Procedural implication: IRS should disclose methodology, sample year, and sensitivity bounds.
Major claim: most participants would report rather than analyze applicability
  • Underlying assumption: Regulated parties prefer over-reporting to threshold analysis because the compliance calculus is skewed toward disclosure.
  • Failure scenario: Sophisticated taxpayers and advisors may instead litigate, seek advice, or apply narrower related-party analysis, reducing actual reporting volume.
  • Procedural implication: The cost estimate depends heavily on an unsupported behavioral assumption.
Major claim: repeal can be treated as retroactive to January 14, 2025
  • Underlying assumption: Retroactive relief under section 7805(b)(7) is appropriate and does not create unaddressed enforcement or reliance problems.
  • Failure scenario: Retroactive nullification may erase data already expected from the rule and create inconsistent treatment among parties that already complied versus those who relied on Notice 2025-23.
  • Procedural implication: IRS should explain transition effects and record-retention consequences more directly.
