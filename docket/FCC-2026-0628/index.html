What This Document Does

  • The FCC is proposing two targeted updates to its 6 GHz unlicensed-device rules.
  • First, it would let automated frequency coordination (AFC) systems account for up to 6 dB of building entry loss (BEL) when they calculate channel and power availability for composite indoor/standard-power access points. In plain terms, the FCC wants certain indoor devices to get more usable power because walls and building materials can reduce the chance that signals will reach and interfere with licensed users outside.
  • Second, it would allow low-power indoor (LPI) access points to operate on cruise ships, even though the current rules generally prohibit these devices on boats.
  • The FCC says both changes could improve indoor Wi-Fi performance while keeping harmful interference risk low. For the BEL proposal, the FCC relies heavily on prior waiver practice by its Office of Engineering and Technology. For the cruise-ship proposal, it relies on two main ideas: cruise ships are limited in number, and their thick metal structures may significantly attenuate signals.
  • The notice also asks broader questions about whether the FCC should update any other 6 GHz unlicensed rules in light of technological and business developments since 2020.

Why It Matters

  • This proposal matters because 6 GHz Wi-Fi is increasingly important for high-capacity indoor connectivity. If the FCC is right, the changes could improve performance in homes, buildings, and dense passenger environments like cruise ships.
  • But the most important comment opportunities are not just pro- or anti-Wi-Fi expansion. They are about whether the FCC has shown enough support for the exact thresholds and boundaries it chose.
  • The BEL proposal depends on several linked assumptions: that AFC systems can reliably identify eligible composite devices, that 6 dB is an appropriate attenuation value for rulewide use, and that real-world indoor deployments will behave roughly the way the FCC expects.
  • The cruise-ship proposal raises a similar issue. The FCC may be right that cruise ships are different from smaller boats, but the available notice text only partly explains why the proposed carveout should stop exactly there.
  • The economic discussion is also worth attention. The FCC estimates a one-time \$4,800 savings from avoiding additional waiver work and an annual \$35.6 million benefit from improved connectivity on cruise ships. At the same time, it says the proposal would not impose substantial costs because many implementation choices would be voluntary. That framing may undercount real costs that operators, equipment makers, or smaller entities would still face in practice.
  • This is still an NPRM, so some incompleteness is normal. The FCC is explicitly asking for evidence. That means the best public comments are likely to be technical, comparative, and practical rather than rhetorical.

Who Is Affected

  • AFC operators that would need to implement, validate, and defend BEL-based calculations.
  • Device manufacturers seeking certification for composite indoor/standard-power access points.
  • Licensed incumbent users in the 6 GHz band, including parties concerned about aggregate interference risk.
  • Cruise ship operators and onboard network providers that may install or expand 6 GHz Wi-Fi systems.
  • Smaller entities and suppliers that may face testing, certification, or implementation costs that the notice does not quantify in much detail.
  • Passengers and building users who could benefit if the FCC's interference assumptions are correct and the resulting indoor coverage gains are real.

Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

  1. The FCC should show more of the engineering basis for the 6 dB BEL threshold. Why it matters: 6 dB is central to the proposal, but the public-facing notice relies heavily on prior waiver experience rather than fully surfacing the technical basis in this record. Practical question a commenter can ask: What data, modeling assumptions, or sensitivity analysis show that 6 dB is protective across realistic indoor building conditions?

  2. The FCC should explain how it will verify that BEL-adjusted AFC treatment is used only for eligible indoor composite devices. Why it matters: The interference case depends not just on indoor attenuation, but also on whether AFC systems can accurately identify and constrain the right devices. Practical question a commenter can ask: What audit, enforcement, or misuse-detection mechanisms would confirm that devices benefiting from BEL treatment are actually certified and operating as assumed?

  3. The FCC should better justify the cruise-ship-only carveout. Why it matters: The notice gives reasons for treating cruise ships differently, but the line between cruise ships and other vessels may look arbitrary if the final record does not compare attenuation, passenger density, and interference risk more carefully. Practical question a commenter can ask: Why is the proposed 33 CFR 101.105 cruise-ship definition a better fit than a more engineering-based standard tied to vessel construction and expected signal attenuation?

  4. The FCC should provide a fuller accounting of implementation costs. Why it matters: The notice quantifies benefits more readily than costs and treats several likely expenses as non-substantial because adoption is voluntary. Practical question a commenter can ask: What real costs would AFC operators, cruise operators, device makers, and smaller entities face for system changes, testing, installation, and compliance if these rules are adopted?

  5. The FCC should commit to stronger post-adoption monitoring. Why it matters: If the proposal is built on engineering assumptions about attenuation and low interference risk, the final rule should include a way to detect whether those assumptions hold up in practice. Practical question a commenter can ask: What reporting, field validation, or review triggers would let the FCC revisit the rule if unexpected interference patterns emerge?

Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Claim A

Claim: Letting AFC systems account for BEL for indoor composite access points will improve indoor performance without materially increasing interference risk.

Underlying assumption: AFC systems can reliably distinguish eligible indoor composite devices from outdoor standard-power devices and can apply BEL in a way that still protects incumbents.

Failure scenario: If indoor-status verification is weak, FCC ID and equipment-class matching are incomplete, or real-world building attenuation differs materially from the assumed values, the interference margin could be smaller than the notice suggests.

Procedural implication: Commenters can ask the FCC to explain what verification, audit, and misuse-detection methods support the assumption that BEL-adjusted calculations will remain confined to genuinely eligible indoor devices.

Claim B

Claim: A 6 dB BEL allowance is an appropriate threshold for codification.

Underlying assumption: The waiver record and engineering basis supporting 6 dB is transferable from case-specific approvals to generally applicable rule text.

Failure scenario: If actual building attenuation varies widely by structure, or if some AFC operators use different modeling choices, a single uniform 6 dB rule could be over- or under-inclusive.

Procedural implication: The FCC should show why 6 dB is not just administratively convenient but technically justified across expected deployment conditions.

Claim C

Claim: Cruise ships can be carved out from the boat prohibition because they are few in number and have high attenuation from thick metal walls.

Underlying assumption: Cruise ships are sufficiently distinct from other boats in both attenuation and usage density to justify a categorical exception.

Failure scenario: If attenuation varies meaningfully across vessel designs, or if some other passenger vessels have similar use cases, the chosen cruise-ship boundary may appear arbitrary.

Procedural implication: Commenters can ask for a clearer record on vessel characteristics, alternative definitions, and why the proposed 33 CFR 101.105 definition is the best fit for the interference rationale.

Claim D

Claim: The proposal creates little or no public cost.

Underlying assumption: Voluntary adoption and producer-surplus framing are enough to avoid counting installation, compliance, and operational costs as meaningful regulatory burdens.

Failure scenario: If cruise operators, AFC operators, or smaller equipment makers face real implementation, testing, or compliance costs, the zero-cost framing may understate economically relevant burdens.

Procedural implication: This is a useful comment target because the notice quantifies benefits but treats several practical cost categories as effectively non-costs.
