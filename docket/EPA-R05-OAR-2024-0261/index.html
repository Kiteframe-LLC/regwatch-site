What This Document Does

  • EPA proposes to approve Illinois' regional haze plan for the second implementation period and, in the same action, approve visibility-related portions of older Illinois infrastructure SIP submissions for the 2012 PM2.5 and 2015 ozone standards.
  • The proposal says Illinois used a screening method based on emissions divided by distance to Class I areas (Q/d) to decide which sources needed deeper review.
  • Illinois screened down to 30 units, excluded retired units and some units with fuel switches or existing enforceable controls, and then performed four-factor analyses for nine units at Prairie State, Southern Illinois Power Cooperative Marion, Rain CII Carbon, Holcim/Lafarge Grand Chain, and Archer Daniels Midland.
  • EPA also says projected 2028 visibility conditions for the affected Class I areas are below both adjusted and unadjusted uniform-rate-of-progress glidepaths.

Why It Matters

  • Regional haze plans determine whether major industrial and power-sector sources must add new pollution controls to improve visibility in national parks and wilderness areas.
  • This proposal is important because it is largely an approval action. EPA is saying Illinois has already done enough, or enough is already enforceable, for this planning period.
  • For commenters, the leverage point is explanation quality. The docket has a real technical spine, but EPA still needs to show why its screening thresholds, existing-control findings, and cost-effectiveness calls are persuasive.
  • If those judgments are thinly explained, commenters can push EPA to add analysis, sensitivity testing, and clearer comparisons before finalizing the approval.

Who Is Affected

  • Illinois power plants, industrial boilers, kilns, and similar sources that were screened for regional haze review.
  • Owners and operators of units treated as already adequately controlled under Illinois' existing standards and permits.
  • Facilities that received four-factor analyses, especially Prairie State, Southern Illinois Power Cooperative Marion, Rain CII Carbon, Holcim/Lafarge Grand Chain, and Archer Daniels Midland.
  • People and organizations concerned with visibility at affected Class I areas in multiple states.
  • Environmental groups, nearby communities, and technical commenters with expertise in source controls, emissions trends, and air-quality modeling.

Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

  • Issue: The proposal states the Q/d screening thresholds, but it does not clearly explain why those cutoffs are the right ones. Why it matters: The thresholds determine which sources get a full reasonable-progress review. If the cutoff is underexplained, important sources may have been left out without a transparent reason. Question to ask: What sensitivity analysis did EPA or Illinois perform to show that modestly different Q/d thresholds would not identify additional meaningful sources?

  • Issue: The record gives cost-per-ton figures for several control options, but the decision rule for calling them not necessary is only lightly explained. Why it matters: Cost-effectiveness is central to rejecting new controls. Commenters need to know whether EPA used a consistent benchmark or source-specific balancing framework. Question to ask: What comparative benchmark or policy logic did EPA use to conclude that the listed control costs are too high to require for reasonable progress?

  • Issue: Several units are treated as already effectively controlled based on existing standards and emissions history. Why it matters: That can be reasonable, but only if recent unit-level performance really shows stable future control. Annual averages alone may hide variability or worsening conditions. Question to ask: Can EPA provide recent unit-specific emissions-rate and compliance data showing why existing controls at Baldwin, Newton, Powerton, and Kincaid are sufficient through 2028?

  • Issue: EPA says projected 2028 visibility conditions are below both adjusted and unadjusted glidepaths, but the public-facing uncertainty discussion is thin in the proposal text. Why it matters: The favorable modeling outcome is a major support for approval. If that outcome depends on assumptions that are not easy to see, commenters should ask EPA to spell them out. Question to ask: What are the key sensitivity or uncertainty drivers in the 2028 modeling, and how would less favorable assumptions affect the approval analysis?

  • Issue: The proposal also approves visibility-related portions of older infrastructure SIP submissions, but the bridge from the regional haze record to those approvals is brief. Why it matters: EPA should explain exactly how the current technical record resolves the separate visibility-prong obligations for the 2012 PM2.5 and 2015 ozone submissions. Question to ask: What specific analysis in this docket supports the section 110(a)(2)(D)(i)(II) approvals, apart from the general regional haze discussion?

Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Claim A

Claim: The Q/d screening method is a reasonable way to decide which Illinois sources merit four-factor review.

Underlying assumption: Using 2016 inventory data and a Q/d threshold of four at facilities above 10 remains representative enough for 2028 visibility impacts.

Failure scenario: The 2016 base year or the threshold values miss materially important sources or newer operating patterns, especially where retirements, fuel switches, or operational changes alter comparative impacts.

Procedural implication: Commenters could ask EPA to justify the threshold numerically, show sensitivity to alternative thresholds, or explain why a source outside the screen would not matter.

Claim B

Claim: Existing controls at Baldwin, Newton, Powerton, and Kincaid are already sufficient for reasonable progress in the second implementation period.

Underlying assumption: Historic emissions trends and enforceable MPS/CPS or permit limits are a reliable proxy for future performance through 2028.

Failure scenario: Existing controls perform inconsistently, or emissions rebound in ways not captured by annual averages or the summarized record.

Procedural implication: Commenters can request unit-level variability data, recent compliance histories, and a clearer explanation of why no incremental controls were modeled.

Claim C

Claim: New controls for Prairie State, Marion, Rain CII Carbon, Holcim/Lafarge, and ADM are not cost-effective and therefore not necessary.

Underlying assumption: EPA and Illinois used a meaningful set of technically feasible controls and evaluated cost per ton in a way that fits regional-haze reasonable-progress practice.

Failure scenario: Intermediate options, operational limits, or source-specific retrofits were screened out too quickly, or the record lacks enough context to judge whether quoted dollar-per-ton figures are high or low for this program.

Procedural implication: This is a primary comment target because the record shows option lists and cost figures, but only limited justification for why those figures resolve the reasonable-progress question.

Claim D

Claim: Projected 2028 visibility conditions below both adjusted and unadjusted URP glidepaths support approval.

Underlying assumption: The underlying modeling platform, 2016 base year, and source-apportionment methods remain sound and are not materially undermined by uncertainty.

Failure scenario: The favorable glidepath projection is sensitive to modeling assumptions or excludes meaningful emissions changes, sector shifts, or local conditions.
