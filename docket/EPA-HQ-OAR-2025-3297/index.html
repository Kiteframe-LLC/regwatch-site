What This Document Does

  • EPA proposes to delay the near-term criteria-pollutant part of its 2024 Tier 4 vehicle rule for two model years.
  • Instead of starting Tier 4 criteria-pollutant standards in model year 2027, the proposal would keep the older Tier 3 standards in place for model years 2027 and 2028 and start the Tier 4 phase-in in model year 2029.
  • The proposal covers light-duty vehicles, light-duty trucks, medium-duty passenger vehicles, and medium-duty vehicles.
  • EPA also proposes related timing changes for certification test procedures and on-board-diagnostics requirements.
  • This is only “Part 1” of EPA’s reconsideration. EPA says broader possible changes to the Tier 4 program will come later in a separate Part 2 rulemaking.

Why It Matters

  • This proposal matters because it would delay pollution-control improvements that were finalized only in April 2024.
  • EPA’s basic argument is that the 2024 Tier 4 schedule assumed much higher battery-electric-vehicle sales than EPA now expects for model years 2027 and 2028.
  • Under the 2024 rule, higher BEV sales would have made it easier for manufacturers to meet fleet-average non-methane organic gases plus NOx limits and some particulate-matter phase-in obligations, because BEVs count as zero-emission vehicles for those calculations.
  • EPA now says lower BEV sales, changed federal and state policy, and automaker product-plan shifts mean manufacturers would need more near-term hardware changes on gasoline vehicles than they originally expected, including more catalyst work, more gasoline particulate filters, more supplier coordination, more calibration work, and more time.
  • EPA estimates the delay would save manufacturers about $1.77 billion in present value at a 3 percent discount rate, or about $1.66 billion at a 7 percent discount rate.
  • But EPA also acknowledges that the proposal would increase emissions compared with keeping the current Tier 4 schedule. The notice describes those increases as small at the national level, but they are still increases.
  • EPA did not do new air-quality modeling for this proposal. Instead, it says the expected ambient impacts are likely small because the emissions changes are relatively small in national onroad inventory terms.

Who Is Affected

  • Vehicle manufacturers and importers are the direct regulated parties.
  • Automotive suppliers are indirectly affected because the proposal changes the timing pressure for catalyst upgrades, gasoline particulate filters, and related emissions-control systems.
  • Buyers of new vehicles may be affected if manufacturers avoid product-plan disruptions or keep certain models on the market that might otherwise become harder to certify on the original schedule.
  • Communities exposed to motor-vehicle pollution, especially people living or working near high-traffic corridors, are also affected because the proposal delays some planned emissions reductions.
  • Commenters with useful evidence include automakers, suppliers, fleet operators, public-health groups, state air agencies, and residents or local groups concerned about near-road pollution.

Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Major claim: lower BEV sales projections make the MY 2027-2028 Tier 4 phase-in infeasible on the original schedule

Underlying assumption Manufacturers materially depended on high BEV sales to satisfy fleet-average NMOG+NOx and per-vehicle PM obligations, and cannot realistically substitute gasoline-vehicle hardware changes quickly enough for MYs 2027 and 2028.

Failure scenario If manufacturers could comply through other tools, such as more aggressive use of ABT, targeted deployment of GPFs, or a narrower redesign of specific high-volume models, the case for a two-year blanket delay weakens.

Procedural implication Commenters can ask EPA to show how much of the claimed feasibility problem remains after accounting for ABT flexibility, already-certified Tier 4 vehicles, and manufacturer-specific rather than sector-wide compliance pathways.

Major claim: the cost-savings estimate is directionally reliable enough to support deregulation

Underlying assumption The catalyst-loadings memo and the one-case DRIA approach capture the main incremental costs that would be avoided, even though some compliance routes and noncompliance costs are not monetized.

Failure scenario If the unmodeled alternatives materially lower compliance costs under the original Tier 4 schedule, or if the omitted noncompliance and sunk-cost categories run in both directions, the headline savings figure may be overstated or incomplete.

Procedural implication A useful comment would not merely attack the presence of uncertainty. It would ask EPA to show sensitivity ranges for alternative ABT use, partial GPF deployment, and sunk Tier 4 investments already made for MYs 2027 and 2028.

Major claim: emissions and air-quality impacts are small enough that new air-quality modeling is unnecessary

Underlying assumption National inventory increases of up to 0.2 percent for NOx and up to 1.6 percent for onroad PM2.5 are small enough that the absence of new air-quality modeling does not materially undermine the decision record.

Failure scenario Small national percentages could still mask localized near-road or corridor effects, especially because the proposal itself notes that people living or working near transportation corridors are especially affected by motor-vehicle pollution.

Procedural implication Commenters can ask for a more explicit explanation of why national-scale inventory deltas are an adequate screening tool for a proposal that delays standards nationwide but may have concentrated local consequences.

Major claim: the change in position is lawful despite prior Tier 4 planning and investment

Underlying assumption Intervening policy and market changes are strong enough that previously incurred research, development, and manufacturing investments do not outweigh the need to revisit the MY 2027-2028 schedule.

Failure scenario If a substantial share of manufacturers already engineered or certified Tier 4-compliant vehicles for the affected model years, the delay may redistribute competitive burdens rather than simply relieve infeasible compliance pressure.

Procedural implication The reliance-interest question is a live administrative-law issue, and the final record will need more than generalized statements that the landscape changed.

Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

Observed Facts

EPA proposes to keep Tier 3 criteria-pollutant standards in place for light-duty vehicles, light-duty trucks, medium-duty passenger vehicles, and medium-duty vehicles during model years 2027 and 2028, delaying the initial Tier 4 criteria-pollutant phase-in until MY 2029. The proposal also delays associated certification-test and on-board-diagnostics changes and requests comment on related implementation details, including whether to extend Tier 3 for three model years instead of two, whether to import additional Tier 4 NMOG+NOx bins into the Tier 3 program for MYs 2027 and 2028, how ABT should affect the cost analysis, how already-certified Tier 4 vehicles should be treated, and whether reliance interests are significant. EPA estimates present-value cost savings of about $1.77 billion at a 3 percent discount rate and $1.66 billion at a 7 percent discount rate, while also projecting small increases in VOC, NOx, PM, and air-toxics emissions relative to the current Tier 4 baseline.

Inference

This is a deregulatory timing proposal rather than a full reconsideration of the substantive Tier 4 limits. EPA is not yet proposing a new final long-term pollution-control architecture. Instead, it is arguing that short-term compliance assumptions collapsed because expected BEV penetration fell and the industry lacks enough lead time to redesign, source, certify, and install additional controls such as GPFs and upgraded catalyst systems on the original schedule.

Step 9 — Failure Modes

One failure mode is forecast error. If BEV sales or manufacturer adaptation capabilities improve materially before MY 2027, EPA may end up delaying controls more than necessary based on an overly pessimistic snapshot.

Another failure mode is asymmetry in burden relief. Firms that already invested heavily in Tier 4 compliance for MYs 2027 and 2028 could be disadvantaged relative to firms that delayed investment and now benefit more from the postponement.

A third failure mode is localized impact blindness. Even small national inventory increases can matter more in high-traffic corridors or communities already burdened by transportation pollution, yet the proposal does not model ambient concentration changes from the delay.

Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

The proposal creates incentives for manufacturers to emphasize lower-BEV scenarios and near-term hardware constraints because those showings support schedule relief. It also reduces immediate pressure to accelerate GPF integration and deeper catalyst upgrades on conventional vehicles for MYs 2027 and 2028.

At the same time, the structure preserves an incentive to keep some Tier 4-compliant product plans alive, because EPA proposes to honor existing Tier 4 certificates and contemplates mixing Tier 3 and Tier 4 sales-weighted values within a model year.
