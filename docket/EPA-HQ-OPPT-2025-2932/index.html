What This Document Does

  • EPA proposes significant new use rules (SNURs) for two proposed 40 CFR part 721 entries covering battery-related mixed metal oxide substances generically described as cobalt lithium manganese nickel oxide, metals-doped.
  • The proposal would not ban the substances outright. Instead, it would require a significant new use notice (SNUN) at least 90 days before anyone manufactures or processes the substances in ways EPA treats as a significant new use.
  • EPA says these chemicals were already reviewed through premanufacture notices (PMNs) and TSCA section 5(e) orders. The new rule would extend those order-style protections beyond the original PMN submitters to any future manufacturer, processor, recycler, or reclaimer working with the same substances.
  • The proposed restrictions are substantial. They include battery-only use, labels on batteries or packaging, 99% dust-control efficiency, no release to water, hazardous-waste landfill or incineration disposal, a property-boundary air limit, hazard communication requirements, and a tiered respirator matrix tied to worker exposure levels.
  • EPA also says some significant new uses are confidential. A prospective manufacturer or processor may need to use the bona fide intent process in 40 CFR 721.11 to learn whether a confidential use trigger applies.

Why It Matters

  • This is a classic TSCA section 5 move: EPA is taking restrictions it already imposed on specific PMN submitters and trying to make them the default rule for everyone else dealing with the same new substances.
  • The practical effect is strong even though the action is framed as a notice rule. A firm can stay inside the order-style conditions or file a SNUN and wait for EPA review before doing something different.
  • The visible record is mixed. The notice gives the core restrictions and the broad hazard rationale, including concerns about carcinogenicity, reproductive toxicity, specific target organ toxicity, dermal and respiratory sensitization, and aquatic toxicity at very low concentrations. But much of the deeper support appears by reference to docket materials rather than full explanation in the preamble.
  • That matters because the proposal uses exact thresholds and categorical limits. EPA does not just say “use controls are needed.” It says 99% capture and control efficiency is required, releases to water are not allowed, and worker respirator requirements escalate across specific exposure bands.
  • The public-facing economic discussion is also thin. EPA estimates about $45,496 per SNUN for large businesses, $14,976 for small businesses, and roughly $106 per export notification, but it also says it cannot estimate how many export notifications are likely.
  • The best comment opportunities are therefore about explanation and administrability: how EPA derived the exact thresholds, why battery-only use is the right boundary, how confidential significant new uses work in practice, and whether the burden discussion is too abstract.

Who Is Affected

  • Battery-material manufacturers and processors that might want to work with the covered mixed metal oxide substances.
  • Importers, because TSCA import-certification rules can attach when a chemical is subject to TSCA controls.
  • Recyclers and reclaimers, because EPA expressly says “processing” includes reclaiming substances from batteries or other items containing the PMN substance.
  • Exporters, because TSCA section 12(b) export-notification requirements apply to substances subject to proposed or final SNURs.
  • Workers who could face inhalation or dermal exposure during manufacture, processing, recycling, packaging, or waste handling.

Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

  1. EPA should explain how the technical evidence supports the exact thresholds it chose. Why it matters: The notice gives precise requirements such as 99% dust control, a 1.3E-4 mg/m3 boundary-air cap, and a detailed respirator matrix, but it does not fully walk readers through how the underlying assessment leads to each cutoff. Practical question a commenter can ask: What parts of the standardized assessment or PMN record support each major numeric threshold in the proposed SNURs?

  2. EPA should explain why battery-only use is the right use boundary. Why it matters: The proposal treats any non-battery use as a significant new use, which is a major scope choice. The notice gives only a compressed explanation for why that line is appropriate. Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence shows that non-battery uses create materially different or less manageable exposure and release concerns than the proposed permitted battery use?

  3. EPA should address how confidential significant new uses work for outside firms in practice. Why it matters: If part of the rule depends on confidential use triggers, new entrants may face planning delays or uncertainty before they can know what EPA considers restricted. Practical question a commenter can ask: How quickly and predictably can a prospective manufacturer or processor obtain a confidential significant-new-use determination through the bona fide process?

  4. EPA should better explain why two separate CFR entries are needed for substances with the same generic chemical description. Why it matters: Outside readers can see that the entries correspond to different PMN sets, but the notice does not fully explain the practical distinction between the two rule sections. Practical question a commenter can ask: What substantive differences between the two PMN groupings justify separate SNUR entries rather than one combined rule treatment?

  5. EPA should make the burden discussion more concrete. Why it matters: Unit-cost estimates are useful, but they do not tell the public much if EPA cannot estimate how often firms are likely to face those burdens. Practical question a commenter can ask: What information does EPA have about the likely number of future SNUNs or export notifications, even if it cannot provide a precise forecast?

Claim: EPA can appropriately generalize order-based restrictions into SNURs for all manufacturers and processors of the same substances.

Underlying assumption: The order terms negotiated with the PMN submitters are a sound proxy for the controls EPA would expect from any future manufacturer or processor of the same substances.

Failure scenario: If the order terms were tailored to submitter-specific manufacturing methods or exposure assumptions, extending them wholesale could overfit one factual setting.

Procedural implication: Commenters can ask EPA to explain which restrictions are substance-driven and which were shaped by site- or process-specific PMN facts.

Underlying assumption: The standardized assessment and PMN record support not just a general concern, but the exact thresholds EPA chose.

Failure scenario: If the supporting assessment shows more uncertainty than the preamble suggests, the precise line-drawing around APF levels, boundary concentrations, or labeling scope could look underexplained.

Claim: Battery-only use is the right permitted use boundary.

Procedural implication: This is a high-value place for commenters to request explanation of the agency's use-scope reasoning.

Claim D

Claim: The proposal's economic discussion is adequate even though EPA cannot estimate likely export-notification counts.

Underlying assumption: A per-unit export-notification estimate and a per-SNUN filing estimate are enough to characterize the proposal's practical burden.

Failure scenario: If the number of affected exporters, likely notifications, or compliance behavior matters meaningfully, the economic discussion may be too abstract.

Procedural implication: Commenters can ask EPA to explain the expected frequency of these burdens or why it cannot do so.

Claim E

Claim: Confidential significant new uses can still be administered fairly through the bona fide intent procedure.

Underlying assumption: Potential manufacturers and processors will be able to determine their obligations with reasonable certainty before beginning activity.

Procedural implication: Commenters can ask EPA to explain how practical and timely the bona fide process is for prospective market entrants.

Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

Observed Facts
  • EPA proposes SNURs under TSCA section 5(a)(2) for two proposed CFR entries, 40 CFR 721.12219 and 40 CFR 721.12220.
  • Both entries cover battery-related mixed metal oxide substances generically described as cobalt lithium manganese nickel oxide, metals-doped.
  • EPA says the substances were the subject of PMNs and TSCA section 5(e) orders, and the proposed SNURs would extend the order protections beyond the original submitters.
  • The proposed controls include battery-only use, battery/exterior packaging labels, 99% dust-control efficiency, no water release, hazardous-waste landfill or incineration disposal, a property-boundary air limit, and a respiratory protection matrix tied to worker exposure levels.
  • EPA also says manufacture or processing for more than one year becomes a significant new use unless there is no air release or inhalation exposure, or unless relevant limits are updated.
  • EPA quantifies estimated SNUN submission costs and a per-export-notification cost, but says it cannot estimate the likely number of export notifications.
Inference

This is a targeted, order-following TSCA proposal rather than a broad cross-market chemical rule. EPA is using the SNUR mechanism to convert submitter-specific new-chemical restrictions into generally applicable advance-notice obligations. The strongest public-comment opportunities concern whether EPA has shown enough visible reasoning for its precise thresholds, whether the confidential-use process provides adequate practical notice, and whether the economic discussion is too thin for outside readers to assess the real burden.
