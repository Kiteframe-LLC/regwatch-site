What This Document Does

  • ATF proposes to change how certain National Firearms Act firearms can be transported across state lines.
  • For domestic short-term trips of 365 days or less, the proposal would stop requiring both notice to ATF and advance ATF approval.
  • For domestic long-term trips of more than 365 days, and for permanent moves, the proposal would still require notice to ATF at least 14 days before travel, but the traveler would no longer have to wait for ATF to approve before moving.
  • The proposal would also expressly allow “pass-through” travel across jurisdictions that otherwise prohibit the firearm, as long as the traveler complies with 18 U.S.C. 926A.
  • ATF would keep the current pre-approval process for temporary foreign transport. Permanent export rules would also stay in place.
  • The proposed rule text would require the traveler to carry proof that the firearm is registered in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record, and would require common or contract carriers to receive a copy of that proof.

Why It Matters

  • This is a deregulatory proposal. ATF says the current Form 20 approval system has become an unnecessary barrier for lawful domestic travel with certain registered firearms.
  • The strongest evidence ATF offers is practical and numerical. The notice says that from January 2020 through May 2025, ATF received about 96,865 domestic transport requests and denied only 516 of them because the destination state's law barred possession of that firearm type.
  • ATF also says the system often denies requests for technical paperwork mistakes rather than real safety problems, and that current processing still averages about 3 days for eForms and 9 days for paper forms.
  • ATF estimates the proposal would save affected owners about $122,525 per year in paperwork time, or about $1.23 million over ten years. The proposal also says the main benefits are qualitative: fewer delays, less trip-planning uncertainty, and less repeated paperwork.
  • The main analytical tension is that ATF wants to reduce burden without fully explaining the legal and practical risks of removing pre-approval. The notice acknowledges that travelers may have to determine for themselves whether a destination is lawful under state, local, or tribal law.
  • The proposal changes where the compliance burden sits. Instead of ATF screening domestic legality in advance, more of that work would fall on the individual traveler and, in some situations, carriers.

Who Is Affected

  • Individuals who lawfully possess registered machine guns, short-barreled rifles, short-barreled shotguns, or destructive devices and want to transport them across state lines.
  • Owners who make repeated short domestic trips, such as travel to competitions, shows, exhibitions, neighboring residences, or shooting ranges.
  • Owners planning longer temporary moves or permanent relocations, because they would still have to notify ATF but would no longer wait for approval.
  • Common and contract carriers handling these firearms in interstate transport.

Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

  1. ATF should justify why 365 days is the right line between “short-term” and “long-term” transport. Why it matters: That single cutoff controls whether a traveler must notify ATF at all, but the notice gives little explanation for why a full year is the right threshold. Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence or historical practice led ATF to choose 365 days instead of a shorter or more conditional threshold?

  2. ATF should show more of the data behind its claim that pre-approval adds little safety value. Why it matters: A low formal denial rate does not necessarily prove the review system has little preventive value. It may also deter bad filings or catch issues before a denial is recorded. Practical question a commenter can ask: Can ATF provide a fuller breakdown of denials, withdrawals, corrections, and other outcomes so the public can evaluate what pre-approval actually prevents?

  3. ATF should explain how travelers are expected to navigate state, local, and tribal law after pre-approval is removed. Why it matters: The proposal openly says individuals may need to make these judgments themselves, which can create legal risk for less sophisticated travelers. Practical question a commenter can ask: Would ATF consider publishing a compliance aid or safe-harbor guidance to reduce avoidable mistakes about destination legality?

  4. ATF should clarify what happens if authorization is rescinded after a long-term move has already started. Why it matters: The notice says a traveler may have to stop transporting the firearm mid-trip, but it gives little practical guidance about routing, storage, return travel, or post-arrival handling. Practical question a commenter can ask: What operational steps would ATF expect a traveler to take if ATF rescinds authorization after travel begins or after the traveler reaches the destination?

Step 1 — Claim Stress Test
Claim A

Claim: Categorical authorization is legally sufficient under 18 U.S.C. 922(a)(4).

Underlying assumption: The statute's requirement for specific authorization does not require individualized pre-transport review in the domestic circumstances covered by the proposal.

Failure scenario: If commenters show that the statutory text or structure expects firearm-by-firearm review for interstate movements of these weapons, the proposal's core legal justification weakens.

Procedural implication: The agency should explain more directly why categorical authorization remains “specific” enough for this statute and why Lopez v. Davis is the right analogy.

Claim B

Claim: Pre-approval provides little safety or legality value because denials based on destination-state illegality are rare.

Underlying assumption: The low denial rate is a reliable indicator that ex ante review is unnecessary rather than evidence that the current system deters unlawful trips before filing.

Failure scenario: If the current process screens out risky or confused applicants before travel in ways the denial percentage does not capture, the proposal may understate the value of the status quo.

Procedural implication: Commenters can ask ATF to disclose denial categories, withdrawals, corrections, and any compliance contacts that do not culminate in a formal denial.

Claim C

Claim: The 365-day threshold is a workable dividing line between short-term and long-term domestic transport.

Underlying assumption: A one-year cutoff is administratively simpler without materially weakening location tracking.

Failure scenario: If the cutoff is arbitrary or easy to manipulate, persons could structure repeated “short-term” moves to avoid notice while effectively relocating firearms for extended periods.

Procedural implication: The record would be stronger if ATF explained why 365 days, rather than a shorter or event-based threshold, best fits the statute and operational need.

Claim D

Claim: A 14-day notice period without approval is enough for long-term and permanent moves.

Underlying assumption: ATF can usually identify legal problems quickly enough that automatic authorization plus possible rescission remains workable.

Failure scenario: If ATF often cannot complete meaningful review within the notice window, the system could shift legal risk onto registrants and create confusion if rescission occurs after travel starts.

Procedural implication: Commenters can ask for data on review times, rescission likelihood, and how ATF would handle mid-route or post-arrival rescissions in practice.

Claim E

Claim: The rule's quantified benefits and qualitative burdens are adequately described.

Underlying assumption: Paperwork time savings capture the main monetizable effects, while other consequences are either minor or too speculative to quantify.

Failure scenario: If legal-research burdens, mistaken-law risks, carrier friction, or trip-disruption costs are material, the cost discussion may be incomplete.

Procedural implication: The agency invited comment on costs, benefits, methodology, and data, so this is a strong record-building comment target rather than an obvious unacknowledged defect.

Step 2 — Essence of the Rule
Observed Facts
  • ATF proposes to revise 27 CFR 478.28 for interstate and foreign transport of certain NFA firearms.
  • For domestic short-term transport of 365 days or fewer, the proposal would eliminate both the notice requirement and the pre-approval requirement.
  • For domestic long-term transport of more than 365 days, and for permanent moves, the proposal would require notice at least 14 days before travel but would no longer require waiting for approval.
