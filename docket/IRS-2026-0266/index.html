What This Document Does

  • Proposes a special electronic-delivery rule for Form 1099-DA statements that digital-asset brokers must furnish to customers.
  • Would let brokers obtain customer consent to electronic delivery without offering a paper-delivery alternative.
  • Would specifically allow brokers to terminate the business relationship if a customer does not consent to electronic delivery.
  • Would not require brokers to let customers withdraw a consent they already gave.
  • Tries to offset those changes with added disclosure, notice, access, and retention requirements, including access through at least October 15 of the following year and seven-year retention.
  • Applies only to 1099-DA statements, not to other broker statements such as 1099-B.

Why It Matters

  • This is a targeted but important change in how tax information for digital-asset sales would reach customers. It affects whether customers have a practical fallback if they do not want electronic delivery or later lose access to the delivery method they originally accepted.
  • The IRS and Treasury say the current paper-default model is too burdensome for brokers because digital-asset trading is highly electronic and some customers may generate very large numbers of transactions. That concern is plausible, but the visible record in the supplied materials is thinner than ideal on the size of the burden and on whether narrower options could solve most of the problem.
  • The biggest commenter leverage point is not whether electronic delivery should be allowed. It already is. The more important question is whether the agency has adequately justified removing two safeguards at once: paper fallback for non-consent and the ability to withdraw consent later.
  • The rule also matters because tax statements are not just convenience documents. They affect filing accuracy, audit response, and the customer’s ability to understand what the IRS has been told about the customer’s transactions.

Who Is Affected

  • Digital-asset brokers that must furnish 1099-DA statements.
  • Customers whose digital-asset sales are reported on 1099-DA statements.
  • Compliance, legal, and product teams designing onboarding, disclosure, and account-access systems.
  • Tax preparers and customer-support teams that help users retrieve prior-year records.
  • Customers with weaker digital access, changing email addresses, lost account credentials, kiosk-only interactions, or accessibility needs.

Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

  1. Issue: The rule removes paper fallback and allows account termination for non-consent.

    • Why it matters: This is the sharpest substantive change in the proposal and may make consent less meaningfully voluntary in practice.
    • Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence shows that allowing termination is necessary, rather than using narrower tools such as paper-on-request hardship exceptions or limited fallback rules for existing customers?

  2. Issue: The rule does not require brokers to permit withdrawal of previously given consent.

    • Why it matters: Customers may later lose digital access, change devices, change email addresses, or need paper records for tax preparation or audit response.
    • Practical question a commenter can ask: Why would a prospective withdrawal right with reasonable timing limits fail to preserve most of the claimed burden reduction?

  3. Issue: The record is thin on the size of the paper-burden problem.

    • Why it matters: The main policy justification is that mailing 1099-DA statements is unnecessarily burdensome, especially for high-volume users, but the supplied materials do not show detailed cost ranges or transaction-volume distributions.
    • Practical question a commenter can ask: Please publish the cost estimates, customer-volume assumptions, and any stakeholder submissions or broker data the agency relied on to conclude paper delivery is impractical.

  4. Issue: The agency’s substitute protections may not fully replace the safeguards it removes.

    • Why it matters: Enhanced notice, retention, and access requirements help, but they do not automatically solve lost-account, bounced-email, or accessibility problems.
    • Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence shows that the proposed notice and access rules are enough to prevent missed statements and tax-filing problems when customers lose access to their accounts?

  5. Issue: The 1099-DA-only carveout needs clearer line drawing.

    • Why it matters: The proposal says digital-asset statements are different, but the visible record is thinner on why the same burden logic would not apply to at least some other electronically mediated broker statements.
    • Practical question a commenter can ask: What feature of digital-asset brokerage makes this market uniquely appropriate for a no-paper, no-withdrawal consent regime?

Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Claim A

Claim
Digital-asset customers transact almost exclusively electronically, so paper delivery requirements are poorly matched to this market.

Underlying assumption
Most affected customers reliably maintain digital access sufficient to receive, retain, and retrieve tax statements during filing season and later correction periods.

Failure scenario
If a meaningful subset of customers loses account access, changes devices, changes email addresses, uses kiosks or limited-access platforms, or has accessibility barriers, the proposed all-electronic structure may increase missed statements and tax-filing errors.

Procedural implication
Commenters can ask for the empirical basis for the “almost exclusively” premise and for evidence about account-access failures, bounced notices, and inaccessible delivery modes.

Claim B

Claim
Paper furnishing for non-consenting customers would impose avoidable compliance burdens because many customers have large transaction volumes.

Underlying assumption
The burden from printing and mailing transaction-heavy statements is large enough to justify eliminating the practical paper fallback.

Failure scenario
If brokers can instead use narrower tools such as consolidated statements, transaction thresholds, paper-on-request exceptions, or limited hardship accommodations, the agency’s chosen solution may look overbroad.

Procedural implication
The alternatives analysis may need more visible discussion of intermediate options rather than a binary paper-versus-termination framing.

Claim C

Claim
Enhanced notice, access-period, and disclosure requirements sufficiently offset the risks created by eliminating withdrawal rights and paper delivery.

Underlying assumption
Notice and access protections are adequate substitutes for the consumer-protection function served by paper fallback and revocable consent.

Failure scenario
If customers can still miss notices, lose platform access, or need paper for tax preparation or recordkeeping reasons, the mitigation package may not fully address the new risk profile.

Procedural implication
Commenters can press the agency to explain why these protections are enough and whether less restrictive options were considered.

Claim D

Claim
Brokers may terminate relationships with non-consenting customers rather than offering paper statements.

Underlying assumption
That outcome is consistent with statutory consent requirements, consumer fairness, and tax-administration goals.

Failure scenario
If termination pressures customers into nominal consent or causes disruption for existing customers who still need accurate tax statements for already-effected transactions, the consent may appear less meaningfully voluntary.

Procedural implication
This is a high-leverage comment point on voluntariness, fair notice, and whether the proposed regime indirectly coerces consent.

Claim E

Claim
The carveout should apply to 1099-DA statements but not to other broker statements such as 1099-B.

Underlying assumption
Digital-asset reporting is sufficiently different from other broker reporting to justify a separate consent regime.

Failure scenario
If the same burden logic exists for other high-volume electronically mediated accounts, the rule’s line-drawing may look under-explained.

Procedural implication
The agency may need a clearer explanation of why digital-asset brokerage uniquely warrants this treatment.

Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

Observed Facts

The NPRM would amend Treasury regulations under section 6045 to create an alternative consent framework for furnishing Form 1099-DA statements electronically.
It would let brokers obtain affirmative consent without being required to offer a paper-delivery alternative.
It would specifically permit brokers to terminate business relationships with customers who do not consent.
It would not require brokers to permit withdrawal of previously given consent.
It would require enhanced disclosure, notice, access, and retention rules, including access through at least October 15 of the following year and retention for seven years.
It is limited to 1099-DA statements and does not extend the same rule to 1099-B statements.

Evidence quotes

  • “These proposed regulations would provide brokers with an alternative process for obtaining consent from their customers to receive 1099-DA statements in an electronic format without offering a paper delivery alternative.”
  • “These proposed regulations would generally not require brokers to furnish the 1099-DA statements on paper ... but, instead, would specifically permit brokers to terminate their business relationship with these customers.”
  • “Additionally, these proposed regulations would not require brokers to give their customers the ability to withdraw a previously provided consent.”
Inference

This is a targeted deregulatory procedural proposal. The main question is not whether electronic delivery is permissible in general, but whether the agency has adequately justified removing two long-standing safeguards at once: paper fallback for non-consent and the ability to withdraw consent later.
