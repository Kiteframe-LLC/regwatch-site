What This Document Does

  • This proposal would make permitted payment stablecoin issuers a defined Bank Secrecy Act-regulated financial institution category and layer in both FinCEN AML/CFT duties and OFAC sanctions-program duties.
  • FinCEN would require PPSIs to maintain a risk-based AML/CFT program, conduct customer due diligence, collect beneficial-ownership information for legal-entity customers, file required reports, keep records, perform independent testing, train staff, and designate a U.S.-based responsible officer.
  • OFAC would require a sanctions compliance program built around five minimum elements: senior-management commitment, risk assessment, internal controls, testing/auditing, and training.
  • The proposal distinguishes between “primary market” activity, where the issuer directly interacts with a customer, and “secondary market” activity, where the issuer is not directly a party except through smart-contract infrastructure.
  • Treasury also proposes technical-capability obligations tied to blocking, freezing, rejecting, and complying with lawful orders.

Why It Matters

  • This is a foundational federal compliance rule for the post-GENIUS Act stablecoin market.
  • The proposal matters not just because it imposes obligations, but because it chooses where not to impose them. Most importantly, FinCEN preliminarily declines to require PPSIs to file SARs on secondary-market transactions, saying the burden may outweigh the benefit.
  • The agencies are also trying to balance two themes that can pull in opposite directions: they say the framework should be tailored to issuer size and complexity, but they also reject one obvious small-issuer accommodation, arguing that lighter AML/CFT obligations could attract illicit actors and that compliance technology is scalable.
  • The record is substantial, with a preamble, regulatory impact analysis, flexibility analysis, and policy alternatives. But several practical judgments still rest on summarized assumptions rather than clearly visible supporting studies in the packet.

Who Is Affected

  • Permitted payment stablecoin issuers are the direct targets of the rule.
  • Existing large stablecoin issuers appear especially affected because Treasury's own analysis suggests many likely future PPSIs are current market participants with at least some onboarding and AML-related infrastructure already in place.
  • Smaller or newer issuers may be affected disproportionately if the agencies are underestimating the cost of building lawful-order tooling, sanctions controls, and auditable compliance functions from a relatively thin baseline.
  • Direct customers in the “primary market,” including legal-entity customers, would be affected through identification, due-diligence, and information-collection requirements.
  • Law-enforcement and national-security agencies are also part of the intended beneficiary set. Treasury grounds the rule in concerns about money laundering, sanctions evasion, terrorism finance, and fraud.

Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Claim A

Claim Stablecoin-linked AML and sanctions obligations are necessary because stablecoins present a substantial and already documented illicit-finance problem.

Underlying assumption The cited SAR, blocked-property, and rejected-transaction figures are a reliable proxy for risk requiring direct PPSI regulation, not just a reflection of broader crypto enforcement that could already be partly addressed through intermediaries.

Failure scenario If most actionable monitoring already occurs through exchanges, banks, and other intermediaries, then the incremental benefits of pushing more obligations directly onto PPSIs may be smaller than the preamble implies.

Procedural implication Commenters can ask the agencies to separate what harms are uniquely attributable to gaps at the issuer level from harms already captured by other regulated entities.

Claim B

Claim The primary-market/secondary-market distinction is a workable way to tailor obligations.

Underlying assumption In real-world stablecoin operations, the line between direct issuer relationships and smart-contract-mediated secondary activity is administrable enough to guide compliance and enforcement.

Failure scenario If custody, wallet screening, whitelisting, reissuance, embedded compliance tooling, or chain-level controls blur this line, firms may struggle to know when direct obligations attach and regulators may enforce inconsistently.

Procedural implication A stronger final rule would likely need more examples, more edge-case discussion, or more explicit treatment of mixed or indirect issuer involvement.

Claim C

Claim Not requiring SARs for secondary-market activity is an appropriate burden-benefit tradeoff.

Underlying assumption The compliance burden of requiring issuer-side SAR reporting for secondary-market activity is materially greater than the marginal investigative benefit.

Failure scenario If PPSIs in fact control important screening, freezing, wallet-permissioning, or analytics information relevant to secondary-market misuse, excluding that category may undercut the proposal's own illicit-finance rationale.

Procedural implication This is currently framed as deliberate solicitation rather than a hidden defect, but the record would be stronger if Treasury offered more concrete evidence for the burden-benefit balance.

Claim D

Claim A risk-based AML/CFT program, training, testing, U.S.-located compliance officer, and customer due diligence framework are the appropriate minimum FinCEN requirements for PPSIs.

Underlying assumption Existing bank/MSB-style AML architecture can be adapted to payment stablecoin issuers without major mismatch.

Failure scenario If stablecoin issuers' core risk points arise more from smart-contract controls, cross-chain tooling, wallet-level permissions, or foreign intermediary relationships than from ordinary customer-onboarding models, a mostly familiar AML architecture may omit some issuer-specific design choices.

Procedural implication Commenters can ask for more explanation of where the agencies intentionally diverge from bank/MSB models and where they deliberately borrow from them.

Claim E

Claim The OFAC five-element framework is sufficiently tailored to the size and complexity of PPSIs while still setting a necessary floor.

Underlying assumption A flexible floor-plus-risk-based-design model gives smaller and larger PPSIs enough room to calibrate without creating a weak sanctions baseline.

Failure scenario If the floor is operationally demanding in practice, especially given required technology, testing, and integration expectations, the agencies may be undercounting burden on smaller issuers while still calling the approach “tailored.”

Procedural implication Commenters can request more concrete examples of what a minimally compliant small PPSI program would look like compared with a large issuer program.

Claim F

Claim The agencies' cost estimates are a reasonable basis for the proposal.

Underlying assumption Current market-practice observations, SAR filing patterns, customer counts, and assumed labor rates are representative enough to forecast future PPSI burdens.

Failure scenario If the future PPSI market differs substantially from today's likely candidates, or if the most expensive technical work centers on lawful-order capability and transaction controls rather than ordinary onboarding, the headline cost estimates may be too low or insufficiently distributed across firm types.

Procedural implication The RIA is substantial, but commenters can still press for sensitivity analysis and more visible treatment of uncertainty.

Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

Observed Facts

The joint proposed rule would implement GENIUS Act directives by:

  • treating permitted payment stablecoin issuers as financial institutions for Bank Secrecy Act purposes;
  • creating a new FinCEN regulatory framework in 31 CFR part 1033 for issuer AML/CFT programs, customer due diligence, beneficial-ownership collection, currency transaction reporting, suspicious activity reporting, recordkeeping, information sharing, enhanced due diligence, and special-measures application;
  • imposing FinCEN requirements for training, independent testing, a U.S.-located responsible officer, and technical capabilities, policies, and procedures to block, freeze, and reject impermissible transactions;
  • creating a new OFAC part 502 framework requiring an effective sanctions compliance program based on five elements: senior-management commitment, risk assessment, internal controls, testing/auditing, and training;
  • relying heavily on a distinction between “primary market” activity involving a direct PPSI relationship and “secondary market” activity involving the PPSI only through a smart contract; and
  • presenting a regulatory impact analysis, an initial regulatory flexibility analysis, and a policy-alternatives discussion that includes rejected options for small-issuer accommodations and more prescriptive technical rules.
Inference

This is not a narrow reporting amendment. It is a foundational compliance-architecture rule for a new class of financial actor. The most important practical questions are not whether PPSIs should face any AML/sanctions duties, but how far issuer obligations should extend into secondary-market activity, how concretely the agencies should specify technical controls, and whether the agencies have justified saying the framework is both risk-based and meaningfully tailored.

Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

Regulatory objective
Reduce illicit-finance, sanctions-evasion, and national-security risks associated with payment stablecoins while implementing the GENIUS Act's issuer-specific AML and sanctions directives.

Problem definition
Stablecoins are increasingly used by illicit actors; existing regulatory treatment of issuers is incomplete or uneven; and future payment stablecoin issuers need a clear, minimum federal compliance baseline.

Data used
Agency-held SAR and sanctions-report figures, prior ANPRM comments, market screening of current stablecoin issuers, observations about existing MSB registration and customer-onboarding practices, and internal burden estimates in the RIA.

Analytical method
Map PPSIs into familiar BSA and sanctions-compliance frameworks, then tailor those frameworks through a risk-based structure, selected exclusions, and nonprescriptive technical flexibility.

Alternatives considered
Small-issuer accommodations based on a $200 million reserve-assets benchmark, additional information-collection requirements, more detailed or proactive block/freeze/reject rules, and OFAC program structures drawn more directly from other banking AML rules.

Agency conclusion
The agencies conclude that a joint, risk-based but minimum-floor regime for AML/CFT and sanctions compliance is necessary and appropriate; that some important requirements should be flexible rather than highly prescriptive; and that some burdens, such as issuer-side SAR duties for secondary-market activity, should not be imposed at this stage.

Missing links / logical jumps

  • The rule repeatedly invokes tailoring to size and complexity, but the strongest visible tailoring choice is actually the rejection of concrete accommodations for smaller issuers.
  • The preamble explains why secondary-market SAR duties may be burdensome, but gives less visible quantitative support for why the investigative value would be lower than the burden.
  • The proposal assumes that many current stablecoin issuers already use screening and compliance tools, but the packet does not include the underlying market screen or a clearer breakdown of which future PPSIs are likely to start from a thin compliance baseline.
  • The lawful-order and block/freeze/reject capability requirements are described as flexible, but that same flexibility can reduce ex ante clarity about what technical implementation will satisfy the rule.

Step 6 — Technical Coherence

Observed Fact The proposal's internal logic is generally coherent. FinCEN grounds AML/CFT requirements in risk-based customer diligence, reporting, recordkeeping, testing, training, and a responsible U.S.-located officer. OFAC grounds sanctions requirements in a five-element program model and repeatedly emphasizes a floor-plus-flexibility structure. The preamble also repeatedly ties direct issuer obligations to the primary market while treating secondary-market obligations more cautiously.

Inference The proposal is conceptually coherent because the agencies are trying to regulate a new kind of issuer without pretending that all stablecoin activity looks like traditional banking. The main coherence risk is that the proposal relies on flexibility in exactly the areas where commenters may want more clarity: technological capability, lawful-order compliance, block/freeze/reject controls, and the operational boundary between direct and indirect issuer involvement.

Primary coherence risk The agencies simultaneously say the rule is tailored and flexible while also declining to adopt more visible small-issuer accommodations. That does not make the rule incoherent, but it does create tension between the “tailored” label and the practical baseline the proposal appears to expect every PPSI to meet.

Step 7 — Legal Grounding

Observed Fact The NPRM grounds the rule in the GENIUS Act's directive that PPSIs be treated as financial institutions for BSA purposes and be subject to federal laws relating to economic sanctions, money laundering prevention, customer identification, and due diligence. It also invokes FinCEN's delegated BSA authority and OFAC's IEEPA-based sanctions authorities.

Assessment The proposal appears legally grounded. The agencies are not inventing a new policy objective detached from statute; they are implementing a recently enacted statutory framework that expressly directs Treasury to issue issuer-specific rules.

Uncertain point The legal risk is not an obvious lack of authority. It is whether some design choices are adequately explained as implementations of a statute that says Treasury's rules should be tailored to the size and complexity of PPSIs. If commenters believe the final rule ends up too undifferentiated or too vague in key technical areas, that concern will likely sound in reasoned explanation more than pure statutory authority.

Step 9 — Failure Modes

  • Issuers may struggle to classify activity at the primary/secondary boundary when smart-contract controls, whitelist permissions, burns, freezes, or reissuance blur direct and indirect involvement.
  • Smaller or newer issuers may face steeper implementation burdens than the RIA suggests if the real cost driver is not standard onboarding software but robust lawful-order and transaction-control capability.
  • By excluding secondary-market SAR duties categorically, the proposal could leave some illicit-finance visibility gaps where the issuer retains important technical or wallet-control information.
  • Flexible block/freeze/reject requirements may reduce adaptability risk but create uneven compliance expectations across examiners and issuers.

Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

Potential incentives created by the rule

  • PPSIs will have stronger incentives to maintain direct customer relationships, customer screening, and auditable compliance functions around primary-market activity.
  • Issuers may redesign wallet permissions, custody arrangements, or smart-contract governance to preserve flexibility while still showing technological capability to freeze, reject, or comply with lawful orders.
  • Larger incumbent issuers with existing AML tools may gain relative advantage over smaller or newer entrants because the rule rewards existing compliance maturity.

Potential gaming concerns

  • Firms may describe some activities as “secondary market” to reduce expectations for direct monitoring or reporting.
  • Firms may rely on high-level written policies while underinvesting in real technological enforcement capacity if the final rule stays flexible and nonprescriptive.
  • If the agencies do not clarify how much “technological capability” is enough, compliance may drift toward minimum defensible documentation rather than robust operational controls.
