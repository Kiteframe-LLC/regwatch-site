What This Document Does

  • Proposes new FSOC interpretive guidance for deciding how to identify and respond to risks from nonbank financial companies and financial activities.
  • Would replace the 2023 interpretive guidance and rescind the separate 2023 analytic framework, while leaving the underlying 2012 procedural rule in place.
  • Moves the Council back toward a more 2019-style structure by putting an activities-based approach first and treating firm-specific designation as a later step when broader tools are not enough.
  • Says FSOC would consider "economic growth" and "economic security" when identifying potential risks to U.S. financial stability.
  • Says FSOC would use a cost-benefit test before making a section 113 designation and would use a higher threshold for what counts as a threat to U.S. financial stability.
  • Adds a process where FSOC can first ask an existing regulator to respond in writing about how it plans to address a risk.

Why It Matters

  • This is a process rule in practical effect, but it matters because process choices shape when and how FSOC can act against systemic risk.
  • The notice would make designation harder to reach in at least three ways: it prioritizes activity-based responses first, it says designation should occur only when expected benefits justify expected costs, and it interprets a qualifying threat as one that could cause "severe damage on the broader U.S. economy."
  • FSOC says these changes would improve rigor, transparency, and engagement with firms and regulators. That is a serious claim, but the notice is stronger on announcing the new framework than on demonstrating why the 2023 framework was inadequate in practice.
  • The notice also introduces "economic security" as part of the risk-identification picture. That may become important in future cases, but the proposal does not do much yet to define its limits or show how it will be applied.
  • The result is a record with a real, commentable tension: the Council wants more disciplined and explainable decisionmaking, but some of the proposal's most important new ideas are themselves only partly explained.

Who Is Affected

  • Large nonbank financial companies that could face future FSOC review or designation.
  • Primary financial regulators that may be asked to respond first before FSOC moves toward a company-specific step.
  • Market participants, investors, and the public who depend on the Council to identify and address emerging systemic risks before they become crises.
  • Firms and advocates trying to comment on future designations, because this guidance affects what evidence and arguments may matter most.

Major claim: an activities-based approach should come first

Underlying assumption System-wide or activity-based responses are generally better calibrated than company-specific designation and can address many risks without direct entity designation.

Failure scenario If a risk is concentrated in one firm or only becomes visible through firm-specific features, a front-loaded activities-based sequence could delay intervention or create uncertainty about when the Council will conclude that system-wide measures are inadequate.

Procedural implication Commenters can ask the Council to explain what evidence or triggers move a matter from activity review to entity-specific review and how long the Council expects that transition to take.

Major claim: economic growth and economic security are valid components of financial-stability analysis

Underlying assumption The relationship between economic growth, economic security, and financial stability is administrable enough to guide future Council decisions.

Failure scenario If those concepts remain broad and underdefined, they may expand the framework's discretion without giving regulated firms or the public a clear basis for predicting how those concepts will affect risk assessment.

Procedural implication Commenters can ask for definitions, limiting principles, and examples showing when "economic security" adds something distinct from ordinary financial-stability analysis.

Major claim: a cost-benefit test should gate section 113 determinations

Underlying assumption The benefits and costs of designation can be compared in a disciplined way before designation, even though the Council is acting under a preventive systemic-risk statute.

Failure scenario Some benefits of designation may be probabilistic, indirect, or crisis-avoidance based, which can make them difficult to quantify or compare to more immediate compliance costs.

Procedural implication Commenters can ask the Council to specify what cost categories, benefit categories, and evidentiary thresholds it expects to use and how it will treat uncertainty or non-quantifiable systemic benefits.

Underlying assumption The statute is better read to reserve designation for especially serious threats rather than the broader 2023 articulation.

Failure scenario If the new threshold is too demanding or too abstract, it may undercut early intervention or encourage disputes over what counts as "severe damage" instead of clarifying the standard.

Procedural implication Commenters can ask the Council to explain why the 2023 approach was inadequate and to identify concrete examples showing how the proposed threshold changes practical decisionmaking.
