Public Summary: TREAS-DO-2026-0232-0001

What This Document Does

  • Treasury proposes a rule to decide when a State stablecoin regulatory regime is “substantially similar” to the Federal framework under the GENIUS Act.
  • The proposal would create new Treasury regulations in 12 CFR parts 1520 and 1521.
  • Its most important move is definitional: Treasury says the “Federal regulatory framework” is not just the GENIUS Act itself. For most topics, States would be compared mainly against the Act plus OCC regulations and interpretations published in the Federal Register.
  • The proposal divides section 4(a) into uniform requirements, where State rules must closely track Federal standards, and State-calibrated requirements, where States keep more room to design their own approach.
  • Treasury also proposes broad principles for licensing, supervision, enforcement, custody, insolvency, transition to Federal oversight, and extra State requirements.

Why It Matters

  • This rule does not directly license or ban stablecoin issuers, but it will shape whether States can build their own regulatory programs instead of forcing issuers into a fully Federal track.
  • In practice, the proposal could decide how much regulatory diversity States have under the GENIUS Act.
  • The NPRM makes several judgment calls about why the OCC should be the main comparator, why some provisions are nationally uniform while others are left to State calibration, and how States are supposed to handle a Federal benchmark that may shift as OCC, Treasury, and the FRB finalize more GENIUS Act rules.
  • Those questions matter because a vague or moving benchmark can make certification harder for States and less predictable for issuers and the public.

Who Is Affected

  • State regulators that may want to certify their own payment stablecoin regimes.
  • State-chartered or nonbank firms that may prefer a State route instead of a Federal one, at least until they exceed the Act's size threshold.
  • Federal agencies whose GENIUS Act regulations may become the comparison baseline for State review.
  • Commenters focused on federalism, financial stability, and consumer protection in the stablecoin market.

Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

  1. Issue: Whether Treasury has justified using the OCC as the main Federal benchmark. Why it matters: The proposal says most State regimes should be measured against OCC materials, but the public-facing explanation is more structural than comparative. That choice could shape how much flexibility States actually retain. Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence or comparative analysis shows that an OCC-centered benchmark is the best fit for the likely population of State qualified payment stablecoin issuers?

  2. Issue: Whether the line between uniform and State-calibrated requirements is fully explained. Why it matters: Appendix A may look technical, but it is where Treasury turns broad statutory language into concrete limits on State policy choices. If the classifications are off, State flexibility could be narrower or looser than Congress intended. Practical question a commenter can ask: For each contested item in Appendix A, what is Treasury's legal basis for classifying it as uniform rather than State-calibrated, or vice versa?

  3. Issue: Whether the rule gives enough guidance for a changing Federal baseline. Why it matters: Treasury acknowledges that parallel Federal implementing rules may change over time. States need to know which version of the Federal framework controls when they design a regime or seek certification. Practical question a commenter can ask: Will Treasury adopt a transition rule or safe harbor specifying which Federal version applies during a pending State certification review?

  4. Issue: Whether excluding most non-Federal Register guidance makes the framework too incomplete or too ambiguous. Why it matters: Treasury is trying to avoid making States chase informal agency materials. That is sensible in one way, but it may also leave uncertainty about which supervisory interpretations actually matter. Practical question a commenter can ask: What exactly counts as an “interpretation” within the Federal framework, and which materials should States be expected to monitor in practice?

Step 2 — Essence of the Rule
Observed Facts

Treasury proposes to add 12 CFR parts 1520 and 1521 to implement section 4(c) of the GENIUS Act. The rule would:

  • define the “Federal regulatory framework” largely by reference to the GENIUS Act plus OCC regulations and interpretations published in the Federal Register, with Treasury and FRB materials controlling for specified topics;
  • define “State-level regulatory regime” broadly enough to include enforceable State statutes, regulations, and some guidance;
  • divide section 4(a) requirements into “uniform requirements” and “State-calibrated requirements”;
  • set broad-based principles for reserve assets, redemption, rehypothecation, reporting, capital, liquidity, risk management, activities, licensing, supervision, custody, insolvency, and transition to Federal oversight; and
  • allow States to impose additional requirements so long as they do not conflict with Federal law or break substantial similarity.

The NPRM also asks a long series of targeted questions about definitions, scope, the proper benchmark agency, State flexibility, and treatment of particular GENIUS Act provisions.

Inference

This is a consequential federalism-and-market-structure rule masquerading as a principles document. It does not directly license stablecoin issuers, but it will shape whether States can build workable parallel stablecoin regimes and how closely they must track future OCC and other Federal actions.
