What This Document Does

  • EPA proposes to move the deadline for a one-time TSCA section 8(d) health-and-safety reporting rule from May 22, 2026 to May 21, 2027.
  • The underlying rule, finalized in December 2024, requires manufacturers and importers of 16 listed chemicals to submit certain unpublished health and safety studies to EPA.
  • This new proposal does not change the list of chemicals or the basic reporting framework. It changes timing only.
  • EPA says the extra year is needed because it is reconsidering the scope of the existing rule and does not want companies preparing submissions under a rule that may soon be revised.
  • The supplied record also includes extracts from the earlier economic analyses for the 2024 rule, which show the rule was expected to cover 161 firms and 299 sites, with 71 firms expected to submit studies.

Why It Matters

  • This is a delay proposal, but it still matters because it postpones when EPA receives unpublished toxicity and safety studies that may inform TSCA section 6 work on these chemicals.
  • EPA's basic theory is straightforward: if the agency may soon revise the reporting rule, it may be wasteful to force companies to keep preparing for a deadline that could soon change.
  • The record is thinner on the next step in the argument. EPA says the current deadline is “infeasible,” but the proposal does not clearly describe what scope changes are under consideration or why they require a full extra year.
  • That creates a useful comment opportunity. A commenter does not need to oppose all deadline relief to ask EPA for a more specific explanation, a comparison with narrower alternatives, and a clearer discussion of public-interest costs from delayed reporting.
  • EPA also asks for comment on “reliance interests,” which signals that the agency knows this is an administrative-law issue. That is a practical opening for commenters to ask how the agency is weighing firms that prepared to comply, firms that sought more time, and members of the public who want the data sooner.

Who Is Affected

  • Manufacturers and importers of the 16 listed chemicals covered by the 2024 TSCA section 8(d) rule.
  • Companies that may already be searching files, reviewing studies, handling confidential-business-information claims, or planning submissions.
  • EPA program offices that expected to receive additional health and safety studies for these chemicals.
  • Workers, downstream users, researchers, advocates, and communities interested in timely chemical-risk information.
  • Trade groups and technical commenters who can speak to compliance burden, timing, study inventories, and the consequences of delay.

Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Claim A

Claim: EPA needs another year because the rule is under reconsideration and the current deadline is infeasible.

Underlying assumption: EPA has a sufficiently concrete reconsideration effort underway that regulated parties cannot reasonably prepare under the existing deadline.

Failure scenario: If reconsideration is only exploratory, or if the possible revisions are modest, commenters could argue EPA has not shown why a full additional year is necessary rather than a shorter extension, phased relief, or targeted amendment.

Procedural implication: EPA may need to explain the contemplated scope changes with more specificity to show that “infeasible” is more than a policy preference.

Claim B

Claim: Extending the deadline provides regulatory certainty and avoids duplicative or inconsistent reporting.

Underlying assumption: Firms are already spending substantial resources preparing for submissions that are likely to become obsolete under the contemplated revisions.

Failure scenario: If many firms have already done most of the search-and-collection work, or if the revisions would not materially change what data must be submitted, then the claimed certainty gains may be overstated and the delay may instead defer useful data without much private-sector benefit.

Procedural implication: Commenters can ask EPA to quantify what preparations have already occurred and what specific duplication it expects to avoid.

Claim C

Claim: The extension does not create new information collection burden.

Underlying assumption: Deferring the deadline changes timing only, not total burden, and does not generate additional uncertainty-management costs.

Failure scenario: Firms may incur extra hold costs, repeated legal review, repeat internal tracking, or re-work if the record is frozen and then reopened later under revised requirements.

Procedural implication: The PRA and burden-relief discussion would be stronger if EPA addressed timing-related transaction costs rather than only saying no new collection is imposed.

Claim D

Claim: EPA is not reopening the underlying rule except for the deadline.

Underlying assumption: The extension can be evaluated cleanly without revisiting the substantive need for the underlying data collection.

Failure scenario: EPA simultaneously says it is reconsidering the rule's scope and aligning it with new executive-policy directives. That can blur the line between a narrow deadline adjustment and a partial suspension of a substantive reporting regime.

Procedural implication: EPA should explain how commenters can assess the extension without knowing what revisions are being contemplated.

Claim E

Claim: Reliance interests are a material issue on which EPA seeks comment.

Underlying assumption: The public record does not yet contain enough information about who relied on the 2024 rule or the 2025 extensions.

Failure scenario: EPA may be shifting a central explanatory burden to commenters even though the agency likely already has significant information from prior compliance discussions and extension requests in the docket.

Procedural implication: Commenters can press EPA to summarize the reliance interests already visible in prior docket submissions, not just ask the public to fill the gap.

What This Document Does

  • EPA finalized a one-time TSCA section 8(d) reporting rule for 16 chemicals.
  • The rule requires manufacturers, including importers, to submit certain unpublished health and safety studies and study lists to EPA.
  • EPA says the studies will support later chemical work under TSCA section 6, including prioritization, risk evaluation, and risk management.
  • The rule is broad. It reaches firms that made or proposed to make the listed chemicals within the prior 10 years, not just current manufacturers.
  • It also reaches some studies involving mixtures, articles, and impurity contexts, as long as the listed chemical is specifically identified in the study.
  • EPA requires OECD Harmonized Template formatting for certain health and environmental effects information when the submission includes confidential-business-information claims and a template exists.

Why It Matters

  • This is an information-forcing rule. EPA is not banning these chemicals here; it is trying to collect unpublished studies that may change how the agency evaluates them later.
  • That means the value of the rule depends on two things at once: EPA needs the studies badly enough to justify the reporting burden, and the reporting scope needs to be tailored well enough that firms do not spend large amounts of time searching for low-value material.
  • EPA says the first-year burden is about $5.884 million and 70,630 hours. It also says 35 small businesses are affected, with 2 potentially above the 1% of revenue impact level.
  • The record looks real, not improvised. The export includes EPA's final-rule economic analysis and related support documents, so this is not a bare assertion-only rule.
  • The better criticism is narrower: EPA explains why it wants more data more clearly than it explains why some of its broadest scope choices are necessary.

Who Is Affected

  • Manufacturers and importers of the 16 listed chemicals.
  • Firms that handled those chemicals in the prior 10 years and may hold older unpublished studies.
  • Companies that may need to search records across multiple sites, review studies for confidentiality, and prepare electronic submissions.
  • Businesses dealing with these chemicals as impurities or within mixtures or articles, where reporting scope may be harder to interpret.
  • EPA staff and outside stakeholders who will later rely on the submitted studies for TSCA decisions.

Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

  • Issue: EPA kept reporting scope broad by covering impurity-only manufacture and any chemical grade or purity level. Why it matters: Those choices may capture useful risk information, but they can also create expensive search obligations in low-value contexts. Question to ask: What evidence shows that impurity-only and any-purity reporting will produce enough high-value studies to justify the added burden?

  • Issue: EPA says only studies in which the listed chemical is specifically identified must be reported, but the line between responsive and non-responsive studies may still be hard to apply in mixtures and articles. Why it matters: Ambiguity here can increase compliance cost and lead to uneven reporting across firms. Question to ask: Will EPA provide clearer implementation guidance or examples for mixture, article, and impurity scenarios?

  • Issue: EPA requires OECD Harmonized Template formatting for covered CBI studies, but not for non-CBI studies. Why it matters: That reduces burden for some submitters, but it may also leave EPA with less standardized non-CBI data that are harder to compare and use. Question to ask: Did EPA consider a lighter standardized format for non-CBI studies that would improve comparability without imposing full OHT burden?

  • Issue: EPA added 6PPD-quinone based on a citizen petition and aquatic-harm concerns, but the visible record is thinner on expected respondent numbers and study inventories for that chemical. Why it matters: If the burden estimate for that chemical is especially uncertain, the overall forecast may be less reliable than it looks. Question to ask: How did EPA estimate the likely reporting universe and expected study volume for 6PPD-quinone?

  • Issue: The burden analysis relies heavily on prior reporting-cycle assumptions and analogies. Why it matters: If actual reporting rates or study counts are higher than EPA expects, small-entity and workload impacts could be understated. Question to ask: What post-implementation monitoring will EPA use to determine whether actual burdens materially exceed the forecast?
