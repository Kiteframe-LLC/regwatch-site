What This Document Does

  • The SEC proposes to let public companies that now file quarterly Form 10-Q reports choose a different interim-reporting schedule: one semiannual report on a new Form 10-S instead of three 10-Q filings each year.
  • The proposal would still leave annual Form 10-K reporting in place. It also proposes related changes to Regulation S-X, transition-report rules, and technical references in other SEC forms so the new reporting option works in practice.
  • This is an optional model. A company could keep reporting quarterly if that remains best for its investors, analysts, lenders, or internal governance needs.
  • The SEC says the goal is to reduce reporting burden and give companies flexibility without abandoning core investor protections.

Why It Matters

  • This is a consequential disclosure proposal because quarterly reporting has been the SEC baseline since 1970. Moving to an optional semiannual model would change how often some investors get standardized, comparable interim financial information.
  • The strongest part of the SEC's case is straightforward: some issuers may spend substantial time and money producing three interim reports per year, and not every company may need the same reporting cadence. The notice especially points to emerging growth companies and smaller reporting companies as firms that may benefit from extra flexibility.
  • The main pressure point is the substitute for lost 10-Q disclosure. The SEC says investors also receive material-event disclosure through Form 8-K, Regulation FD, earnings releases, and annual reports. But the notice also admits that Item 2.02 earnings releases are not equivalent to Form 10-Q disclosure and may disappear for some issuers that switch.
  • Another important issue is how much switching the SEC expects. Its analysis assumes 20% of reporting companies would move to semiannual reporting, based partly on a 2019 Nasdaq survey and SEC judgment about which firms would still prefer quarterly disclosure. That is a plausible starting point, but still a soft basis for a number that drives the paperwork and economic analysis.

Who Is Affected

  • Public companies subject to Exchange Act periodic reporting, especially those that currently file Form 10-Q.
  • Emerging growth companies and smaller reporting companies, which the SEC repeatedly identifies as likely beneficiaries of additional flexibility.
  • Investors, analysts, lenders, and market intermediaries who rely on comparable interim disclosure.
  • Companies with limited analyst coverage or thinner information environments.

Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

  1. The SEC should explain why this option should apply to all eligible issuers rather than a narrower class. Why it matters: The notice's strongest burden-reduction rationale seems tied to smaller or emerging issuers, but the proposal extends the option much more broadly. Practical question a commenter can ask: Why is a universal option preferable to limiting semiannual reporting to smaller reporting companies, emerging growth companies, or a phased pilot group?

  2. The SEC should test the 20% adoption assumption more openly. Why it matters: The estimated savings, burdens, and investor effects all depend heavily on how many companies actually switch. Practical question a commenter can ask: Please provide sensitivity analysis showing how the rule's costs and benefits change if far fewer or far more than 20% of issuers elect semiannual reporting.

  3. The SEC should address the gap between 10-Q disclosure and voluntary quarterly disclosures more directly. Why it matters: The notice itself says Form 8-K earnings materials are not equivalent to Form 10-Q information and may disappear for some issuers after they switch. Practical question a commenter can ask: What investor-protection safeguards, if any, should apply when a company elects semiannual reporting and also stops regular quarterly earnings releases or calls?

  4. The SEC should analyze who is most likely to switch, not just how many companies might switch. Why it matters: If the firms that opt out of quarterly reporting are the ones with the weakest analyst coverage or thinnest trading, information losses may be concentrated where investors are already most vulnerable. Practical question a commenter can ask: Which issuer segments does the SEC expect to use Form 10-S first, and how would that affect investors in less-followed companies?

  5. The SEC should compare broader optionality with more tailored alternatives. Why it matters: The proposal mostly frames the choice as today's quarterly regime versus a broad optional semiannual regime, but middle-ground models appear possible. Practical question a commenter can ask: Did the SEC consider narrower options such as a smaller-company-only regime, phased implementation, or minimum quarterly disclosure standards for companies that elect semiannual reporting?

Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Claim

The SEC can reduce mandatory 10-Q cadence for willing issuers without materially undermining investor protection because other disclosure channels and issuer incentives will continue to supply relevant information.

Underlying assumption

Form 8-K, Regulation FD, voluntary earnings releases, earnings calls, and annual reporting together will provide enough timely and comparable information for many issuers.

Failure scenario

If issuers that switch to semiannual reporting also reduce or stop voluntary quarterly earnings disclosures, investors may lose a standardized, accountant-reviewed, comparable disclosure source without a reliable substitute.

Procedural implication

A high-value comment can ask the SEC to explain more concretely which information losses it considers acceptable and whether additional safeguards are needed for issuers that elect the new option.

Claim

The 20 percent adoption estimate is a reasonable basis for forecasting costs, savings, and market effects.

Underlying assumption

A 2019 survey of 183 listed companies plus qualitative judgment about investor expectations, industry practice, contractual obligations, and insider-trading concerns is a sufficiently grounded proxy for real adoption behavior.

Failure scenario

If actual uptake is concentrated in a narrow set of firms, or if uptake differs materially from 20 percent, the economic analysis may understate either information-loss effects or the savings case.

Procedural implication

Commenters can ask for sensitivity analysis using lower and higher adoption scenarios and for clearer discussion of which issuer segments are most likely to switch.

Claim

Offering the option to all eligible Exchange Act reporting companies is preferable to limiting the option to smaller or emerging issuers.

Underlying assumption

Issuer-specific self-selection will sort firms efficiently, making a universal option workable even if the largest expected beneficiaries are smaller reporting companies and emerging growth companies.

Failure scenario

If the strongest costs of reduced interim comparability fall on investors in thinly covered firms, or if larger issuers also switch without the same burden rationale, the all-issuer model may be overbroad relative to the articulated problem.

Procedural implication

Commenters can ask the SEC to compare a narrower eligibility design, a phase-in, or reporting triggers tied to issuer class.

Claim

Existing non-SEC reporting norms and obligations will naturally keep some issuers on quarterly-style disclosure even after the rule change.

Underlying assumption

Investor expectations, analyst pressure, exchange practices, contractual arrangements, and industry norms will preserve more frequent disclosure where it is most valued.

Failure scenario

If those constraints are uneven, firms with the weakest analyst following and thinnest information environments may be the ones most likely to opt out of quarterly reporting.

Procedural implication

The record would be stronger with more issuer-segment analysis on who is likely to switch and how that maps to investor vulnerability.
