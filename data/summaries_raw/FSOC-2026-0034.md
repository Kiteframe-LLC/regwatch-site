## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Major claim: an activities-based approach should come first

**Underlying assumption**
System-wide or activity-based responses are generally better calibrated than company-specific designation and can address many risks without direct entity designation.

**Failure scenario**
If a risk is concentrated in one firm or only becomes visible through firm-specific features, a front-loaded activities-based sequence could delay intervention or create uncertainty about when the Council will conclude that system-wide measures are inadequate.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask the Council to explain what evidence or triggers move a matter from activity review to entity-specific review and how long the Council expects that transition to take.

### Major claim: economic growth and economic security are valid components of financial-stability analysis

**Underlying assumption**
The relationship between economic growth, economic security, and financial stability is administrable enough to guide future Council decisions.

**Failure scenario**
If those concepts remain broad and underdefined, they may expand the framework's discretion without giving regulated firms or the public a clear basis for predicting how those concepts will affect risk assessment.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask for definitions, limiting principles, and examples showing when "economic security" adds something distinct from ordinary financial-stability analysis.

### Major claim: a cost-benefit test should gate section 113 determinations

**Underlying assumption**
The benefits and costs of designation can be compared in a disciplined way before designation, even though the Council is acting under a preventive systemic-risk statute.

**Failure scenario**
Some benefits of designation may be probabilistic, indirect, or crisis-avoidance based, which can make them difficult to quantify or compare to more immediate compliance costs.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask the Council to specify what cost categories, benefit categories, and evidentiary thresholds it expects to use and how it will treat uncertainty or non-quantifiable systemic benefits.

### Major claim: the higher "severe damage" threshold is the right interpretation of section 113

**Underlying assumption**
The statute is better read to reserve designation for especially serious threats rather than the broader 2023 articulation.

**Failure scenario**
If the new threshold is too demanding or too abstract, it may undercut early intervention or encourage disputes over what counts as "severe damage" instead of clarifying the standard.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask the Council to explain why the 2023 approach was inadequate and to identify concrete examples showing how the proposed threshold changes practical decisionmaking.

### Major claim: requesting written responses from primary regulators will improve outcomes

**Underlying assumption**
Primary regulators can respond promptly and effectively enough that an added interagency step improves risk management rather than slowing it.

**Failure scenario**
If response timing, adequacy standards, or follow-up consequences are unclear, the added step may create process without clear accountability.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask for timelines, minimum response content, and an explanation of what happens when a regulator declines to act or proposes incomplete action.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

**Observed Facts**

- The selected document is an NPRM-stage proposed interpretive guidance for the Financial Stability Oversight Council.
- It would replace the 2023 interpretive guidance on nonbank financial company determinations and rescind the separately issued 2023 analytic framework if finalized.
- It would keep the Council's 2012 procedural rule at 12 CFR 1310.1-23 in place while changing Appendix A guidance.
- It would prioritize an activities-based approach, restore a cost-benefit requirement before section 113 determination, reinterpret "threat to the financial stability of the United States" to require potential "severe damage on the broader U.S. economy," and add an agency-response step before some escalations.
- It also states that the proposal would not amend existing hearing procedures and describes reevaluation and hearing opportunities for designated firms.

**Inference**

- This proposal is less about imposing immediate substantive obligations on the public than about setting the internal decision architecture the Council expects to use when deciding whether and how to address nonbank financial risk.
- The key public-comment questions are therefore about discretion, clarity, and accountability: whether the guidance cabins the Council's choices enough to make future interventions predictable and reviewable.

**Evidence Quotes**

> "The Proposed Guidance would help ensure that the Council's work is clear, transparent, and analytically rigorous."

> "The Council would pursue entity-specific determinations under section 113 of the Dodd-Frank Act only if a potential risk to U.S. financial stability cannot be, or is not, adequately addressed through an activities-based approach."

> "The Council would make a determination under section 113 only if the expected benefits to financial stability from Federal Reserve supervision and prudential standards justify the expected costs that the determination would impose."

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**
Revise the Council's interpretive guidance so that future financial-stability oversight is more activities-based, more transparent, and more analytically rigorous than under the 2023 framework.

**Problem definition**
The notice treats the 2023 guidance and analytic framework as insufficiently aligned with the Council's preferred method for identifying and addressing risks, especially in how they handle activities-based review, designation thresholds, and analytical discipline.

**Data used**

- statutory text and prior FSOC guidance history;
- conceptual judgments about how financial stability relates to economic growth and economic security;
- process descriptions for agency coordination, company engagement, annual reevaluation, and hearings; and
- a qualitative judgment that a cost-benefit screen and higher threshold will improve rigor.

**Analytical method**

- compare the proposed guidance against the 2023 framework and, implicitly, against the 2019 guidance;
- use interpretive reasoning about statutory terms such as "threat" and "material financial distress";
- prioritize activities-based tools before designation; and
- require a cost-benefit comparison before final company-specific determination.

**Alternatives considered**

- the 2023 framework and guidance, which the proposal would replace;
- the 2019-style approach, which the proposal substantially revives in several areas; and
- primary-regulator action before entity designation.

**Agency conclusion**
The Council concludes that the proposed guidance would better support transparency, rigor, and system-wide risk management than the 2023 approach.

**Missing links / logical jumps**

- The notice describes several policy choices as improvements but gives only limited concrete examples showing how the 2023 approach failed in practice.
- The economic-security concept is introduced with broad language but without a clearly bounded methodology.
- The cost-benefit requirement is stated clearly as a gate, but the notice offers only limited detail on how the Council will compare uncertain systemic benefits against identifiable costs.
- The agency-response step is presented as a useful escalation mechanism, but the proposal gives little detail on timing, adequacy standards, or consequences for incomplete responses.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- The activities-based sequence could become a delay mechanism if the Council does not specify when an activity-focused process has failed.
- The economic-security concept could expand discretion without improving predictability if it is not bounded.
- The cost-benefit gate could overemphasize measurable private costs relative to hard-to-quantify systemic-risk benefits.
- The higher "severe damage" threshold could make intervention appear clearer in theory while making it harder in practice to act before systemic problems deepen.
- The primary-regulator-response step could become procedurally thin if response adequacy, timing, and follow-up are left vague.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Observed Fact**

- It would require the Council to compare expected benefits and costs before designation.

**Inference**

- These choices may create stronger incentives for early regulator engagement and for more explicit internal justification before designation.
- They may also create incentives to avoid designation where costs are easier to describe than crisis-prevention benefits, unless the Council clarifies how it will treat uncertain but potentially severe systemic harms.

- The record offers little concrete evidence about how the proposed framework would perform differently in practice.
- The economic-security addition is not supported by a defined methodology or examples.

## Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

- Missing baseline: the notice gives little concrete side-by-side analysis showing where the 2023 framework produced poorer results than the proposed approach.
- Missing quantification: the proposal does not quantify or meaningfully structure how expected financial-stability benefits will be compared to expected designation costs.
- Missing alternatives rationale: the notice does not deeply analyze narrower alternatives between the 2023 framework and the proposed 2019-style reset.
- Missing implementation detail: the record gives limited criteria for when regulator responses are enough, when activity-based measures are inadequate, and what qualifies as economic-security risk in this context.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

- **Unexplained inconsistency risk:** the Council proposes to replace the 2023 framework with a more 2019-like structure, but the notice gives only limited practical examples of why the earlier framework was inadequate.
- **APA arbitrary and capricious risk:** commenters may argue that concepts like economic security and "severe damage" remain too abstractly justified relative to their effect on future decisionmaking.
- **Response-to-comments risk:** if commenters ask for examples, thresholds, or bounded definitions and the final guidance does not address them, the record could look underexplained.
- **Authority mismatch risk:** this looks weaker than the other risks because the notice is heavily cited to Dodd-Frank authority, but disputes may still arise over whether the proposed cost-benefit gate or narrowed threat interpretation underweights the statute's preventive purpose.
