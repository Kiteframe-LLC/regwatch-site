## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Major claim: the 2024 ELG baseline materially understated unmanaged-CRL costs

**Underlying assumption**
The omitted capture-and-pumping costs, retirement assumptions, and treatment assumptions were large enough to change the BAT conclusion rather than merely refine it.

**Failure scenario**
If the baseline correction is directionally correct but overstated, or if the forgone pollution benefits remain larger than the preamble now suggests, the justification for moving from national limits to BPJ-style discretion weakens.

**Procedural implication**
EPA should show more clearly how the corrected baseline changes the actual BAT judgment, not just the cost totals.

### Major claim: Option 1 is better because site-specific BPJ is more realistic

**Underlying assumption**
State permitting authorities will have the data, expertise, and incentives to set coherent case-by-case BAT limits across highly variable sites.

**Failure scenario**
Different states or permit writers may reach inconsistent results, rely on uneven records, or treat similar facilities differently.

**Procedural implication**
EPA may need more explanation about guardrails for comparability, minimum expectations, and how BPJ decisions will remain tied to a national rationale.

### Major claim: Option 2 and Option 3 are economically unachievable industry-wide

**Underlying assumption**
The revised cost estimates are reliable enough to support rejection of both national approaches, and the costs are not offset by pollutant-reduction benefits or avoided downstream burdens.

**Failure scenario**
If the record shows certain facilities can comply at materially lower cost, or if costs are concentrated in a narrower subset of sites than EPA assumes, a more targeted national standard may remain plausible.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can press EPA on whether it rejected too much once it found problems with the prior across-the-board assumptions.

### Major claim: the rule can leave “functional equivalence” determinations outside scope

**Underlying assumption**
Permitting authorities can apply County of Maui case-by-case without generating substantial uncertainty about coverage.

**Failure scenario**
Facilities and communities may not know when NPDES obligations attach, which can delay or dilute implementation.

**Procedural implication**
EPA may not need to redefine Maui, but it may need clearer implementation guidance if the final rule continues to rest heavily on site-by-site determinations.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

EPA proposes to revise the 2024 steam electric ELG as it applies to unmanaged CRL at existing sources. The NPRM presents three options. Option 1, the preferred option, keeps national mercury and arsenic limits for pumped unmanaged CRL but uses permitting-authority BPJ for discharges that are the functional equivalent of a direct discharge through groundwater. Option 2 would retain the 2024 ELG mercury and arsenic limits for both categories. Option 3 would impose zero-discharge requirements, with spray dry evaporators as the BAT basis and interim numeric limitations. The proposal also clarifies that unmanaged-CRL limitations do not apply to retired power plants and do not apply to landfills closed by July 8, 2024, with BPJ continuing to apply in those settings.

### Inference

This is a deregulatory reconsideration of a very recent EPA rule. The practical move is away from a more uniform nationwide standard and toward broader state permitting discretion, justified mainly by corrected cost assumptions, uncertainty about groundwater capture and treatment feasibility, and concern about utility retirement and electric reliability.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**
Revise the 2024 unmanaged-CRL ELG so BAT requirements are, in EPA's current view, technologically realistic and economically achievable.

**Problem definition**
EPA now believes the 2024 rule used an incorrect and incomplete baseline, underestimated capture-and-pumping costs, over-assumed full groundwater capture and treatability, and did not adequately account for retired or closing utilities.

**Data used**
Updated cost assumptions, post-2024 retirement and treatment information, data received during the 2025 deadlines rule process, site variability information, and screening-level revenue-impact analysis. The preamble cites DCN SE12105 and DCN SE12127 for deeper support.

**Analytical method**
Compare three regulatory options against a 2024-rule baseline; assess plant and parent-entity cost-to-revenue ratios; evaluate economic achievability; consider uncertainty about unmanaged-CRL characterization and capture; and rely more heavily on BPJ where site variability is high.

**Alternatives considered**
Option 1 BPJ plus retained numeric limits for pumped unmanaged CRL; Option 2 retention of 2024 mercury and arsenic limits for both categories; Option 3 zero discharge; other alternative BAT technologies discussed but not preferred.

**Agency conclusion**
Option 1 is preferred because it reduces industry cost burdens and, in EPA's view, better accounts for site-specific conditions while still allowing permitting authorities to impose technology-based requirements.

### Missing links / jumps

The largest jump is from “the 2024 baseline was flawed” to “national limits should give way to broader case-by-case discretion.” The preamble makes that move plausible, but the available text does not fully show why narrower national revisions, clearer default guardrails, or more segmented requirements would be inadequate.

**Explanation of analytical thresholds:** Partially satisfied

**Consideration of foreseeable impacts:** Partially satisfied

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

### Regulatory objective: revise unmanaged-CRL limits to reflect corrected costs and site variability

**Alternatives evaluated**
EPA evaluates three principal options and mentions alternative technologies that could achieve the same pollutant reductions as Option 3 but at higher cost.

**Plausible alternatives not fully analyzed in the reviewed text**
Retain national limits only for facility subgroups with stronger evidence of feasible capture and treatment.
Adopt a rebuttable national floor with BPJ only for defined exceptional cases.
Keep more uniform nationwide standards but extend compliance timing or add variance-like mechanisms tied to documented site constraints.
Publish a more explicit national framework for BPJ decisions on functional-equivalent discharges.

**Evidence suggesting feasibility**
EPA already identifies recurring site factors permitting authorities should consider, which suggests a national decision framework could be made more concrete without eliminating site-specific adjustments.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Fact**
The rule is technically coherent in the sense that it identifies the relevant wastestream, distinguishes pumped unmanaged CRL from functional-equivalent discharges through groundwater, and matches each option to a different control approach: BPJ, chemical precipitation, or zero discharge via spray dry evaporators.

**Inference**
The technical weakness is not internal contradiction. It is that EPA uses variability and uncertainty both as a reason to reject more stringent national limits and as a reason to rely on decentralized case-by-case decisions, without fully showing what minimum technical consistency will remain.

**Annex A — EPA technical shortcut**
The proposal repeatedly says unmanaged CRL is unusually site-specific and difficult to characterize nationally. That may be true. But once EPA adopts that framing, commenters can ask whether the Agency should also provide stronger national guidance on how site-specific hydrology, plume age, pollutant speciation, and background groundwater conditions are supposed to be translated into actual permit limits.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Uncertain**

**Classification:** Mostly normal, with a meaningful policy reversal dynamic

**Observed Fact**

**Inference**

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

Permitting-authority BPJ decisions may become inconsistent across states and facilities.

Communities may face weaker protection if the shift from national limits to BPJ is not paired with clearer implementation guardrails.

Functional-equivalent discharges may remain underidentified because the rule leaves the threshold determination outside scope.

Economic-achievability conclusions may be attacked as underexplained if the final rule does not make the revised cost logic and forgone-benefit tradeoffs more legible.

Retired or closing facilities may pose long-duration contamination problems that are difficult to fit into EPA's usual revenue-based achievability analysis.

---

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Observed Fact**
Option 1 gives permitting authorities broad discretion over functional-equivalent discharges and specifically directs attention to factors such as local hydrology, plume age, pollutant concentrations, energy demand, consumer energy costs, and non-water-quality impacts.

**Inference**
That structure creates an incentive for both facilities and states to frame difficult unmanaged-CRL sites as uniquely constrained, energy-sensitive, or poorly suited for nationally standardized treatment expectations. That is not necessarily improper, but it does mean factual framing at the permit stage will matter a great deal.

The proposal also creates an incentive to contest whether a discharge is functionally equivalent at all, because once coverage is uncertain, the national ELG discussion may never reach the permitting stage.

---

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Overall assessment:** Moderate

**Strengths**
EPA identifies a concrete reason for reconsideration: a claimed flaw in the 2024 baseline and major uncertainty about unmanaged-CRL capture, treatment, and facility economics. The NPRM does not pretend certainty; it repeatedly solicits data and acknowledges site variability. The docket plainly contains a large support record.

**Weaknesses**
The most consequential conclusion in the NPRM is not merely that prior costs were understated, but that EPA should move toward materially weaker nationwide requirements. The available text does not fully show:

* how much corrected costs versus policy preference drives the shift
* how forgone pollutant reductions compare to cost relief
* how BPJ decisions will be made consistently
* why narrower national revisions would be insufficient

---

## Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

Only calling these gaps where support is incomplete or compressed in the reviewed materials:

1. **National-to-BPJ transition gap**
   The NPRM does not fully explain why identified flaws in the 2024 approach require a move toward case-by-case BAT for functional-equivalent discharges rather than a more bounded national revision.

2. **Forgone-benefits gap**
   The preamble is far more concrete about utility-cost relief than about the magnitude and distribution of forgone water-quality benefits from loosening the 2024 unmanaged-CRL approach.

3. **Implementation-consistency gap**
   The proposal relies heavily on permitting-authority judgment but gives limited visible detail on how similarly situated facilities should be treated across jurisdictions.

4. **Coverage-identification gap**
   EPA says the functional-equivalence determination is outside the rule's scope, but commenters may want more clarity on how that threshold question will be handled in practice.

5. **Post-retirement burden gap**
   EPA raises a serious issue about long-term treatment costs after plant retirement, but the available text does not fully show how those cases interact with cleanup responsibility, timing, and public-health tradeoffs.

---

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

This NPRM does **not** look facially lawless. The more realistic vulnerabilities are classic reasoned-decisionmaking issues:

* inadequate explanation for replacing a recent national approach with broader BPJ discretion
* underexplained linkage between corrected costs and the degree of regulatory rollback
* insufficient explanation of how the rule will preserve consistent treatment when functional-equivalent coverage and BAT limits are both highly case-specific
* weakly developed discussion of forgone benefits and affected communities relative to utility cost relief

The vulnerability is mainly arbitrary-and-capricious risk through incompleteness or unexplained inconsistency, not a clean statutory-authority defect.

## Step 16 — Evidence Requests

A commenter could request:

* a clearer side-by-side explanation of what corrected baseline assumptions changed between the 2024 ELG and this proposal
* the basis for concluding BPJ is preferable to more bounded national revisions
* a clearer accounting of forgone pollutant reductions and downstream burdens under Option 1 compared with Option 2 or Option 3
* more detail on how permitting authorities should identify functional-equivalent discharges consistently
* a framework for how state permit writers should weigh site hydrology, plume age, background contaminants, and energy-reliability concerns
* more detail on how post-retirement or post-closure unmanaged-CRL treatment obligations are expected to work in practice

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

### 1. Ask EPA to connect the corrected baseline to the exact regulatory rollback it proposes

Constructive angle: accept that the 2024 cost baseline may need correction, but ask EPA to explain why the correction supports Option 1 specifically rather than a narrower revision of the national standard.

### 2. Ask EPA to explain the practical consequences of moving to BPJ for functional-equivalent discharges

Request clearer national guardrails for consistency, minimum factors, and comparable treatment across jurisdictions.

### 3. Ask EPA to show the pollution-control tradeoff more transparently

The NPRM is concrete about cost relief. Commenters can ask EPA to be equally concrete about pollutant-load, benefit, and community-protection consequences of stepping back from the 2024 approach.

### 4. Ask EPA to clarify functional-equivalence implementation

EPA may be right that County of Maui determinations are case-specific, but commenters can still ask for implementation guidance so the final rule does not leave a major threshold question too murky.

### 5. Ask EPA to address long-duration contamination at retired facilities more directly

EPA raises an important point about facilities that stop earning revenue while unmanaged CRL continues. Commenters can ask how the Agency expects protection, funding, and accountability to work in those cases.

## Step 18 — Decision Brief

### Leverage Point 1: corrected cost baseline versus scope of rollback

**Key NPRM sentences**
“The EPA also identified a flaw in the baseline utilized by the 2024 ELG … by omitting capture and pumping costs….”
“The EPA is not proposing Option 2 … because total costs to the industry as a whole are unacceptably high….”

**Routine description**
The comment opportunity is not to deny that the 2024 baseline may have had problems. It is to ask EPA to show why those problems justify the specific regulatory retreat it now prefers.

**Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy**
This is mainly procedural adequacy and reasoned explanation. If EPA is changing course, it should explain why this amount of change follows from the corrected facts.

**Real-world consequence**
Without that link, the final rule may look less like a targeted correction and more like an underexplained policy reversal.

**Worth a comment?** High

---

### Leverage Point 2: BPJ discretion for functional-equivalent discharges

**Key NPRM sentences**
“Option 1 would rely on discretion of the permitting authority to set case-by-case BAT limitations….”
“The threshold standard for the ‘functional equivalence’ determination is outside the scope of this rule.”

**Routine description**
EPA is proposing to lean heavily on permit-stage judgment while declining to resolve the threshold coverage test in the rule itself.

**Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy**
This is a regulatory-effectiveness and procedural-adequacy issue. The more discretion EPA creates, the more it should explain how that discretion will be bounded and made consistent.

**Real-world consequence**
If left vague, similarly situated sites may receive different treatment, and some discharges may never be clearly addressed.

**Worth a comment?** High

---

### Leverage Point 3: cost relief versus forgone pollution controls

**Key NPRM sentences**
The rule is “estimated to reduce costs by $446 to $1,090 million dollars annually at a 3 percent discount rate.”
EPA says Option 1 reduces impacts relative to the baseline option.

**Routine description**
EPA has clearly quantified cost relief at a headline level. The comment opportunity is to ask for equally clear treatment of what protection is being traded away or delayed.

**Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy**
This is partly benefit-cost transparency and partly record adequacy.

**Real-world consequence**
A final rule that foregrounds utility savings but backgrounds water-quality consequences will be harder to evaluate on the merits.

**Worth a comment?** High

---

### Leverage Point 4: retired plants and long-duration unmanaged CRL

**Key NPRM sentences**
“The EPA is concerned that the 2024 ELG did not fully account for the lack of revenue generated by closed utilities….”
The proposal states unmanaged-CRL limitations do not apply to retired power plants and BPJ continues to apply.

**Routine description**
EPA is surfacing a real problem: contamination can continue after electricity generation and revenue stop. That does not automatically answer what the final policy should be.

**Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy**
This is mostly about implementation realism and public accountability.

**Real-world consequence**
If the final rule is not clear about these cases, communities may face the longest-lived unmanaged-CRL problems under the least determinate regulatory framework.

**Worth a comment?** Moderate to high

---

## Bottom line

This NPRM has a substantial record and a plausible basis for reconsidering the 2024 unmanaged-CRL approach. The best public comments are unlikely to argue that EPA may never revisit its prior ELG. The stronger approach is narrower and more concrete:

* ask EPA to show why corrected costs justify this scale of rollback
* ask for clearer national guardrails if BPJ discretion remains central
* ask for a more legible account of forgone pollution reductions and community impacts
* and ask how retired-facility and functional-equivalence cases will work in practice

Those are the places where the administrative record still looks most vulnerable and most improvable.
