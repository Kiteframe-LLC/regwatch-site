## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Major claim: Alaska's program satisfies all requirements for final authorization

**Underlying assumption**

The attachments and cross-referenced state materials fully support EPA's equivalence, consistency, and enforcement findings even though the NPRM summarizes them at a high level.

**Failure scenario**

The underlying application materials reveal unresolved mismatches, ambiguities, or enforcement limitations that the preamble does not surface clearly enough for public evaluation.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask EPA to explain the key equivalence findings in a more public-facing way instead of relying mainly on incorporated docket materials.

### Major claim: the carve-out map between state and federal authority is clear enough for regulated parties

**Underlying assumption**

Facilities, transporters, and generators will understand which requirements move to Alaska and which remain federal, including HSWA provisions, delisting, e-manifest functions, and Indian-country or exclusive-jurisdiction areas.

**Failure scenario**

The split regime creates confusion about who issues permits, who handles delisting or import/export matters, and what rules apply at facilities with mixed federal and state obligations.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask EPA to provide a cleaner operational crosswalk for the post-authorization division of authority.

### Major claim: Alaska's state-only electronic-waste universal-waste approach is sufficiently justified

**Underlying assumption**

EPA's statement that Alaska may include electronic waste in its universal-waste program without adopting subpart G is supported by an adequate comparison to the 40 CFR 273.81 factors.

**Failure scenario**

The final rule approves the state-only approach without showing enough analysis of whether the electronic-waste category fits the federal criteria EPA says are relevant.

**Procedural implication**

The record would be stronger if EPA summarized the specific 273.81-style factors it considered and how Alaska's program satisfied them.

### Major claim: EPA has shown adequate enforcement capacity

**Underlying assumption**

The attorney general statement, statutes, memorandum of agreement, and data-sharing arrangements are sufficient to ensure real-world enforcement, oversight, and coordination.

**Failure scenario**

The formal authorities exist on paper, but the public-facing record does not explain practical oversight implementation well enough to reassure commenters about data quality, permitting transitions, and split-jurisdiction enforcement.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask EPA to explain how oversight, quarterly reviews, and corrective actions will function after authorization takes effect.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

EPA proposes to grant Alaska final authorization to run its hazardous-waste program in lieu of most of the federal Subtitle C program. EPA says Alaska adopted federal hazardous-waste regulations largely verbatim through July 26, 2024, with some state-specific modifications. The NPRM identifies several state provisions as more stringent, including annual reporting and notification requirements and incorporation of groundwater-monitoring guidance. It identifies Alaska's siting regime for new hazardous-waste facilities as broader in scope rather than part of the federally authorized program. It also describes Alaska's addition of electronic waste as a state-only universal-waste stream.

The proposal says some functions remain federal even after authorization, including certain import/export rules, land-disposal restrictions, e-manifest functions, delisting, Indian-country implementation, and exclusive federal-jurisdiction areas. The available memorandum of agreement says EPA retains independent oversight and enforcement authority, ongoing permit oversight, corrective-action review, and quarterly review meetings with the State.

### Inference

This is partly a legal-administrative approval rule and partly a real allocation of regulatory power. The major public-interest question is not whether Alaska should be allowed to run a hazardous-waste program at all, but whether EPA has explained clearly enough how the unusual Alaska-specific features, federal carve-outs, and oversight backstops will work in practice.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**
Allow Alaska to operate a federally approved hazardous-waste program in lieu of most of the federal program.

**Problem definition**
Alaska currently lacks an authorized hazardous-waste program, so EPA remains the direct implementing authority for Subtitle C requirements that the State now seeks to administer.

**Data used**
Alaska's application materials, program description, statutes and regulations, amended attorney general statement, memorandum of agreement, and state public-participation materials.

**Analytical method**
EPA compares Alaska's program against RCRA section 3006 and 40 CFR part 271 requirements, identifies more-stringent and broader-in-scope provisions, reserves federal-only areas, and evaluates whether the State has adequate enforcement authority.

**Alternatives considered**
The visible record mainly presents authorization versus non-authorization. The NPRM does not deeply examine narrower forms of approval, staged clarifications, or targeted conditions addressing the most unusual state-only features.

**Agency conclusion**
Alaska's program is equivalent, consistent, and enforceable enough to receive final authorization, subject to retained EPA authority for enumerated federal areas.

### Missing links / unsupported premises / logical jumps

The main jumps are:

1. from extensive underlying application materials to a very compressed public explanation of equivalence and enforcement;
2. from EPA's acknowledgment of multiple retained federal functions to confidence that the post-authorization boundary line will remain operationally clear;
3. from Alaska's electronic-waste addition to confidence that the state-only universal-waste approach has been evaluated with enough transparency;
4. from formal legal authority and MOA oversight provisions to assurance about day-to-day implementation, permit transition, and data quality.
