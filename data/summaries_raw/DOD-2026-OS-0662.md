## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim A

**Claim:** The system needs new `(k)(1)` and `(k)(2)` exemptions because some records may contain classified information and law-enforcement investigatory material.

**Underlying assumption:** The current `(j)(2)` exemption does not fully cover the records or situations the agency expects this renamed system to contain.

**Failure scenario:** If the existing `(j)(2)` exemption already addresses most of the relevant records, commenters could argue the notice has not shown why additional exemption layers are needed or what distinct gap they fill.

**Procedural implication:** The agency may need to explain the marginal work done by `(k)(1)` and `(k)(2)` beyond the continuing `(j)(2)` exemption.

### Claim B

**Claim:** Allowing notice, access, amendment, or accounting of disclosures could harm national security or impede criminal investigations.

**Underlying assumption:** The affected records will predictably include sensitive information whose disclosure would create these harms in real cases, not only in abstract statutory terms.

**Failure scenario:** If the harms are framed only generically, the rule may look formulaic rather than tailored to this particular system of records.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask for examples of record categories or scenarios, even at a high level, showing why each claimed exemption is practically necessary.

### Claim C

**Claim:** The exemptions remain limited because records are exempt only to the extent the purposes underlying the exemption pertain to the record.

**Underlying assumption:** The DoD OIG has workable internal criteria for deciding when a record falls inside or outside a claimed exemption.

**Failure scenario:** Without visible limiting principles, a record-specific qualifier can become hard for the public to assess and easy for the agency to apply broadly.

**Procedural implication:** Comments can ask for clearer decision rules, examples, or procedural safeguards for partial access and segregability.

### Claim D

**Claim:** It is not always possible in early investigatory stages to determine relevance and necessity, so exemption from `(e)(1)` is needed.

**Underlying assumption:** Overcollection risk is outweighed by the need to preserve investigative leads, patterns, and potentially exculpatory material.

**Failure scenario:** A broad relevance exception can weaken Privacy Act minimization expectations if the agency does not explain retention or review controls.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask how the system manages stale, immaterial, or misclassified information once investigations mature.

### Claim E

**Claim:** The rule has no significant economic effect on small entities because it concerns internal administration of DoD Privacy Act systems.

**Underlying assumption:** The only meaningful effects are intra-governmental and individual-rights related rather than business-compliance related.

**Failure scenario:** Even if there is no classic small-business compliance burden, the rule still affects individuals' access and amendment rights; the explanation may understate real-world stakes by treating the rule as merely internal administration.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters can press the agency to discuss rights impacts more directly even if economic-analysis statutes do not strongly apply.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

* DoD proposes to revise `32 CFR 310.28(c)(1)` so system `CIG-04` is renamed `Inspector General Criminal Investigation Records`.
* The proposal would continue the existing `5 U.S.C. 552a(j)(2)` exemption and add exemptions under `5 U.S.C. 552a(k)(1)` and `(k)(2)`.
* The proposed regulatory text would exempt the system from specified Privacy Act provisions governing disclosure accounting, access, amendment, notice, recordkeeping, agency rules, and civil remedies, with the exact list varying by exemption authority.
* The notice says the system contains criminal-investigation records and may include properly classified information and investigatory material compiled for law-enforcement purposes.
* The notice states that a modified system-of-records notice for `CIG-04` is published elsewhere in the same Federal Register issue.
* The rule includes standard determinations that it is not significant under Executive Order 12866, not a major rule under the CRA, imposes no PRA burden, and does not require a regulatory flexibility analysis.

### Inference

This NPRM is a records-governance rule that narrows individual Privacy Act rights for a criminal-investigation records system. The agency's legal theory is conventional, but the practical comment leverage is whether the proposed exemptions are sufficiently explained and tailored, especially because the system already carries an existing `(j)(2)` exemption and the notice gives mostly generalized harm statements rather than system-specific examples.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

**Alternatives evaluated:** The notice visibly evaluates only the proposed addition of `(k)(1)` and `(k)(2)` exemptions, paired with a continuing `(j)(2)` exemption.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed:**

* relying on the existing `(j)(2)` exemption plus narrower use of `(k)(1)` only for records that are actually classified;
* adding examples or limiting text describing when partial access or segregable release would remain possible;
* exempting fewer subsections under `(k)(2)` if some investigatory records do not present the same level of sensitivity;

**Evidence suggesting feasibility:** The notice already distinguishes among subsection-specific harms and states that records are exempt only to the extent the exemption rationale pertains to the record. That suggests the agency could explain narrower operational boundaries if it chose to do so.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Fact:** The proposed rule is internally coherent as a legal amendment: it updates the system name, lists specific Privacy Act subsections, and provides paragraph-by-paragraph explanations for the claimed exemptions.

**Observed Fact:** The explanation is strongest where it links access or accounting rights to risks of tipping off investigative subjects or exposing classified information.

**Inference:** The weaker point is not legal structure but calibration. The same notice claims both broad investigatory need and record-specific tailoring without giving the public much visibility into how those two ideas are reconciled operationally.

**Potential inconsistency:** The system is said to need continued `(j)(2)` coverage and added `(k)(1)` and `(k)(2)` coverage, yet the record does not clearly separate which categories of records fall under each theory or why the overlap is necessary.

## Step 7 — Legal / Authority Check

**Observed Fact:** The proposal relies on explicit Privacy Act exemption authorities: `5 U.S.C. 552a(j)(2)`, `(k)(1)`, and `(k)(2)`.

**Observed Fact:** The notice explains the legal predicates for `(k)(1)` and `(k)(2)`: properly classified information and investigatory material compiled for law-enforcement purposes outside the full scope of `(j)(2)`.

**Inference:** The legal authority basis appears facially sound. The more vulnerable issue is whether the administrative record adequately explains necessity and tailoring, especially given the overlap among the cited exemption authorities.

**Comment leverage:** Ask the agency to explain what records fall within `(k)(2)` but outside `(j)(2)`, and how it will ensure the broader exemption authorities are not applied more broadly than necessary.

## Step 10 — Distributional / Practical Effects

**Observed Fact:** The proposal does not impose ordinary compliance costs on private firms; it mainly affects how individuals can obtain notice, access, amendment, and disclosure-accounting information about DoD OIG criminal-investigation records.

**Observed Fact:** The notice emphasizes harms to investigations, witnesses, informants, and national security.

**Inference:** The most meaningful practical tradeoff is between investigative secrecy and individual transparency rights. People whose records are retained in the system may face reduced ability to inspect or challenge information that affects them.

**Unknown:** Whether the system also captures records about complainants, witnesses, or third parties whose interests differ from those of investigative subjects and might warrant more nuanced treatment.

**Potential unacknowledged weakness:**

* the notice provides limited public-facing explanation of how record-specific tailoring will work;

**Unknown:**

## Step 12 — Best Comment Opportunities

* Ask DoD to explain what record categories justify `(k)(1)` and `(k)(2)` coverage beyond the continuing `(j)(2)` exemption.
* Ask for concrete examples, at a high level, of when access or amendment would create the harms described in the notice.
* Ask the agency to explain how it will handle mixed files, segregable information, and closed investigations when applying the exemptions.
* Ask whether the companion system-of-records notice changes retention, data sources, or information categories in ways that make the new exemptions newly necessary.
* Ask the agency to describe any internal review or periodic reassessment process used to determine whether exemption grounds still pertain to a record.

## Step 13 — Bottom Line

**Observed Fact:** The proposal rests on explicit statutory exemption authorities and provides paragraph-specific legal explanations for each claimed withholding rationale.

**Inference:** The rule is not obviously unlawful on its face, but the administrative record is thin on necessity and tailoring. The clearest public-value comments would push DoD to explain what the new exemptions do that the old exemption does not, and how the agency will avoid overbroad application in practice.

**Bottom-line assessment:** This is comment-worthy, especially for transparency, due-process, civil-liberties, and records-governance commenters. The strongest comments are likely to request narrower tailoring and clearer operational safeguards rather than challenge the existence of any exemption authority at all.
