## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

- The rule is technically coherent at a high level: it maps proposed provisions to specific CFR sections and separates them by topic.
- The weak point is not internal contradiction so much as reliance on cross-references, data fallback methods, and later adjustments.
- Commenters can still ask CMS to publish the fallback hierarchy for sparse data, the cross-walk assumptions for billing units, and the validation steps for later corrections.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

- The rule has strong legal grounding in the IRA and Social Security Act sections CMS cites.
- The available text does not create an obvious authority problem; the stronger question is whether each operational choice is adequately explained as a statutory implementation choice rather than a policy preference.
- Comments should focus on whether each new codified rule is tied to a specific statutory provision or to a broader administrative convenience rationale.

## Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

- Procedurally, this looks like a normal NPRM: CMS proposes text, invites comment, and says it will finalize later.
- The transition from 2026-2028 guidance to 2029+ regulation is clear enough to count as ordinary rulemaking practice, not a procedural defect by itself.
- The main procedural question is whether the notice gives commenters enough detail to respond meaningfully on each subtopic.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- **Sparse-data failure:** If the fallback calculations are wrong, some selected drugs or HCPCS codes could be assigned the wrong MFP application method.
- **Gatekeeping failure:** If bona fide marketing review is too discretionary, CMS could create an unpredictable compliance gate.
- **Timing failure:** If the 2029 transition is not phased carefully, manufacturers and acquiring entities may have to retool processes on a compressed schedule.
- **Classification failure:** If off-label use, new formulations, or small-biotech status are defined too loosely, the wrong products could fall into the wrong track.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

- Manufacturers are incentivized to want objective criteria, predictable timing, and clear appeal paths.
- CMS is incentivized to centralize control over the most ambiguous steps, especially review, calculation, and penalty triggers.
- Acquiring entities are incentivized to know exactly when transfer responsibility attaches and what information they must inherit.
- Commenters can point out where incentives are aligned and where ambiguity could distort compliance behavior.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

- The statutory recitations are strong, and the summary shows CMS knows which policy areas it is addressing.
- The evidence is thinner on why the chosen implementation mechanics are the best choices among reasonable alternatives.
- The strongest evidence in the notice is for the existence of the problem and the need to codify; the weaker evidence is for the exact shape of several operational solutions.

## Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

- The available record does not fully spell out how CMS will validate sparse-data estimates after the first calculation.
- The record does not fully explain how often the bona fide marketing review will run or what concrete inputs count as sufficient.
- The record does not fully quantify the administrative burden of transfer-of-responsibility provisions or the small-biotech floor.
- Those gaps are worth comments because they affect whether the rule can be implemented cleanly at scale.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

- A commenter could argue that some provisions are justified at a high level but not yet supported by a detailed problem-to-remedy chain.
- A commenter could also argue that the most vulnerable parts are the ones that depend on discretion, cross-walks, and fallback data rather than on bright-line statutory text.
- The best litigation-adjacent comment points are therefore the explanation gaps, not any obvious authority defect.
