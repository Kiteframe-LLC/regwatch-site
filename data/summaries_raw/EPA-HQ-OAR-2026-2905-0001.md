# Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim: “The EPA did not intend to subject refrigerant-containing road and intermodal container TRUs to the leak repair requirements.”

Underlying assumption

- Prior rulemaking materials support EPA’s statement of original intent strongly enough to justify a clarifying exemption.

Failure scenario

- If the prior record shows EPA knowingly chose the 15-pound threshold with awareness that many road/intermodal TRUs exceeded it, the current proposal is less a clarification and more a policy reversal requiring fuller explanation.

Procedural implication

- Commenters can ask EPA to point to the exact prior-rule record passages showing that TRUs were assumed outside the leak repair program by design rather than merely overlooked.

### Claim: “All road and intermodal container TRUs would be exempt, regardless of charge size.”

Underlying assumption

- Operational characteristics, service patterns, and emissions significance justify categorical treatment instead of a charge-size threshold for this equipment class.

Failure scenario

- If larger-charge TRUs or closely related rail TRUs pose similar leak-management concerns as other regulated appliances, a blanket exemption may look under-explained.

Procedural implication

- The main stress point is line drawing: why exempt this class entirely while leaving other mobile or refrigerated equipment categories in or leaving rail TRUs unresolved.

### Claim: “The EPA incorrectly assumed” the charge-size distribution.

Underlying assumption

- Stakeholder-supplied equipment data are reliable enough to update the agency’s factual baseline.

Failure scenario

- If the `360,000` units or `15-18 pound` range are not well documented, the proposal rests on a corrected assumption that is itself only partially substantiated.

Procedural implication

- A commenter can support or challenge the exemption by supplying fleet, leak-rate, and charge-size data instead of arguing only at a high level.

### Claim: “The EPA estimates cost savings of this rule of $90 million per year.”

Underlying assumption

- A simplified adaptation of the 2024 RIA leak-inspection cost methodology gives a useful proposal-stage estimate.

Failure scenario

- If monitoring, recordkeeping, downtime, repair timing, and leak-threshold distributions differ materially from the simplified assumptions, the estimate may understate or overstate burden reduction.

Procedural implication

- This looks like a **Deliberate solicitation** of better cost data rather than a fatal defect, but it remains a meaningful leverage point because EPA is using the preliminary estimate to support a categorical exemption.

### Claim: “The EPA has not assessed market impacts … outside of the preliminary engineering cost estimates.”

Underlying assumption

- Market effects are secondary in a narrow deregulatory action and can be explored through comments without undermining the proposal’s basic logic.

Failure scenario

- If the proposal materially changes transportation costs, competitive conditions, or refrigerant-emissions behavior, the absence of a fuller market analysis may become a more serious record gap.

Procedural implication

- The notice is candid about uncertainty, which lowers litigation-style risk somewhat, but it also invites targeted comments asking EPA to finish the analysis before finalizing.

# Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

- EPA proposes to amend `40 CFR 84.106(a)(3)` so that road and intermodal container TRUs are explicitly exempt from HFC leak repair requirements.
- The proposal is limited to this exemption and does not reopen the rest of the 2024 ER&R rule.
- EPA says the 2024 analysis assumed road/intermodal TRUs were at or below the `15-pound` threshold, but stakeholders later indicated roughly `360,000` units operate in the `15-18 pound` range.
- EPA gives a preliminary burden-reduction estimate of about `$90 million` annually and asks for comment on cost savings, environmental impacts, leak rates, charge sizes, repair frequency, and whether rail TRUs should also be exempted.
- The notice cites AIM Act subsection `(h)` as authority and frames the change as a clarification of applicability.

### Inference

- The practical effect is to remove a large class of refrigerated transport equipment from a leak-repair regime that otherwise applies to appliances with a full charge of `15 pounds or more`.
- The proposal is narrow enough that EPA is using a relatively lean analysis package, but it depends heavily on whether the revised factual picture about TRU charge sizes is accurate.

- EPA requests comment on rail TRUs, which signals unresolved boundary logic between road/intermodal units and nearby comparator categories.

- **Transparent methodology:** EPA explains the rough `$250` per-unit assumption and admits it has not completed a broader market-impact analysis.

# Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

### Regulatory objective

Avoid unintended inclusion of road and intermodal TRUs in the leak-repair program.

### Alternatives evaluated

- Explicit exemption for road and intermodal container TRUs.
- Possible future consideration of rail TRUs via comment solicitation.

### Plausible alternatives

- Raise the applicability threshold for some or all transport refrigeration equipment instead of exempting a class categorically.
- Exempt only road/intermodal TRUs below a higher charge-size band.
- Create a tailored compliance approach for transport refrigeration rather than a full exemption.
- Explicitly include or exclude rail TRUs in the same action instead of postponing that scope question.

### Evidence suggesting feasibility

- Carrier’s petition explicitly asks EPA to consider either a specific TRU exemption or raising the threshold to `30 pounds`.
- EPA’s request for information on rail TRU charge sizes and unit counts suggests the agency recognizes adjacent scope alternatives exist.

# Step 6 — Technical Coherence

### Observed Fact

- EPA states that roughly `360,000` road/intermodal TRUs fall between `15` and `18` pounds and that, assuming an average `17-pound` charge, they represent about `3,000 metric tons` of installed charge above the threshold.
- EPA compares that figure with approximately `312,000 metric tons` of installed charge for all appliances over `15 pounds` covered by the 2024 RIA addendum.
- The Vintaging Model peer-review summary says refrigerated transport end uses contributed about `5%` of total ODS-substitute emissions in 2020 and updated road-transport annual loss rates to `15%` for HFC road transport vintages starting in 2000.

### Inference

- EPA is implicitly arguing that this equipment class is numerous but comparatively small in total refrigerant mass, which tends to reduce the apparent environmental consequence of exemption relative to the broader rule.
- That does not eliminate the need to explain leak behavior, because a large number of smaller units can still create meaningful cumulative emissions depending on servicing patterns and leak rates.

### Assessment

- The proposal is technically coherent at a high level, but the environmental side of the justification is thinner than the cost side.

# Step 7 — Legal Grounding

### Statutory authority

- Observed Fact: The NPRM invokes subsection `(h)` of the AIM Act and proposes a targeted amendment to existing ER&R regulations under `40 CFR part 84`.
- Inference: EPA appears to be acting within the same statutory program it used for the 2024 management rule, so this is not a facial authority mismatch.

### Legal interpretation

- Observed Fact: EPA frames the action as a clarification of applicability and repeatedly says it is not reopening the 2024 rule more broadly.
- Inference: The main legal vulnerability is not lack of authority; it is whether EPA has adequately explained the switch from a threshold-based regime to a class-based exemption for these units.

# Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

- Observed Fact: This is a standard NPRM with a defined comment deadline, hearing request procedure, and an express list of questions for commenters.
- Observed Fact: EPA openly states where its analysis is preliminary and requests data on leak rates, charge sizes, repairs, costs, and rail TRUs.
- Inference: Most of the uncertainty here looks like **Deliberate solicitation** during notice-and-comment rather than concealment of a final agency determination.
- Inference: The proposal would look procedurally stronger if EPA finalizes only after docketing a more complete response to the cost and environmental data it is seeking.

# Step 9 — Failure Modes

- If the `360,000`-unit estimate is too high, EPA may overstate the burden reduction from exemption.
- If leak rates for the exempted population are materially higher than EPA expects, the environmental tradeoff may be understated.
- If rail TRUs are similarly situated but treated differently without explanation, scope inconsistency could become a recurring objection.
- If the prior-rule record does not support EPA’s “did not intend” framing, the agency may need a fuller justification than simple clarification language.

# Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

- Exemption removes compliance incentives to calculate leak rates and repair leaks on this equipment when leak rates exceed the threshold.
- Owners and operators would still have ordinary maintenance incentives because refrigerant loss is costly, but private incentives do not fully substitute for a leak-repair standard.
- The proposal likely reduces administrative burden for fleets, lessors, maintenance providers, and some small entities operating refrigerated transport equipment.
- The line between routine maintenance and regulatory compliance becomes clearer for road/intermodal fleets, but potentially less clear for rail TRUs until EPA resolves that comparator.

# Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

### Observed Fact

- EPA relies on a prior EPA analytical framework, a Vintaging Model support document, and stakeholder-supplied briefing/petition materials.
- EPA expressly says it “has not assessed market impacts of this proposal outside of the preliminary engineering cost estimates described above.”

### Assessment

- The evidentiary record is adequate for an NPRM-stage proposal, but it is not especially deep on proposal-specific data verification.
- The strongest record support is the candid acknowledgment that the prior charge-size assumption appears wrong.
- The weakest record support is the absence of a fully developed EPA analysis comparing categorical exemption with narrower alternatives.

# Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

- Exact source support for the `360,000` road/intermodal TRU figure and the `15-18 pound` distribution should be clearer in the docket narrative.
- Proposal-specific emissions implications of the exemption are not quantified in a transparent way.
- The NPRM does not explain why a class exemption is preferable to a revised threshold or tailored transport-refrigeration compliance mechanism.
- The record does not yet show a complete comparison between road/intermodal TRUs and rail TRUs.

# Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

- **Moderate APA-style explanation risk:** EPA is changing practical coverage and should explain why categorical exemption is the right remedy for the corrected factual premise.
- **Moderate cost-analysis vulnerability:** EPA’s own text labels its estimate preliminary and incomplete, which is acceptable at proposal stage but needs strengthening for finalization.
- **Lower pure-authority risk:** The proposal appears to fit within the same AIM Act authority as the existing rule.
- **Lower procedural ambush risk:** EPA is openly requesting comment on the main disputed facts and adjacent rail-TRU issue.

- Focus: verify the `360,000`-unit and `15-18 pound` assumptions.
- Why it matters: those figures drive both the burden-reduction narrative and the case for clarifying scope.
- Useful ask: identify the datasets, fleet surveys, or manufacturer information used to revise the charge-size assumption.

### 2 — Categorical Exemption Versus Narrower Fix

- Focus: why exempt the entire class instead of adjusting thresholds or tailoring requirements.
- Why it matters: this is the key policy choice, not just a clerical correction.
- Useful ask: compare the proposed exemption against at least one narrower alternative in the final rule response.

### 3 — Environmental Consequence Disclosure

- Focus: emissions and leak-rate implications of removing these units from leak-repair obligations.
- Why it matters: cost savings are discussed more concretely than environmental tradeoffs.
- Useful ask: provide a transparent estimate or explain why the emissions effect is de minimis with supporting data.

### 4 — Rail TRU Boundary

- Focus: consistency between road/intermodal TRUs and rail TRUs.
- Why it matters: unresolved comparator treatment can make the scope choice look arbitrary.
- Useful ask: explain the principled distinction, or address both categories in the same final action.

# Step 18 — Decision Brief

### Leverage Point

The strongest public-comment leverage point is the mismatch between EPA’s broad categorical exemption and its still-preliminary support for the factual and analytical assumptions behind that exemption.

### Context

EPA is not defending the existing scope aggressively. It is openly signaling that the earlier record likely missed a substantial population of TRUs above the `15-pound` threshold and that the agency now wants a narrow corrective rule.

### Procedural adequacy

Observed Fact: the notice satisfies basic NPRM process norms and discloses uncertainty.

Inference: the record would be materially stronger if EPA substantiates the revised equipment population, quantifies environmental tradeoffs more clearly, and explains why full exemption is the best fix rather than merely one plausible fix.

### Real-world consequence

If finalized as proposed, fleets operating road and intermodal container TRUs would avoid leak-repair compliance costs, but the public record may remain underdeveloped on the environmental significance of that deregulatory choice.

### Worth a comment?

**Yes.**

This is a strong candidate for a focused, evidence-seeking comment rather than a generalized objection. The most productive comments would press EPA for better data support, clearer alternative analysis, and a more explicit explanation of the road/intermodal versus rail boundary.

# Bottom Line

This NPRM is a narrow deregulatory correction built on a plausible premise: EPA appears to have underestimated how many road and intermodal TRUs exceed the `15-pound` threshold. The best-supported public comment themes are not broad attacks on EPA’s authority, but targeted requests for the agency to verify the revised fleet assumptions, explain why a categorical exemption is the right remedy, and disclose the environmental and comparator consequences more clearly before finalizing the rule.
