**Why that matters**

The strongest comments are unlikely to challenge EPA's general redesignation authority. The more useful leverage is to ask EPA to explain the exceptional-events dependency, the durability of the maintenance showing through 2038, the basis for the 2032 and 2038 budgets, and why postponing Serious-area obligations is appropriate when the attainment showing sits exactly at the standard and depends on separate wildfire exclusions.

## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Major claim: the area is now attaining the 2015 ozone standard

**Underlying assumption**

The `2023-2025` design value of `0.070 ppm` remains valid for redesignation purposes once EPA concurs with Ohio's exceptional-events demonstrations and no monitor slips above the standard before final action.

**Failure scenario**

If EPA does not concur with the wildfire-related exceptional-events demonstrations, or if updated monitoring causes any site to exceed the standard before final action, the redesignation logic collapses because EPA expressly says it will not finalize redesignation in that circumstance.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask EPA to explain the practical significance of a redesignation case that sits exactly at the attainment threshold and depends on separate exceptional-events treatment for some of the most consequential data days.

### Major claim: Serious-area planning obligations need not be due before redesignation

**Underlying assumption**

EPA can lawfully extend the Serious SIP deadline to December 5, 2026 and finalize redesignation before that date, making the more burdensome Serious-area attainment-planning requirements irrelevant for redesignation purposes.

**Failure scenario**

If the redesignation or clean-data-related steps take longer than expected, or if commenters expose unresolved gaps in the maintenance or exceptional-events logic, the timing rationale for avoiding those Serious obligations becomes less stable.

**Procedural implication**

A constructive comment can ask EPA to explain why this timing approach is appropriate here rather than simply finalizing the redesignation only after the record makes clear that the Serious-area obligations truly need not be addressed.

### Major claim: current controls and PSD are enough to maintain attainment through 2038

**Underlying assumption**

Existing federal transport programs, state rules, PSD, and declining emissions trends are durable enough to keep this seven-county area below the ozone standard through 2038.

**Failure scenario**

If the maintenance showing is sensitive to meteorology, future mobile-source or point-source growth, or assumptions embedded in the 2026/2032/2038 inventories and modeling, the long-horizon maintenance claim may be more asserted than stress-tested in the public-facing record.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can request a more legible explanation of the assumptions behind the 2032 and 2038 budgets and the maintenance demonstration, especially because the area is redesignated on an attainment showing that only barely clears the standard.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

EPA proposes to redesignate the Cleveland, Ohio 2015 ozone nonattainment area to attainment, approve Ohio's maintenance plan through `2038`, approve `2032` and `2038` VOC and NOx motor-vehicle emissions budgets, extend Ohio's Serious SIP deadline to `December 5, 2026`, and approve the state's Enhanced I/M, CFVP, and EMP certifications. The area includes Cuyahoga, Geauga, Lake, Lorain, Medina, Portage, and Summit Counties. The notice says the area's `2023-2025` design value is `0.070 ppm`, which meets the standard, and the Ohio transmittal letter says that showing is contingent on EPA concurrence with Ohio's exceptional-events demonstrations for wildfire-related ozone days. The record also says EPA will not finalize redesignation if the clean-data determination is not finalized or if any monitor violates the NAAQS before final approval.

### Inference

This is a redesignation-and-maintenance NPRM with an unusually visible timing and data-contingency structure. EPA is not only saying the area now attains; it is also using that current status to justify redesignation, to avoid additional Serious-area planning obligations before they come due, and to lock in long-range budgets and maintenance assumptions at the same time.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**
Move the Cleveland area from Serious nonattainment to attainment for the 2015 ozone NAAQS while preserving conformity budgets and avoiding unnecessary additional attainment-planning obligations.

**Problem definition**
The area failed to attain by its Marginal and Moderate deadlines, became Serious by operation of law, and now must show current attainment plus a valid maintenance plan to qualify for redesignation.

**Data used**
2023-2025 certified ozone monitoring data, exceptional-events treatment for wildfire-related high-ozone days, historic emissions inventories, projected 2026/2032/2038 inventories, MOVES5-based mobile-source analysis, existing state and federal control programs, and Ohio's SIP history for RFP, RACT, NSR, I/M, CFVP, and EMP requirements.

**Analytical method**
EPA and Ohio apply the redesignation criteria under CAA section 107(d)(3)(E), rely on a clean-data determination plus continued monitoring, project future emissions and budgets through 2038, treat Serious planning obligations as not yet due if the deadline extension is finalized, and use the maintenance-plan structure to satisfy continued-attainment requirements.

**Alternatives considered**
The visible record largely presents approval of the submitted package rather than a comparison against narrower alternatives, such as redesignation without the deadline extension, redesignation with more conditional maintenance safeguards, or a more explicit explanation of what happens if exceptional-events treatment fails.

**Agency conclusion**
Ohio has met the redesignation criteria, the area is attaining the standard, the maintenance plan and budgets are approvable, the Serious SIP deadline should be extended, and the three related certifications should be approved.

### Missing links / unsupported premises / logical jumps

The main jumps are:

1. from attainment at exactly `0.070 ppm` to confidence that the maintenance showing is robust through `2038`,
2. from separate exceptional-events treatment to a stable redesignation record,
3. from extending the Serious SIP deadline to the conclusion that no additional Serious-area planning analysis matters here,
4. from broad references to existing state and federal controls to confidence that the `2032` and `2038` budgets remain adequately explained for public scrutiny.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

### 1. Ask EPA to explain the exceptional-events dependency more directly

A constructive comment can accept that wildfire smoke can distort ozone data while still asking EPA to explain, in plain terms, how much the redesignation case depends on excluding those specific days and what the practical consequences are if EPA's concurrence is only partial.

### 2. Ask EPA to justify redesignation on an exact-threshold attainment showing

The public-facing record should explain why an area sitting exactly at `0.070 ppm` is ready for redesignation and a long maintenance horizon rather than only for continued cautious oversight.

### 3. Ask EPA to make the long-range maintenance case legible

Commenters can request a short table or narrative summary explaining the assumptions behind the `2032` and `2038` budgets, the role of MOVES5, and the expected contribution of existing controls and PSD.

### 4. Ask EPA to justify the Serious-deadline sequencing

The strongest timing-related comment is not that EPA lacks authority to adjust deadlines, but that the agency should explain why it is appropriate to rely on that timing move in a redesignation case with narrow attainment margins and a contingent data record.

### 5. Ask EPA to explain the fallback path if data worsen before final action

Because EPA expressly says it will not finalize redesignation if the clean-data determination fails or a monitor violates before final approval, commenters can ask what happens to the related maintenance-plan, budget, and certification approvals if that occurs.

## Step 18 — Decision Brief

### Strongest leverage point

"Monitoring of existing air quality indicates that the 2015 National Ambient Air Quality Standard (NAAQS) for ozone has been attained throughout this area based on certified 2023-2025 air quality data, contingent upon U.S. EPA concurrence with Ohio's Exceptional Events Demonstrations."

### Routine description situated in context

EPA plainly has authority to redesignate ozone areas that attain the standard and satisfy the Clean Air Act's redesignation criteria. For this NPRM, the best public-interest questions are narrower: whether the record explains the exceptional-events dependency clearly enough, whether maintenance through `2038` is justified strongly enough for a threshold-level attainment case, and whether the Serious-deadline sequencing is being used carefully enough.

### Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy

The proposal is substantial enough to support meaningful comment and probably strong enough to proceed if EPA fills in the explanatory gaps. Its main weakness is not lack of authority but limited public-facing explanation of sensitivity, timing, and fallback conditions.

### Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage

If commenters press these points well, EPA may strengthen the final record with a clearer explanation of the wildfire-day exclusions, the `2032` and `2038` budget assumptions, the maintenance logic for a threshold-level attainment case, and the consequences if monitoring or exceptional-events review changes the outcome before final action.
