## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- The Commission could overread generalized allegations in the petition and move toward a broad online-marketplace rule without first defining what conduct is actually unfair or deceptive under FTC law.
- A platform-accountability regime could become impractical if it assumes platforms can reliably distinguish genuine rescue or adoption postings from commercial sellers operating at scale.
- The record could conflate animal-welfare concerns, fraud concerns, and competition or tax-enforcement concerns without establishing which of those concerns the FTC is actually positioned to address through trade regulation.
- If the FTC relies too heavily on this petition-stage record, a later rulemaking could lack the empirical depth, alternatives analysis, and implementation testing needed for a stronger administrative record.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

- If a rule were eventually adopted, sellers might have stronger incentives to provide verifiable information about licensing, health history, and animal origin.
- Platforms might face incentives to collect more seller identity data, retain more records, and police higher-volume repeat sellers more aggressively.
- On the other hand, actors could respond by moving transactions off-platform, using coded language, or structuring activity to avoid thresholds for monitoring or disclosure.
- A rule aimed at deceptive commercial activity could also affect legitimate small-scale rescue or rehoming activity if the line between commercial and noncommercial conduct is not defined carefully.
