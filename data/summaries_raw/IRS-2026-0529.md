## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim

Only the same taxpayer that paid the section 4081 tax may receive the section 6435 payment.

**Underlying assumption**

The best reading of section 6435, section 6402, and the refund appropriation statute requires strict identity between the prior tax payer and the later claimant.

**Failure scenario**

If the statute can reasonably support payment to the terminal remover even when that party did not directly remit the earlier section 4081 tax, the rule may be underinclusive and may deny the benefit to parties Congress intended to assist.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can press the agency to explain why other plausible readings fail, rather than only asserting that the agency lacks authority absent a statutory amendment.

### Claim

Treating the payment as an overpayment refund under section 6402 is legally coherent.

**Underlying assumption**

The overpayment concept can be extended to a case where the second removal is tax-exempt and the statute separately creates a payment equal to previously paid tax.

**Failure scenario**

If the overpayment theory is seen as strained because the earlier tax was properly imposed when paid, the legal basis for the same-taxpayer limitation may look more like a policy workaround than a compelled interpretation.

**Procedural implication**

The final rule may need a fuller explanation of why the cited case law and section 6402 framework fit this new statutory design.

### Claim

The reporting structure imposes only modest burden.

**Underlying assumption**

Taxpayers eligible for section 6435 are sophisticated fuel-market participants familiar with nearby excise-tax refund procedures and able to complete the new report quickly.

**Failure scenario**

If claims require difficult tracing of prior tax payment, coordination across related entities, or reconciliation with revoked section 4081(e) reports, the filing burden could exceed the estimated one hour per report.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask for more detail on the practical recordkeeping and tracing burden, especially where fuel passes through complex commercial chains.

### Claim

The rule does not significantly affect a substantial number of small entities.

**Underlying assumption**

Most affected taxpayers are large businesses, and the narrow subset of smaller taxpayers faces only limited reporting burdens.

**Failure scenario**

If a meaningful set of smaller terminal operators or fuel distributors are affected, but must overhaul internal systems to preserve claimant identity and proof of tax payment, the certification may understate implementation burdens.

**Procedural implication**

The SBA and commenters can test whether the rule's small-entity reasoning rests on too little market-specific data.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

The proposal would add `§ 48.6435-1` to the Manufacturers and Retailers Excise Tax Regulations. It would allow a section 6435 payment only where the claimant is the same taxpayer that previously paid section 4081 tax to the IRS on diesel fuel or kerosene and later removes that same fuel from an approved terminal after it has been dyed for exempt use.

The rule requires a taxpayer to file a section 6435 taxpayer's report, submit the claim on `Form 8849`, include `Schedule 5 (Form 8849)`, avoid mixing section 6435 claims with unrelated claims on the same form, and file within the section 6511 limitations period tied to the earlier section 4081 tax.

Affected entities are a narrow slice of the fuel industry, especially taxpayers that pay section 4081 tax and later remove previously taxed dyed fuel from approved terminals.

The claimed statutory authority comes from sections `6435(a)`, `6001`, and `7805(a)`, with the refund theory tied to section `6402` and the appropriation statute, `31 U.S.C. 1324(b)`.

### Inference

This NPRM is not just a clerical implementation rule. Its most important move is a limiting legal interpretation: the agency is using refund-law concepts and appropriation constraints to narrow who may obtain the payment Congress created. That is the main place where constructive comments can test the reasoning.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**  
Implement section 6435 in a way the IRS can administer and fund lawfully.

**Problem definition**  
Congress created a payment for previously taxed dyed fuel but did not expressly deem it an overpayment refund and did not provide a specific appropriation.

**Data used**  
The notice relies on statutory text, refund-law case citations, the existing section 4081(e) refund structure, and limited information about the expected claimant population.

**Analytical method**  
The agency reads section 6435 together with section 6402 and the general refund appropriation statute, then ports reporting mechanics from section 4081(e).

**Alternatives considered**  
The materials implicitly reject broader claimant eligibility and implicitly choose a report-and-revocation structure instead of a new standalone filing architecture.

**Agency conclusion**  
The IRS may pay section 6435 only to the same taxpayer that paid the earlier section 4081 tax, and it can do so through an overpayment-refund framework.

**Missing links / logical jumps**

- The record strongly states that broader payments are unauthorized, but does not fully unpack why other readings of section 6435 are unavailable.
- The notice says the reporting model reduces burden, but it gives little operational detail about tracing prior tax payment through real fuel-distribution arrangements.
- The small-entity certification rests on limited market data and assumptions about claimant sophistication rather than a detailed burden showing.

## Step 4 — Analysis Obligations Review

**Adequate data:** **Partially satisfied**  
The record includes legal reasoning and some market observations, but very little direct data about how many eligible claimants exist, how often claimant identity will be straightforward, or how often filings will involve difficult tracing problems.

**Transparent methodology:** **Partially satisfied**  
The legal pathway is identifiable, but the notice does not fully show why the chosen same-taxpayer interpretation is the only workable one.

**Evaluation of reasonable alternatives:** **Partially satisfied**  
The notice effectively rejects broader eligibility and other administrative designs, but mostly by implication rather than by a side-by-side alternatives discussion.

**Explanation of analytical thresholds:** **Partially satisfied**  
The operative conditions are clear, but the justification for the boundary between eligible and ineligible claimants depends on a legal interpretation that could use more elaboration.

**Consideration of foreseeable impacts:** **Partially satisfied**  
The notice addresses filing burden and small entities at a high level, but it does not deeply address commercial-chain complications or the practical effects on parties that remove dyed fuel but did not directly pay the earlier tax.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

**Alternatives evaluated**  
The record effectively evaluates one main path: treat the payment as a refund of overpayment for the same taxpayer that paid the earlier section 4081 tax.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed well**

- a broader interpretation allowing payment to the remover upon proof that section 4081 tax was previously paid on the fuel, even if by another party
- a more explicit safe-harbor tracing regime for related-party or chain-of-title situations
- more detailed examples showing when revocation of a prior section 4081(e) report is required and how conflicts are resolved
- an expanded explanation of whether other administrative payment mechanisms were considered but rejected because of appropriation limits

**Evidence suggesting feasibility**

The agency already borrowed heavily from the existing section 4081(e) framework. That suggests additional examples, tracing rules, and safer administrative alternatives are feasible even if the same-taxpayer rule remains.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Assessment:** coherent but narrow

The rule is internally coherent in the sense that the definition of `section 6435 refund`, the conditions for allowance, the reporting requirements, the claim form, and the filing deadline all align around the same-taxpayer interpretation.

The weaker point is external coherence with commercial reality. Fuel transactions can involve multiple parties, and the record does not show much about how often the taxpayer that paid the earlier tax is also the party later removing the dyed fuel from the terminal.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact**

The agency cites sections `6435`, `6402`, `6001`, and `7805(a)`, plus refund case law and the general refund appropriation statute, as the legal foundation for the rule.

**Assessment**

The proposal is legally grounded enough to be serious, but its most consequential limitation is not self-evident from section 6435 alone. The agency's theory depends on reading the new payment provision through older refund-law concepts and appropriation constraints.

**Unknown**

The available record does not fully show whether Treasury considered and rejected other legally plausible claimant structures in internal analysis that is not included here.

## Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

**Classification:** **Complex but normal**

The agency paired a temporary rule with a concurrent NPRM, requested comments, and explained its good-cause rationale for immediate temporary effectiveness. That is procedurally recognizable for tax guidance, even though the compressed process leaves less room in the NPRM itself for a fully developed alternatives analysis.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- taxpayers that remove the dyed fuel may be unable to claim because a different entity paid the earlier section 4081 tax
- related entities may struggle to prove whether the same taxpayer requirement is satisfied
- taxpayers may accidentally duplicate or conflict with section 4081(e) reporting if recordkeeping is incomplete
- claim processing may be slowed if the IRS must evaluate complex tracing disputes not reflected in the one-hour burden estimate
- smaller operators may forgo claims because the legal and documentation standard appears too uncertain

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

Potential incentives created by the rule:

- restructuring fuel transactions so the tax-paying entity is also the terminal remover
- consolidating functions within one entity to preserve refund eligibility
- avoiding transactions that could blur claimant identity
- over-documenting tax payment and chain-of-custody records to guard against refund denial

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Assessment:** moderate

The record is stronger on legal explanation than on empirical support. The agency has a clear theory, and the temporary-rule preamble lays it out at length. The weaker part is support for the practical consequences of that theory, including claimant population estimates, burden estimates, and treatment of edge cases.

## Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

Material gaps in the available materials:

- limited explanation of why broader claimant eligibility is legally unavailable rather than merely disfavored
- little real-world evidence about how fuel-industry transaction structures map onto the same-taxpayer rule
- no visible sensitivity analysis behind the estimate that fewer than `60` taxpayers will file and that each report takes no more than one hour
- limited examples addressing tracing, related-party arrangements, and interactions with section 4081(e) claims

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

The most plausible APA-style vulnerabilities are:

- **inadequate explanation** if the final rule does not better explain why section 6435 cannot support a broader claimant class
- **failure to address reasonable alternatives** if commenters propose workable tracing or safe-harbor approaches and the final rule does not meaningfully respond
- **unsupported burden assumptions** if the one-hour estimate and small-entity certification remain thinly supported
- **arbitrary practical line-drawing** if commercial-chain realities show the same-taxpayer rule excludes common arrangements without enough explanation

2. The lack of concrete discussion about real-world commercial arrangements and tracing burdens.
3. The thin support for the small-entity and paperwork-burden conclusions.
4. The need for clearer examples covering related parties, revoked reports, and interactions with section 4081(e).

## Step 15 — Procedural Concern Score

**Score: 5/10**

This is not a flimsy rulemaking. The core legal theory is stated clearly, and the temporary rule provides meaningful context. The comment opportunity lies mostly in testing whether the agency has explained and operationalized that theory well enough.

## Step 16 — Suggested Evidence to Submit

Commenters could submit:

- examples of common fuel-distribution arrangements where the remover and the earlier tax payer are different parties
- data on the administrative time needed to trace prior tax payment through terminal and distributor records
- practical descriptions of how section 4081(e) reporting and section 6435 reporting may overlap
- legal analysis on whether section 6435 can support a broader claimant model or clearer safe harbors without violating appropriation limits

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

### Angle 1

Ask Treasury and the IRS to explain more fully why section 6435, read with section 6402 and `31 U.S.C. 1324(b)`, forecloses any claimant other than the same taxpayer that paid the earlier section 4081 tax.

### Angle 2

Request examples or safe harbors for situations involving related entities, changed title holders, or complex terminal-removal chains so taxpayers can tell ex ante whether they qualify.

### Angle 3

Press for more support behind the estimate that fewer than `60` taxpayers will file and that each report takes no more than one hour, especially if tracing prior tax payment requires significant internal reconciliation.

### Angle 4

Ask the agency to explain how the revocation of prior section 4081(e) first-taxpayer reports will work in practice and how it will prevent duplicate claims or accidental forfeiture.

## Step 18 — Decision Brief

### Angle 1: Same-taxpayer limitation

**Key sentence**
“Absent a statutory change, the Treasury Department and the IRS lack the authority to pay section 6435 claims to anyone other than the person that paid the section 4081 tax to the IRS with respect to the eligible dyed fuel to which the claim relates.”

**Routine description**
This is the most important sentence in the rulemaking. It turns a payment statute into a narrow refund regime and determines who can participate.

**Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy**
The agency offers a coherent legal pathway, but the record would be stronger if it more directly addressed why other claimant structures are unavailable.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**
A focused comment here could force Treasury to clarify its legal theory or add examples showing how the limitation applies in ordinary fuel-market transactions.

**Worth a comment?** **High**

### Angle 2: Burden and small-entity assumptions

**Key sentence**
“It is estimated that fewer than 60 taxpayers will prepare one or more of such reports annually and that each report will take no more than one hour to complete.”

**Routine description**
The notice uses a very low filing-count and time estimate to support both the PRA and RFA posture.

**Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy**
This may be directionally plausible, but the available record does not show much support for the estimate or for the assumption that claimants can easily prove eligibility.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**
Comments with operational data could materially improve the final record on burden and implementation.

**Worth a comment?** **Moderate to high**

### Angle 3: Reporting and revocation mechanics

**Key sentence**
“The model report also differs by identifying and revoking any prior first taxpayer report filed pursuant to § 48.4081-7(c) by the taxpayer with respect to the fuel that is the subject of the section 6435 taxpayer's report.”

**Routine description**
The reporting structure is meant to prevent duplication and reduce burden, but the notice does not supply many examples of how it works when records are messy or fuel histories are complex.

**Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy**
The idea is sensible, but the implementation discussion is thin.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**
Commenters could secure examples, safer revocation procedures, or better instructions that reduce downstream claim disputes.

**Worth a comment?** **Moderate**

### Angle 4: Commercial-chain fit

**Key sentence**
“These proposed regulations would affect a narrow subset of businesses within the fuel industry: taxpayers removing dyed fuel from an approved terminal with respect to which they previously paid tax.”

**Routine description**
That sentence assumes the same party often both paid the tax and later removes the dyed fuel. The record does not show much evidence on how often that is true in practice.

**Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy**
The rule may fit some transactions well, but its fit across the broader market is not demonstrated in detail.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**
Industry comments could help the final rule either justify the boundary more clearly or add examples that reduce uncertainty.

**Worth a comment?** **Moderate**
