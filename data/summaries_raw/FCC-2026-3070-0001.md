## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

- **Claim:** Supply-chain disclosures will close white-labeling and component-origin gaps.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** Applicants can accurately identify every relevant producer, location, and value share and update it within 30 days.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** Complex multi-tier suppliers may lack reliable data, producing incomplete disclosures or delaying certification without proving improved security.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Ask the FCC to define critical components, verification standards, safe harbors, and measurable security benefits.
- **Claim:** Sector-wide certification is necessary for UAS, routers, and related devices.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** Category membership is a reliable proxy for national-security risk and SDoC/exemption pathways create a material loophole.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** Low-risk or diverse products in a broad sector incur certification costs without a demonstrated risk distinction.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Request category-specific evidence, risk tiers, exemptions, and a performance-based alternative.
- **Claim:** Importation and marketing restrictions can be administered through authorization and labeling data.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** The FCC and marketplaces can verify identifiers and status in real time across changing products and rebrands.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** Stale databases, ambiguous brand/model relationships, or cross-border supply chains lead to mistaken holds or evasion.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Ask for data-quality controls, notice-and-cure procedures, and marketplace implementation timelines.
- **Claim:** Expedited revocation protects national security while preserving fairness.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** Ten days and agency review are adequate for complex technical allegations and willful-misconduct determinations.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** A mistaken or incomplete record can interrupt lawful equipment before meaningful review.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Request emergency criteria, access to evidence, extensions for good cause, and a prompt independent appeal path.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

**Rule Summary:** The FCC proposes a broad set of equipment-authorization controls addressing Covered List classification, white-labeling, HBOM/SBOM disclosures, covered components and software, sector-wide certification, importation and marketing, revocation, device updates, definitions, authorization term limits, SDoC registration, database modernization, submarine-cable rules, and a U.S.-based liable party.

**Policy Problem:** The FCC says recent expansion of the Covered List to production-location entries exposes gaps in equipment authorization and supply-chain visibility that may create national-security vulnerabilities.

**Affected Entities**
- Equipment manufacturers and importers
- Foreign producers and U.S. distributors
- Certification applicants, test laboratories, and Telecommunication Certification Bodies
- Online marketplaces and retailers
- UAS, router, semiconductor, software, and communications-equipment businesses
- Small entities subject to new certification, registration, and information-collection burdens
- Consumers and government users of covered equipment

**Key Mechanisms**
- HBOM/SBOM and producer disclosures with 30-day updates
- Certification requirement for devices in Covered List sectors
- Importation limits and marketing verification proposals
- Public registration of SDoC devices
- Expedited revocation with a 10-day written response
- U.S.-based liable party for foreign-granted certifications
- Definitions and term limits for equipment authorizations

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Strengths**
- The proposed obligations are concrete and mapped to specific FCC provisions.
- The notice recognizes production-location distinctions, software and hardware updates, rebranding, and SDoC devices.
- Some alternatives and cost estimates are expressly invited for comment.

**Limitations**
- The term critical component and the scope of required producer/location information may be difficult to apply across multi-tier supply chains.
- The record does not specify how FCC databases and marketplaces will synchronize authorization, registration, rebranding, and revocation data.
- A 30-day update deadline and 10-day revocation response may not match complex international supply chains or technical investigations.

**Overall Assessment:** Technically specified at the rule-text level but underdeveloped as an end-to-end operating system.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact:** The proposal identifies FCC equipment-authorization provisions and proposes amendments to parts 1, 2, and 15, including expedited revocation and a U.S.-based liable party.

**Inference:** The statutory fit and procedural authority may be plausible, but the available notice does not fully resolve the legal limits of sector-wide certification, domestic liability, confidential disclosures, or expedited revocation.

**Authority Discussion:** The record should explain the source and limits of authority for each distinct control, especially where the proposal affects foreign manufacturers, non-radio components, marketplaces, or devices otherwise exempt from authorization.

**Open Questions**
- What statutory authority supports each new disclosure, registration, marketplace, and liability obligation?
- How will the FCC preserve fair notice and review for revocation and status determinations?
- How are confidential trade secrets protected while public registration remains useful?

**Inference:** The notice follows ordinary notice-and-comment form, but the breadth of the proposal creates a risk that affected parties cannot assess all implementation details from the available record.

**Normalcy Assessment:** Procedurally recognizable but analytically demanding; the FCC should identify which proposals are sufficiently specified for final adoption and which require a further notice.

**Concerns**
- The proposal bundles many distinct controls with different affected parties and evidence bases.

- The record does not show how confidential supply-chain information will be handled during comment and enforcement.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- False or stale producer and production-location data could produce erroneous Covered List determinations.
- Broad sector definitions could sweep in low-risk devices and impose disproportionate certification costs.
- Marketplace or FCC database errors could block lawful marketing or importation.
- Short revocation response periods could interrupt equipment before meaningful technical review.
- Public registration and disclosure could expose sensitive supply-chain information or create exploitable inventories.
- Inconsistent treatment of software, firmware, hardware swaps, and rebrands could create evasion incentives.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Positive Incentives**
- Encourages traceability and more disciplined supplier documentation.
- May motivate U.S.-based entities to assume compliance responsibility for foreign-certified equipment.
- Could reduce incentives for undisclosed rebranding if brand/model relationships are verified.

**Adverse Incentives**
- May encourage firms to avoid covered sectors or abandon products with difficult supply-chain documentation.
- Could favor larger firms able to maintain continuous HBOM/SBOM systems and certification counsel.
- May incentivize strategic restructuring of production, branding, or component descriptions rather than genuine risk reduction.

**Design Questions**
- Will compliance incentives improve information quality or merely increase attestations?
- What safe harbors reward prompt correction of good-faith errors?
- How will the FCC measure whether controls reduce actual security risk?

## Step 13A — Structural Integrity / Nonsense Detector

**Observed Fact:** The proposal is internally coherent as a layered authorization and supply-chain control architecture, but it leaves several operational interfaces and safeguards unspecified.

**Inference:** The main structural concern is not that the mechanisms are unintelligible; it is that broad information and market-access gates could operate before data quality, correction, and proportionality controls are mature.

**Coherence Assessment:** Partially coherent; requires clearer threat-to-control mapping, implementation sequencing, and review safeguards before finalization.

**Red Flags**
- Broad sector categories combined with certification gates
- Public or marketplace reliance on changing supply-chain and authorization data
- Expedited revocation and import consequences with limited stated review detail

**Comment Leverage:** Ask the FCC to publish a control-by-control threat model, data-quality plan, burden model, and error-correction pathway.
