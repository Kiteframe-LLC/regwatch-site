## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

- **Claim:** Average market size is a defensible proxy for the scale at which a firm may become dominant in an industry.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** National industry output, geographic market count, and net-import exposure jointly capture competitive structure across highly different industries.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** An industry with national customers, platform effects, specialized supply chains, or substantial non-price competition could have a large calculated average market size without a correspondingly high dominance threshold.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** SBA should show validation examples and sensitivity tests comparing the proxy with observed concentration and firm-level competitive conditions.
- **Claim:** Grouping at the 4-digit NAICS level will improve consistency and competition.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** The two proposed grouping criteria identify industries with sufficiently similar structure and size-standard needs.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** A common standard could fit the largest or most visible industries in a group while disadvantaging a smaller 5- or 6-digit industry with different margins, labor intensity, or market geography.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Commenters can ask SBA to publish industry-by-industry justification and show the firms that move across the small-business boundary.
- **Claim:** Employment-based standards are generally more stable and appropriate than receipts-based standards where SBA has discretion.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** Employment is a better long-term measure of competitive scale and will not systematically exclude capital-intensive or highly productive small firms.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** Two firms with similar competitive capacity could receive materially different treatment where one relies on automation, subcontractors, or independent contractors rather than employees.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** SBA should provide industry-specific comparisons of receipts, employment, productivity, and affected-firm classifications before shifting measures.
- **Claim:** A 22.1 percent productivity-and-inflation adjustment is appropriate for receipts-based values.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** The adjustment period, productivity series, inflation series, and compounding method represent the relevant industries and can be applied uniformly.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** Industry productivity and price changes may diverge sharply from the aggregate adjustment, causing some thresholds to move too far or not far enough.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** The record should expose the formula, period selection, alternative indices, and industry-level sensitivity results.
- **Claim:** The proposed method will reduce growth disincentives and improve contracting opportunity.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** Firms respond to size standards mainly by limiting growth or losing program access, and the new standards will broaden eligibility without undermining program targeting.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** Higher or more generalized thresholds could expand eligibility in ways that dilute benefits for the smallest firms or create new boundary gaming.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** SBA should quantify both firms gaining and losing status and model program-participation, competition, and transition effects.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

**Regulatory Action:** The notice presents SBA's revised methodology for establishing, reviewing, and adjusting small-business size standards; it invites comments on the method rather than directly codifying the complete new table in this document.

**Affected Entities:** Businesses whose eligibility for federal contracting, lending, regulatory relief, and other small-business programs depends on SBA size standards; federal contracting officers; trade associations; agencies relying on SBA classifications; and firms that may cross a size boundary under the proposed method.

**Statutory Authority:** The notice relies principally on the Small Business Act, including 15 U.S.C. 631 and 632, the Jobs Act five-year review requirements, and related amendments concerning measurement periods, industry variation, and public notice and comment.

**Key Regulatory Change:** The proposed framework would replace the 2024 methodology's seven-factor averaging approach with a market-structure approach centered on national industry size, the number of geographic markets, and net-import adjustment; it would use 4-digit grouping where specified criteria are met, favor employment measures where permitted, and adjust receipt-based values for inflation and productivity.

**Observed Facts**
- The notice describes 338 proposed size standards: 276 at the 4-digit industry-group level and 62 at the 5-digit industry level, compared with a current table containing 995 standards.
- It reports 208 proposed employment-based standards, 129 receipts-based standards, and one asset-based standard for depository institutions.
- The notice identifies data from sources including Statistics of U.S. Businesses, Nonemployer Statistics, County Business Patterns, Gazetteer Files, County Population Totals, ACS commuting flows, county adjacency data, BEA trade/output tables, and industry-specific federal sources.
- The notice says the method has been applied to a concurrent proposed review of size standards and requests comment on reliance interests and exceptions for federal contracting.

**Inference:** The practical effect is a major redesign of the classification machinery beneath many federal small-business programs, even though this notice itself is framed as a request for feedback on the methodology.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- A 4-digit common standard can fit one constituent industry while misclassifying another.
- Firms can shift labor, subcontracting, receipts, or corporate affiliation structures to remain below a favored measure.
- A lagged or aggregate data source can cause thresholds to reflect old conditions during a fast-changing market.
- Contracting officers may apply a simplified grouping rule inconsistently at the solicitation level.
- A firm can gain or lose eligibility without a clear transition or reliance rule when the methodology and table are revised together.
- Special cases for sparse data or unusual industries can become opaque exceptions that are difficult for affected firms to anticipate.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Positive Incentives**
- A less fragmented table may reduce classification errors and search costs for firms and contracting officers.
- Employment-based thresholds may reduce incentives to suppress receipts solely to preserve eligibility in some industries.
- Regular review and transparent data sources may improve updating over time.

**Risk Incentives**
- A firm may substitute contractors, affiliates, or automation for employees if employment-based standards are materially more favorable.
- A common 4-digit standard can encourage strategic selection of the most favorable NAICS code or solicitation classification.
- Higher thresholds can dilute targeting if program participation expands without a parallel analysis of benefits to the smallest firms.

**Needed Safeguards:** Publish examples of boundary behavior, affiliation and NAICS-selection implications, and monitoring indicators for firms gaining or losing status.
