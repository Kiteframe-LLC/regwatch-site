## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim

The FCC can identify enough measurable non-core work to shift 61 FTEs into direct fee allocation while leaving most non-core work indirect.

**Underlying assumption:** The staff's internal assessment can reliably distinguish work that directly benefits fee payors from work that broadly benefits the Commission or industry as a whole.

**Failure scenario:** If the underlying work-allocation judgments are coarse, outdated, or not reproducible from the public record, sectors may be charged more or less based on opaque internal line drawing.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask the FCC to publish more of the allocation rationale or a category-by-category explanation for why particular non-core work was reassigned.

### Claim

Population-based television fees remain the best way to assess full-service broadcast television regulatory fees.

**Underlying assumption:** Population covered by contour remains a reasonably accurate proxy for the benefits broadcasters receive from FCC regulation.

**Failure scenario:** If contour-based population no longer tracks economic reality, station burden, or competitive conditions well, some stations may pay fees that are poorly matched to present-day operations.

**Procedural implication:** The FCC should explain why population remains superior to available alternatives and what evidence would justify a change.

### Claim

NRUF assigned-number data should perhaps continue to anchor CMRS fee assessments.

**Underlying assumption:** Assigned numbering data remains an adequate proxy for current CMRS market presence and regulatory burden.

**Failure scenario:** If assigned-number data diverges from actual subscriber relationships, traffic, or market activity, CMRS fee burdens may drift away from the benefits rationale section 9 requires.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters can press for a comparison of NRUF against other plausible data sources before the FCC locks in another year of the same approach.

### Claim

Small-entity hardship is adequately mitigated through fee relief tools and the de minimis threshold.

**Underlying assumption:** Existing waiver, reduction, deferral, installment, and de minimis mechanisms are enough to buffer the sectors most affected by the proposed reallocations and methodology choices.

**Failure scenario:** If the main fee increases fall on smaller broadcasters, carriers, or specialty providers that are above the de minimis threshold but still resource-constrained, the generic mitigation tools may not answer the distributional concern.

**Procedural implication:** The record would be stronger with more category-specific discussion of small-entity effects rather than only generic references to available relief.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

* The FCC says section `9` of the Communications Act requires it to collect `416,112,000` dollars in FY 2026 regulatory fees, equal to its annual salaries and expenses appropriation.
* The NPRM proposes a FY 2026 fee schedule and methodology in Tables `3` and `4`.
* The FCC proposes to shift `61` indirect FTEs into direct allocation across core bureaus and to shift `2` Media Bureau direct FTEs to indirect, for a net increase of `59` direct FTEs.
* The FCC proposes to continue using population covered by a television station's contour to calculate full-service broadcast television fees.
* The FCC seeks comment on whether to continue using NRUF assigned-number data for CMRS fee assessment and whether to reexamine the indirect classification of non-core bureau work more broadly.
* The notice includes payment-procedure reminders, no new PRA collection burden, and an IRFA that describes potentially affected small-entity sectors.

### Inference

This NPRM is not mainly about whether the FCC may collect fees. That is largely fixed by statute. The meaningful public-comment leverage lies in how the FCC chooses to distribute that required collection burden across industries and unit categories. The most commentable questions are whether the FCC has shown enough public-facing support for its FTE reallocations, whether its chosen proxies still match current market realities for television and CMRS, and whether its small-entity discussion is specific enough to test practical burden.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

**Alternatives evaluated:** The NPRM considers maintaining the existing fee framework with selective FTE reallocations, continuing the population-based television method, continuing NRUF for CMRS, and possibly reexamining more of the indirect-classification framework.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed:** The available text does not visibly provide a side-by-side comparison of other television proxies, alternative CMRS datasets, phased or capped transitions for categories facing sharp fee changes, or a more transparent publication of the work-allocation method used to convert non-core FTEs into direct assignments.

**Evidence suggesting feasibility:** The FCC itself signals that these choices are contestable by asking whether to use a different CMRS data source and whether to revisit indirect classifications. That suggests the record could support more explicit comparative analysis than the visible NPRM presently provides.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

The NPRM is technically coherent at the framework level. Section `9` ties fees to FTE burden and benefits, and the FCC's annual process of combining staff allocation decisions with projected unit counts is intelligible. The notice also explains the arithmetic relationship between direct FTEs, unit estimates, and resulting fee levels.

The weaker point is verification detail. Several important choices depend on internal assessments rather than publicly surfaced calculations. The NPRM says the staff analysis is comprehensive and that payment-unit estimates were cross-checked, but the available text does not let a public commenter independently trace how much each reallocating office contributed to specific fee-category shifts or how sensitive certain fee categories are to alternate inputs.

**Observed Fact:** The FCC identifies specific proposed reallocations from the Office of Economics and Analytics, the Office of General Counsel, and the Public Safety and Homeland Security Bureau to named core bureaus.

**Inference:** The presence of that allocation detail helps, but the record would still be more technically robust if it showed the criteria, evidence, or examples used to classify particular work streams as direct rather than indirect.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact:** The notice grounds the annual fee collection in section `9` of the Communications Act and repeatedly frames the methodology as one that must reflect FTE counts adjusted for benefits provided to fee payors.

**Observed Fact:** The NPRM does not appear to push an aggressive new statutory theory; it mainly applies an existing annual fee framework to FY 2026 data and methodology choices.

**Inference:** The legal footing appears strong at the high level because Congress requires the FCC to collect the full appropriation through annual fees. The more vulnerable issues are methodological and explanatory rather than basic statutory authority.

**Unknown:** The available export does not include any deeper legal memo addressing how much discretion section `9` gives the FCC in choosing among competing unit proxies within a fee category.

## Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

**Classification:** **Routine but analytically important**

**Observed Fact:** The FCC issued a standard NPRM with comment and reply deadlines, ex parte procedures, payment reminders, PRA discussion, and an IRFA.

**Observed Fact:** The rulemaking fits the Commission's recurring annual regulatory-fee process rather than a novel emergency action.

**Inference:** The procedure looks ordinary. The comment opportunity is therefore not about procedural irregularity so much as whether the FCC has made the annual burden-allocation choices transparent enough for affected payors to test.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

* The public record may not let commenters test whether the `61` proposed indirect-to-direct reallocations accurately reflect actual work rather than rough internal judgment.
* Population-based television fees may over- or under-state benefits for some stations if contour-based population is a weak proxy for present-day competitive or financial realities.
* NRUF assigned-number data may not track actual CMRS burden as well as other available market indicators.
* Smaller regulated entities may absorb category-specific fee increases without much targeted discussion if the FCC relies too heavily on generic fee-relief language.
* Payment-unit rounding and estimate adjustments may embed assumptions that are reasonable in aggregate but distort some fee categories at the margin.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

The proposal may create incentives for:

* stakeholders to challenge the direct-versus-indirect classification of staff time when shifting burden away from their own sector
* affected industries to advocate for the unit proxy that best matches their present economics rather than the proxy that most cleanly maps to regulatory benefits
* smaller payors to seek more waivers, installment arrangements, or category-specific adjustments if reallocations raise burdens unevenly
* commenters to push the FCC to reveal more internal methodology so future annual fee proceedings become easier to audit

These incentives are expected in an annual fee proceeding, but they reinforce why methodological transparency matters.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

The evidentiary strength is moderate from the NPRM alone. The FCC has a familiar statutory framework, a recurring annual methodology, and concrete proposed reallocations. That is enough to make the proposal administratively serious rather than speculative.

But the record is stronger on structure than on public verifiability. The notice does not surface much of the underlying staff-allocation evidence, does not appear to quantify the effects of alternative television or CMRS proxies, and does not provide much category-specific burden discussion for small entities. The result is a record that can support constructive comments asking the FCC to disclose more support without overstating the likelihood of a major legal defect.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

This NPRM does not present an obvious headline statutory-authority problem. The more plausible vulnerability is ordinary APA-style underexplanation if the FCC finalizes burden-shifting allocations or proxy choices without supplying more public support for why those choices best reflect benefits to fee payors. A practical comment strategy is therefore to focus on transparency, category-specific impacts, and alternative metrics rather than claiming the FCC lacks authority to run the annual fee process at all.

## Step 16 — Evidence Requests

Commenters could request:

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

1. Ask the FCC to publish more support for the FTE reallocation logic. This is the cleanest way to test whether the net `59` direct-FTE increase is justified rather than simply asserted.

5. Encourage the FCC to treat this annual proceeding as a chance to improve methodological transparency for later years, not just to set one year's schedule.
