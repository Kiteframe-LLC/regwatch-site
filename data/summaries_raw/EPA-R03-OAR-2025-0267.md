## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Major claim: Maryland's startup/shutdown/warm-up limits provide continuous and equivalent control

**Underlying assumption**
Mass-based limits during non-normal operating modes are actually comparable in stringency to the concentration-based limits that apply during normal operation.

**Failure scenario**
If the mass-based limits are materially looser in practice, a facility could emit more NOx during extended warm-up or repeated startup/shutdown periods than the record suggests while still appearing compliant.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask EPA to spell out the equivalency calculations in a more digestible form and explain how often those non-normal modes occur in practice.

### Major claim: cross-state comparisons support Maryland's chosen RACT rates

**Underlying assumption**
Comparing Maryland's 24-hour block averages to other Ozone Transport Region state limits is a fair comparison despite different averaging periods, combustor types, and facility configurations.

**Failure scenario**
If the comparison table mixes unlike standards, the interstate benchmark is more rhetorical than analytical.

**Procedural implication**
A useful comment would ask EPA to explain why the cited out-of-state limits are technically comparable to Maryland's two specific facilities.

### Major claim: enforceability is adequate because of CEMS, reporting, and logging

**Underlying assumption**
Quarterly reports, stack-flow data, and contemporaneous logs are enough to verify whether facilities stayed within the mass-based limits during startup, shutdown, and warm-up.

**Failure scenario**
If the boundary between warm-up and startup is hard to verify, or if facilities can aggregate data in a way that hides short spikes, the enforceability showing weakens.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can request clearer discussion of how mode transitions are identified and how exceedances would be flagged for public review.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

EPA proposes to approve portions of Maryland SIP revision `#18-04` and all relevant portions of SIP revision `#20-10` for municipal waste combustor NOx RACT. The action covers statewide definitions and applicability provisions needed to support the updated MWC rule, plus the large-MWC emission limits in COMAR 26.11.08.10. The rule applies directly to Maryland's two large municipal waste combustor facilities, Montgomery County Resource Recovery Facility and Wheelabrator Baltimore. The record says those facilities must meet 24-hour block average limits of 140 ppmv and 150 ppmv respectively, 30-day rolling averages of 105 ppmv and 145 ppmv respectively, and mass-based limits during startup, shutdown, and warm-up periods, together with CEMS monitoring and quarterly reporting.

### Inference

This is a fairly narrow approval action, but not an empty one. The practical question is whether EPA has adequately shown that Maryland closed a real SIP defect left by the older startup/shutdown treatment without creating a softer or harder-to-enforce back door for non-normal operations.

### Evidence Quotes

“The EPA is only proposing approval on the statewide RACT control regulations and definitions that relate to MWCs.”

“During all hours of large MWC operation, there was an applicable NO X emission standard in place.”

“The EPA is proposing to approve the Maryland SIP revisions as meeting the CAA's major source NO X RACT requirement for municipal waste combustors for both the 2008 ozone NAAQS and the 2015 ozone NAAQS.”

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**
Approve Maryland's MWC NOx rules as satisfying statewide RACT duties for the 2008 and 2015 ozone NAAQS.

**Problem definition**
Maryland's prior rule set did not fully supply continuously applicable limits for large MWCs during startup, shutdown, and warm-up, which mattered under EPA's SSM policy.

**Data used**
Maryland's SIP submissions, the MWC regulatory text, out-of-state comparison limits, facility-specific design-flow assumptions, and the TSD's discussion of warm-up and mass-based alternative emission limitations.

**Analytical method**
EPA identifies the specific SIP provisions it needs from `#18-04`, pairs them with the strengthened `#20-10` limits, and relies on the TSD to conclude that mode-specific mass limits can operate as a lawful alternative emission limitation during non-normal operations.

**Alternatives considered**
The visible NPRM does not discuss many alternatives beyond approving or not approving Maryland's submission. The TSD explains why concentration-based limits are not practical during warm-up/startup/shutdown, but the reviewed materials do not present a broader menu of alternative compliance designs.

**Agency conclusion**
Maryland's revised MWC rule is stringent enough, continuous enough, and enforceable enough to qualify as RACT for these sources.

### Missing links / unsupported premises

The biggest missing link in the visible record is the jump from “mass-based limits were derived from facility design assumptions and existing approvals” to “therefore they provide equivalent stringency.” The TSD says this, but the NPRM itself does not walk readers through the numbers. Another softer gap is the interstate comparison table, which is useful context but not a full explanation of why Maryland's specific mix of 24-hour, 30-day, startup/shutdown, and warm-up limits is the best available RACT package.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Fact**
The technical reasoning is coherent at a high level. The TSD explains that concentration-based limits corrected to seven percent oxygen can become artificially high during warm-up, startup, and shutdown because extra ambient air changes the stack-gas conditions. It also explains that the alternative mass limits were tied to design flue-gas flow and existing facility approvals.

**Inference**
The weakest technical point is not contradiction. It is visibility. The record says the limits are equivalent and facility-specific, but the reviewed materials do not make it easy for a non-specialist commenter to see the calculations or test their sensitivity to different warm-up lengths and flow conditions.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

The most realistic failure mode is that warm-up, startup, and shutdown occur often enough, or last long enough, that the alternative limits become more operationally significant than the brief NPRM suggests.

Another failure mode is classification error around the transition from warm-up to startup, especially because warm-up can last from three to sixteen hours while startup/shutdown are capped at three hours per occurrence.

A third failure mode is public visibility: quarterly reports may technically exist while still being too aggregated for outside commenters to evaluate whether the facilities are repeatedly nearing or exceeding the alternative limits.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

The rule does not obviously create classic threshold gaming in the way a broad exemption might. But it does create incentives to characterize operating time carefully by mode because different limits apply in warm-up, startup/shutdown, and normal operation.

There is also some incentive to rely on the complexity of facility-specific mass calculations, which can make external oversight harder even when compliance systems are formally in place.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

The strongest evidentiary point is that EPA is not relying only on a bare conclusion. It has a TSD, a state submittal history, specific numerical limits, and some interstate comparison data.

The weaker point is that the most consequential conclusion, equivalent stringency during non-normal operations, is only partly demonstrated in the reviewed materials. The TSD supplies the logic, but the record excerpt available here does not show a full sensitivity analysis, a quantified emissions comparison across scenarios, or a plainly accessible explanation of why these exact limits were chosen instead of nearby alternatives.
