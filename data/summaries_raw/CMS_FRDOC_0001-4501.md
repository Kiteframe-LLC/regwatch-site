## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

CMS proposes a broad anti-fraud package across Medicare, Medicaid, CHIP, and the Marketplace. It would expand reporting, audits, enrollment screening, payment-suspension tools, and DMEPOS marketing protections. Eligible providers receiving MA payments would need approved Original Medicare enrollment beginning in 2030. Authority comes from Social Security Act and ACA provisions. Medicaid eligibility audits would support disallowances above the statutory threshold. A separate payment-deduction RFI seeks input without adopting a new restriction. **Observed Fact:** CMS projects $379 million in quantified costs during 2027–2036, excluding over $90 billion in transfers. **Inference:** Outcomes depend on audit methods and safeguards for legitimate care.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

**Payment integrity with accountable enforcement.** CMS evaluates no action, mandatory acceptance of Federal credible-fraud referrals, mandatory maintenance of suspensions, and monthly versus quarterly reporting (IX.G.1.d). It rejects mandatory reliance partly because the statute reserves a State determination. A useful additional option is a minimum referral-evidence package plus expedited review or partial-payment criteria after repeated quarterly continuations. Existing good-cause and quarterly-review machinery makes this a plausible refinement, not a demand for an unrelated new program. Its burden and anti-fraud tradeoffs are Unknown.

**Individualized owner screening.** V.F supports identity documents only where other checks fail or identity theft is suspected, and expressly avoids a blanket foreign-ownership ban. Compare that proposal with a written individualized-risk test that prohibits exclusion based solely on nationality, country, lawful association or lawful viewpoint and allows verifiable alternative identity documentation. These refinements are within the screening mechanism; the proposal's country-associated example makes them material.

**Continuity while improving MA enrollment and EVV checks.** CMS expressly solicits MA exceptions and liability protections (V.H.3), while IX.G.1.h rejects automatic EVV claims rejection because of unresolved operational issues. Evaluate time-limited enrollment grace periods for otherwise payable emergency/out-of-network care and EVV correction queues with audit trails. These are **solicited_by_agency**, not concealed alternatives. Preventing loopholes and duplicate claims remains a legitimate design constraint.

**Small-entity protection.** IX.J identifies delayed Marketplace termination and DMEPOS alternatives. Add a participant-only, size-stratified burden analysis and targeted transition assistance where it identifies concentrated fixed costs. The evidentiary task should precede choosing any exemption.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Fact:** The RADV projection uses approximately $37.6 million / 29,900 sampled enrollees = $1,258 per enrollee; multiplying approximately 71.7 million future sampling-frame enrollees by $1,258 gives approximately $90.2 billion. The arithmetic broadly matches the narrative. It does not establish representativeness, collectability, audit completion capacity or robustness to hardship exclusions. Contract-level and sub-cohort random sampling can coexist with risk-based contract selection if each extrapolation stays within the correctly defined universe. CMS explicitly recognizes that loss of records can defeat sample validity.

**Observed Fact:** IX.C.2.d estimates 826 contract audits concluded per year, $478.7 million in program costs over 2027–2036 and roughly $48 million annualized cost. Therefore a categorical claim of no implementation planning would be wrong. **Unknown:** A workload/staffing model and sensitivity to appeals and multiple audit payment years; 826 audits should not be assumed to mean 826 different organizations.

**Small-entity coherence concern:** IX.I.4.c alternates between 306,693 and 306,963 firms, says average revenue is divided by annualized cost while describing a cost-to-revenue test, and allocates roughly $46.08 million evenly across sector firms. IX.I.5 calls the fifth-percentile firm's annual revenue over $29 billion. That language requires reconciliation of firm versus aggregate receipts and units. Tables are not reproduced sufficiently in the extract to establish the intended correction; this review does not substitute a guessed figure.

**Other implementation boundaries:** EVV data linkage needs an exception/correction process; CMS seeks operational comments and rejects mandatory automatic rejection. Original Medicare enrollment does not convert supplemental MA benefits into FFS benefits. The 2030 transition gives lead time but does not itself resolve emergency payment and beneficiary-liability conflicts. No EPA technical annex is applicable.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact:** II.A and provision-specific discussions invoke Social Security Act rulemaking, enrollment, program-integrity and payment authorities, and ACA Exchange authorities. III.H links Medicaid/CHIP eligibility disallowances to section 1903(u)'s 3-percent threshold, while III.G distinguishes the proposed eligibility audits from PERM measurement. III.K ties the Medicaid FFS overpayment duty to section 1128J(d), excludes beneficiaries, and explains why CHIP is outside that provision.

**Uncertain interpretation:** IV.C.2.d treats RADV policies as longstanding rather than retroactive new requirements and alternatively invokes section 1871(e)(1)(A)'s public-interest exception. CMS addresses contrary arguments and prior policy notices; those explanations do not settle legal validity. Request year-specific notice, reliance and operational analyses, including how the proposal fits the litigation history recounted in IV.C. This is not a conclusion that past-year recovery is unlawful.

**Uncertain application:** V.F's broad “undue risk” authority should explain the individualized basis and nondiscriminatory limits for a country-associated risk example. VI.C's misdemeanor/felony and Federal-program/licensure triggers should distinguish relevant proven misconduct from broad collateral exclusions and preserve reconsideration. Existing authority to regulate enrollment does not by itself answer each boundary question.

The authorities and litigation descriptions above are reported from the proposal, not independently verified legal holdings. No conclusion is made that the package exceeds statutory authority, inverts the statute, or requires any specific judicial outcome.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- **Liquidity and access:** A State maintains a credible-fraud suspension across successive quarters; legitimate providers or their patients bear disruption before a mistaken premise is corrected. Quarterly documentation and good cause mitigate this, but the new continuation route merits progress and expedited-review standards.
- **Overbroad exclusion:** An otherwise qualified provider loses enrollment because its owner is associated with a country where unrelated parties were suspected of fraud. The proposal creates discretion, not proof of actual misuse. An individualized risk nexus can reduce that exposure.
- **Unpayable legitimate services:** Emergency or out-of-network MA care is furnished while an eligible provider lacks approved Original Medicare enrollment; plan payment rules and patient liability protections need alignment. Non-enrollable supplemental-benefit providers require careful scope definitions.
- **Data mismatch:** EVV offline work, corrected time records, or identifier mismatches delay payment for care actually delivered. Correction pathways should preserve evidence without making verification optional.
- **Recovery forecast error:** Old audit cohorts, changing coding models, hardship exclusions, slower audit closures or successful disputes lower recoveries. Recoveries are transfers; forgone provider or plan revenue is not automatically a social resource saving.
- **Broker enforcement mistakes:** Broad abusive-conduct or collateral-conviction standards can reach cases with disputed facts. The proposed inadvertent-error exclusion, reasonable safeguards and de novo reconsideration should be operationally usable.
- **Underestimated small-business burden:** A national per-firm average hides fixed compliance costs concentrated in actual participants. The RIA admits it cannot allocate transfers among industries.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Observed Fact:** CMS proposes to reduce the incentive to retain unsupported risk-adjustment payments, use opaque ownership arrangements, delay enrollment updates, exploit information gaps between agencies, or use third parties for prohibited DMEPOS solicitation. It also replaces an expected opportunity to cure broker noncompliance with an opportunity to refute it, while retaining consideration of remedial efforts.

**Inference:** More recovery exposure could improve documentation but also encourage defensive documentation, avoidance of difficult-to-document patients, or ownership arrangements designed around disclosure rules. More aggressive suspensions could encourage providers to avoid high-risk administrative environments. These are monitoring hypotheses, not established outcomes. Publish reversal rates, time to correction, provider exits and access changes alongside recovery amounts.

**Boundary:** A geographically broad owner-risk standard may encourage concealment of legitimate foreign relationships rather than better disclosure. Individualized findings and neutral criteria would better align truthful disclosure with fraud prevention. The rule does not impose a blanket foreign-ownership prohibition.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Strong for the existence of a problem:** The proposal supplies historical unsupported-diagnosis findings, examples of eligibility process errors, DMEPOS schemes and Marketplace complaint experience. These support a need for program-integrity action; improper payments, allegations, complaints and adjudicated fraud remain different evidence categories.

**Partially supported for magnitude:** RADV gross recovery arithmetic is reconstructable from stated inputs, but the future yield, throughput and collection timing are assumptions. OACT's unfinished final score is openly acknowledged. Treat the estimates as projections, not verified realized savings.

**Weak for the firm low-impact small-entity conclusion:** A sector-wide denominator, unresolved firm/aggregate-revenue units and unallocated transfers undermine confidence in the categorical conclusion. The issue is not the mere use of estimates; it is the relationship between the chosen calculation and actually affected firms.

**Partially supported for owner-risk scope:** Identity theft and foreign verification difficulties explain targeted verification. They do not by themselves justify treating unrelated suspected fraud in a country as evidence sufficient against an identified owner.

**Thin but usable for operational safeguards:** Existing reviews, error exclusions, good cause and reconsideration supply a real starting structure. Continuity and timing evidence from States, clinicians, plans, brokers and patients can improve it. Missing detail is not proof of an absent record or bad faith.

- **Rebuild the small-business test.** Sector-wide averaging can hide concentrated costs; the text also has inconsistent firm counts and questionable revenue units. **Question:** Will CMS publish a participant-only, size-stratified calculation before relying on its low-impact conclusion?
- **Bound owner-risk discretion.** A country-associated example could burden legitimate providers without owner-specific evidence. **Question:** Will CMS require an individualized fraud nexus, neutral criteria, notice and prompt correction, prohibiting nationality or lawful-association proxies alone?
- **Stress-test recoveries.** The $90.2 billion projection depends on historical per-enrollee errors and completion of 826 contract audits annually. **Question:** Will CMS publish low/base/high yields, capacity and collection timing, and separate transfers from resource savings?
- **Constrain prolonged suspensions.** Quarterly reviews help, but an administrative investigation could keep payments frozen after law enforcement declines. **Question:** What progress, expedited-review, partial-payment and patient-access protections should govern repeated continuation?
- **Protect legitimate care during verification.** CMS expressly asks about missing EVV records, MA exceptions and patient liability; these are open design questions. **Question:** What correction queues, grace periods and enforceable liability protections preserve care while retaining anti-fraud checks?
