## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim A

**Claim**
Stablecoin-linked AML and sanctions obligations are necessary because stablecoins present a substantial and already documented illicit-finance problem.

**Underlying assumption**
The cited SAR, blocked-property, and rejected-transaction figures are a reliable proxy for risk requiring direct PPSI regulation, not just a reflection of broader crypto enforcement that could already be partly addressed through intermediaries.

**Failure scenario**
If most actionable monitoring already occurs through exchanges, banks, and other intermediaries, then the incremental benefits of pushing more obligations directly onto PPSIs may be smaller than the preamble implies.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask the agencies to separate what harms are uniquely attributable to gaps at the issuer level from harms already captured by other regulated entities.

### Claim B

**Claim**
The primary-market/secondary-market distinction is a workable way to tailor obligations.

**Underlying assumption**
In real-world stablecoin operations, the line between direct issuer relationships and smart-contract-mediated secondary activity is administrable enough to guide compliance and enforcement.

**Failure scenario**
If custody, wallet screening, whitelisting, reissuance, embedded compliance tooling, or chain-level controls blur this line, firms may struggle to know when direct obligations attach and regulators may enforce inconsistently.

**Procedural implication**
A stronger final rule would likely need more examples, more edge-case discussion, or more explicit treatment of mixed or indirect issuer involvement.

### Claim C

**Claim**
Not requiring SARs for secondary-market activity is an appropriate burden-benefit tradeoff.

**Underlying assumption**
The compliance burden of requiring issuer-side SAR reporting for secondary-market activity is materially greater than the marginal investigative benefit.

**Failure scenario**
If PPSIs in fact control important screening, freezing, wallet-permissioning, or analytics information relevant to secondary-market misuse, excluding that category may undercut the proposal's own illicit-finance rationale.

**Procedural implication**
This is currently framed as deliberate solicitation rather than a hidden defect, but the record would be stronger if Treasury offered more concrete evidence for the burden-benefit balance.

### Claim D

**Claim**
A risk-based AML/CFT program, training, testing, U.S.-located compliance officer, and customer due diligence framework are the appropriate minimum FinCEN requirements for PPSIs.

**Underlying assumption**
Existing bank/MSB-style AML architecture can be adapted to payment stablecoin issuers without major mismatch.

**Failure scenario**
If stablecoin issuers' core risk points arise more from smart-contract controls, cross-chain tooling, wallet-level permissions, or foreign intermediary relationships than from ordinary customer-onboarding models, a mostly familiar AML architecture may omit some issuer-specific design choices.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask for more explanation of where the agencies intentionally diverge from bank/MSB models and where they deliberately borrow from them.

### Claim E

**Claim**
The OFAC five-element framework is sufficiently tailored to the size and complexity of PPSIs while still setting a necessary floor.

**Underlying assumption**
A flexible floor-plus-risk-based-design model gives smaller and larger PPSIs enough room to calibrate without creating a weak sanctions baseline.

**Failure scenario**
If the floor is operationally demanding in practice, especially given required technology, testing, and integration expectations, the agencies may be undercounting burden on smaller issuers while still calling the approach “tailored.”

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can request more concrete examples of what a minimally compliant small PPSI program would look like compared with a large issuer program.

### Claim F

**Claim**
The agencies' cost estimates are a reasonable basis for the proposal.

**Underlying assumption**
Current market-practice observations, SAR filing patterns, customer counts, and assumed labor rates are representative enough to forecast future PPSI burdens.

**Failure scenario**
If the future PPSI market differs substantially from today's likely candidates, or if the most expensive technical work centers on lawful-order capability and transaction controls rather than ordinary onboarding, the headline cost estimates may be too low or insufficiently distributed across firm types.

**Procedural implication**
The RIA is substantial, but commenters can still press for sensitivity analysis and more visible treatment of uncertainty.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

The joint proposed rule would implement GENIUS Act directives by:

- treating permitted payment stablecoin issuers as financial institutions for Bank Secrecy Act purposes;
- creating a new FinCEN regulatory framework in 31 CFR part 1033 for issuer AML/CFT programs, customer due diligence, beneficial-ownership collection, currency transaction reporting, suspicious activity reporting, recordkeeping, information sharing, enhanced due diligence, and special-measures application;
- imposing FinCEN requirements for training, independent testing, a U.S.-located responsible officer, and technical capabilities, policies, and procedures to block, freeze, and reject impermissible transactions;
- creating a new OFAC part 502 framework requiring an effective sanctions compliance program based on five elements: senior-management commitment, risk assessment, internal controls, testing/auditing, and training;
- relying heavily on a distinction between “primary market” activity involving a direct PPSI relationship and “secondary market” activity involving the PPSI only through a smart contract; and
- presenting a regulatory impact analysis, an initial regulatory flexibility analysis, and a policy-alternatives discussion that includes rejected options for small-issuer accommodations and more prescriptive technical rules.

### Inference

This is not a narrow reporting amendment. It is a foundational compliance-architecture rule for a new class of financial actor. The most important practical questions are not whether PPSIs should face any AML/sanctions duties, but how far issuer obligations should extend into secondary-market activity, how concretely the agencies should specify technical controls, and whether the agencies have justified saying the framework is both risk-based and meaningfully tailored.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**  
Reduce illicit-finance, sanctions-evasion, and national-security risks associated with payment stablecoins while implementing the GENIUS Act's issuer-specific AML and sanctions directives.

**Problem definition**  
Stablecoins are increasingly used by illicit actors; existing regulatory treatment of issuers is incomplete or uneven; and future payment stablecoin issuers need a clear, minimum federal compliance baseline.

**Data used**  
Agency-held SAR and sanctions-report figures, prior ANPRM comments, market screening of current stablecoin issuers, observations about existing MSB registration and customer-onboarding practices, and internal burden estimates in the RIA.

**Analytical method**  
Map PPSIs into familiar BSA and sanctions-compliance frameworks, then tailor those frameworks through a risk-based structure, selected exclusions, and nonprescriptive technical flexibility.

**Alternatives considered**  
Small-issuer accommodations based on a $200 million reserve-assets benchmark, additional information-collection requirements, more detailed or proactive block/freeze/reject rules, and OFAC program structures drawn more directly from other banking AML rules.

**Agency conclusion**  
The agencies conclude that a joint, risk-based but minimum-floor regime for AML/CFT and sanctions compliance is necessary and appropriate; that some important requirements should be flexible rather than highly prescriptive; and that some burdens, such as issuer-side SAR duties for secondary-market activity, should not be imposed at this stage.

**Missing links / logical jumps**

- The rule repeatedly invokes tailoring to size and complexity, but the strongest visible tailoring choice is actually the rejection of concrete accommodations for smaller issuers.
- The preamble explains why secondary-market SAR duties may be burdensome, but gives less visible quantitative support for why the investigative value would be lower than the burden.
- The proposal assumes that many current stablecoin issuers already use screening and compliance tools, but the packet does not include the underlying market screen or a clearer breakdown of which future PPSIs are likely to start from a thin compliance baseline.
- The lawful-order and block/freeze/reject capability requirements are described as flexible, but that same flexibility can reduce ex ante clarity about what technical implementation will satisfy the rule.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

### Objective: impose issuer-level AML/CFT and sanctions obligations without overextending compliance burdens

**Alternatives evaluated**

- no SAR duty for secondary-market activity;
- no mandatory collection of certain additional customer information in every instance;
- no more prescriptive block/freeze/reject regulation;
- no small-issuer accommodation benchmark based on reserve assets; and
- OFAC's choice of a five-element sanctions floor rather than a more bank-style AML-program formulation.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed in depth**

- a phased rollout or delayed compliance window tied to issuer size or existing regulatory status;
- a narrower issuer-side SAR trigger for secondary-market activity when the issuer actually exercises control over wallets, whitelists, freezes, or burns;
- safe-harbor examples showing what “technological capability” and “lawful order” compliance look like for different PPSI designs;
- a middle-ground set of mandatory minimum data fields for high-risk legal-entity or wallet-linked relationships; and
- a more explicit matrix separating obligations for direct retail issuers, institutional-only issuers, and issuers operating primarily through intermediaries.

**Evidence suggesting feasibility**

The agencies already distinguish primary from secondary markets, identify a $200 million reserve-assets benchmark for “small PPSIs,” and discuss alternatives involving more or less prescriptive information collection and technical requirements. Those moves suggest that further calibrated alternatives were feasible enough to analyze more concretely.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Fact**
The proposal's internal logic is generally coherent. FinCEN grounds AML/CFT requirements in risk-based customer diligence, reporting, recordkeeping, testing, training, and a responsible U.S.-located officer. OFAC grounds sanctions requirements in a five-element program model and repeatedly emphasizes a floor-plus-flexibility structure. The preamble also repeatedly ties direct issuer obligations to the primary market while treating secondary-market obligations more cautiously.

**Inference**
The proposal is conceptually coherent because the agencies are trying to regulate a new kind of issuer without pretending that all stablecoin activity looks like traditional banking. The main coherence risk is that the proposal relies on flexibility in exactly the areas where commenters may want more clarity: technological capability, lawful-order compliance, block/freeze/reject controls, and the operational boundary between direct and indirect issuer involvement.

**Primary coherence risk**
The agencies simultaneously say the rule is tailored and flexible while also declining to adopt more visible small-issuer accommodations. That does not make the rule incoherent, but it does create tension between the “tailored” label and the practical baseline the proposal appears to expect every PPSI to meet.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact**
The NPRM grounds the rule in the GENIUS Act's directive that PPSIs be treated as financial institutions for BSA purposes and be subject to federal laws relating to economic sanctions, money laundering prevention, customer identification, and due diligence. It also invokes FinCEN's delegated BSA authority and OFAC's IEEPA-based sanctions authorities.

**Assessment**
The proposal appears legally grounded. The agencies are not inventing a new policy objective detached from statute; they are implementing a recently enacted statutory framework that expressly directs Treasury to issue issuer-specific rules.

**Uncertain point**
The legal risk is not an obvious lack of authority. It is whether some design choices are adequately explained as implementations of a statute that says Treasury's rules should be tailored to the size and complexity of PPSIs. If commenters believe the final rule ends up too undifferentiated or too vague in key technical areas, that concern will likely sound in reasoned explanation more than pure statutory authority.

## Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

**Classification:** Normal

**Observed Fact**
The agencies issued an ANPRM in September 2025, reviewed approximately 450 comments, and now present a joint NPRM with statutory background, proposed regulatory text analysis, an RIA, an IRFA, a UMRA discussion, and multiple targeted requests for comment.

**Inference**
This looks like a procedurally normal, substantial NPRM. The main comment opportunities are therefore not about irregular process, but about improving the evidentiary basis and the fit between the agencies' tailoring rhetoric and the practical compliance framework they propose.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- Issuers may struggle to classify activity at the primary/secondary boundary when smart-contract controls, whitelist permissions, burns, freezes, or reissuance blur direct and indirect involvement.
- Smaller or newer issuers may face steeper implementation burdens than the RIA suggests if the real cost driver is not standard onboarding software but robust lawful-order and transaction-control capability.
- By excluding secondary-market SAR duties categorically, the proposal could leave some illicit-finance visibility gaps where the issuer retains important technical or wallet-control information.
- Flexible block/freeze/reject requirements may reduce adaptability risk but create uneven compliance expectations across examiners and issuers.
- The assumption that many current issuers already have meaningful compliance infrastructure may hold for large incumbent issuers but be less reliable for smaller or future entrants.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Potential incentives created by the rule**

- PPSIs will have stronger incentives to maintain direct customer relationships, customer screening, and auditable compliance functions around primary-market activity.
- Issuers may redesign wallet permissions, custody arrangements, or smart-contract governance to preserve flexibility while still showing technological capability to freeze, reject, or comply with lawful orders.
- Larger incumbent issuers with existing AML tools may gain relative advantage over smaller or newer entrants because the rule rewards existing compliance maturity.

**Potential gaming concerns**

- Firms may describe some activities as “secondary market” to reduce expectations for direct monitoring or reporting.
- Firms may rely on high-level written policies while underinvesting in real technological enforcement capacity if the final rule stays flexible and nonprescriptive.
- If the agencies do not clarify how much “technological capability” is enough, compliance may drift toward minimum defensible documentation rather than robust operational controls.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Overall assessment:** Moderate

The record is stronger than a thin notice. It contains statutory grounding, risk discussion, an RIA, and explicit alternatives analysis. But several of the most important practical judgments still rest on summarized assumptions rather than clearly visible supporting material in the supplied packet.

**Observed Fact**
The agencies provide concrete figures on stablecoin-linked SARs and sanctions reporting, discuss current stablecoin-issuer MSB registration and onboarding practices, estimate three-year net present value costs of $3.44 million at a 7 percent discount rate, and identify rejected policy alternatives.

**Inference**
That is enough to support meaningful commenting, but not enough to make every load-bearing judgment fully self-proving. The most exposed areas are secondary-market tailoring, the asserted scalability of compliance tools for smaller issuers, and the practical meaning of flexible technical-control obligations.

**Main weakness**

- the market-practices baseline is summarized more than shown;
- the small-issuer tailoring rejection relies on plausible but lightly supported claims about scalability and illicit targeting risk;
- the burden-benefit rationale for excluding secondary-market SAR duties is visible, but still fairly high level; and
- the relationship between flexible technical standards and enforceable clarity is underexplained.

## Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

### Gap 1

**Gap type**  
Thin visible support for the secondary-market SAR exclusion.

**Why this matters for commenters now**  
The agencies treat secondary-market activity as a major illicit-finance concern, yet the proposal also preliminarily excludes issuer SAR duties there. The record would be stronger if it more clearly explained when issuer-side knowledge is too limited to justify those duties and when it is not.

**Agency already solicits input on this point?**  
Yes. The NPRM expressly requests comment on the proposed approach.

### Gap 2

**Gap type**  
Limited visible support for the claim that most AML/CFT technology services are scalable enough to avoid a tailored small-issuer regime.

**Why this matters for commenters now**  
That premise is one of the key reasons FinCEN rejects a concrete accommodation alternative despite invoking tailoring throughout the proposal.

**Agency already solicits input on this point?**  
Partly. The alternatives discussion invites comment, but the packet does not show a more targeted request for empirical evidence on small-issuer scaling.

### Gap 3

**Gap type**  
Ambiguity about what counts as sufficient technological capability and lawful-order readiness.

**Why this matters for commenters now**  
This requirement could drive major implementation cost and architecture choices, especially for issuers using different chains or contract designs.

**Agency already solicits input on this point?**  
Yes, indirectly through the alternatives and technical-flexibility discussion, but more explicit examples would still help.

### Gap 4

**Gap type**  
Incomplete visible breakdown of which future PPSIs already have BSA-like compliance maturity and which do not.

**Why this matters for commenters now**  
The burden analysis appears more credible for large incumbent issuers than for marginal or future entrants. A commenter could reasonably ask the agencies to show how burden differs across those populations.

**Agency already solicits input on this point?**  
Partly. The RIA invites comment on assumptions and estimates broadly.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

This NPRM does not show an obvious major APA defect on its face. The strongest litigation-adjacent themes are instead:

- reasoned-explanation risk if the agencies finalize a broad issuer framework without better reconciling “tailored to size and complexity” with their decision to reject clearer small-issuer accommodations;
- arbitrary-line-drawing risk if the final rule relies heavily on primary/secondary distinctions or technical flexibility without giving enough administrable criteria or examples; and
- cost-benefit and data-quality risk if the agencies continue to rest important burden estimates on summarized market-practice assumptions without more visible sensitivity analysis.

Those are best understood at the proposal stage as record-strengthening comment opportunities, not as conclusive defects.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

### 1. Press for clearer boundaries around primary versus secondary market obligations

The proposal depends heavily on this distinction. A useful comment can ask Treasury to provide examples covering issuer-controlled wallets, burns, freezes, reissuance, custody, and indirect issuer involvement through smart contracts or intermediaries.

### 2. Ask Treasury to better justify the secondary-market SAR exclusion

The agencies explicitly invite comment here. A strong comment could ask for a narrower alternative instead of an all-or-nothing approach, such as limited SAR expectations when the issuer has direct technical control or actual knowledge.

### 3. Press the agencies on what “tailored” means in practice

The proposal invokes size-and-complexity tailoring, yet rejects a concrete small-issuer accommodation. A high-value comment can ask Treasury to explain why that rejection still produces a genuinely tailored framework.

### 4. Request more specificity on lawful-order and transaction-control capability

This may be one of the most operationally significant parts of the rule. A commenter can ask for safe harbors, examples, or implementation factors rather than purely abstract flexibility.

### 5. Ask for better burden distribution analysis

The rule includes real economic analysis, but it is still reasonable to ask how costs differ across issuer types and where the agencies think the heaviest implementation burdens actually sit.

- “Creating some category of PPSI for small issuers that would entail lessened AML/CFT requirements would conceivably result in the targeting of these PPSIs by illicit actors seeking to circumvent regulatory scrutiny.”

**Routine description**
FinCEN recognizes a plausible small-issuer accommodation but rejects it, relying mainly on illicit-targeting concerns and the asserted scalability of compliance technology.

**Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy**
That may ultimately be the right policy choice, but it is one of the clearest places where the record can still be strengthened. The issue is especially important because the statute and proposal both emphasize tailoring to size and complexity.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**
A thoughtful comment could improve the final rule's explanation, produce a better burden distribution analysis, or encourage a more nuanced accommodation than the one the agency rejected.

**Worth a comment?**  
High value

**Comment posture:** evidence request

---

## Bottom Line

This is a substantial and procedurally normal NPRM that makes a serious effort to translate the GENIUS Act into an issuer-level AML/CFT and sanctions framework. The best public-comment opportunities are not generic objections to regulating stablecoin issuers. They are targeted requests for Treasury to better explain three things: why the primary/secondary market line is administrable, why the secondary-market SAR exclusion is the right burden-benefit choice, and why the resulting regime is genuinely tailored to issuer size and complexity despite rejecting more visible accommodations for smaller firms.
