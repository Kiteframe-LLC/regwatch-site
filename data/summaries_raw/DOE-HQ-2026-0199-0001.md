### Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim A

**Claim**
DOE should consider opening a rulemaking to rescind the clause even though DOE already stopped enforcing it.

**Underlying assumption**
Temporary non-enforcement through deviation is materially different from formal rescission in a way that affects regulated parties and future agency behavior.

**Failure scenario**
If the deviation plus contract modifications already remove the clause's practical effects, commenters may argue that immediate rescission is lower priority or that DOE should first document actual remaining impacts.

**Procedural implication**
DOE would benefit from comments and record evidence on whether current deviation practice leaves real compliance uncertainty or merely theoretical future risk.

### Claim B

**Claim**
The current regulation could be "reactivated" at any time and therefore should be removed.

**Underlying assumption**
Leaving the clause in the CFR creates meaningful legal or operational risk even when DOE has expressly directed contracting officers not to enforce it.

**Failure scenario**
If internal agency controls, contract modifications, and public deviation guidance already make reactivation difficult or unlikely without new process, the urgency argument weakens.

**Procedural implication**
A commenter can ask DOE to explain the realistic pathway by which the clause could resume practical effect absent formal rescission.

### Claim C

**Claim**
The clause is inconsistent with current administration policy, as reflected in Executive Order 14151.

**Underlying assumption**
Current executive policy is a sufficient and durable basis to support permanent regulatory repeal rather than temporary suspension.

**Failure scenario**
If DOE relies too heavily on current policy alignment without separately explaining procurement, compliance, or legal reasons for formal rescission, the eventual record may look too administration-specific.

**Procedural implication**
DOE should build an agency-record rationale that would stand on its own even apart from a change in presidential priorities.

### Claim D

**Claim**
The clause imposed regulatory burdens with no legitimate purpose.

**Underlying assumption**
There is no continuing procurement, management, workforce, or compliance benefit from keeping a diversity-plan clause in the DEAR.

**Failure scenario**
If DOE or commenters can identify administrative, anti-discrimination, subcontracting, or reporting value not captured in the petition, the petition's categorical framing is overstated.

**Procedural implication**
Comments should test both sides: what burdens actually existed, and what concrete functions the clause did or did not serve.

### Claim E

**Claim**
The text of `48 CFR 970.5226-1` is vague and may pressure contractors toward race- or identity-conscious conduct that conflicts with civil-rights law.

**Underlying assumption**
Terms like "innovative strategies" and "promoting diversity" create meaningful ambiguity in contract administration.

**Failure scenario**
If DOE historically administered the clause as a general planning and outreach requirement without compelling unlawful preferences, the petition's legal-risk narrative may exceed the supplied evidence.

**Procedural implication**
A useful comment could ask DOE for actual examples of administration, approvals, rejections, or contractor confusion before concluding the rule was unworkable or unlawful.

### Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

DOE is not proposing a new rule in this document. It is publishing notice of a petition for rulemaking and asking whether it should initiate rulemaking to rescind the DEAR clause at `48 CFR 970.5226-1`, titled "Diversity Plan." DOE states that it already issued a February 3, 2025 class deviation removing the requirement to include the clause, directing contracting officers not to enforce it, and instructing them to remove it from solicitations and contracts as soon as practicable. The petition argues that formal rescission is still needed because the clause remains in the CFR and could theoretically be revived later. The clause text reproduced in the supporting document required management and operating contractors to submit a DEIA plan within 90 days and update it annually, including strategies for workforce diversity, educational outreach, community involvement, subcontracting, economic development, and prevention of profiling, harassment, discrimination, and retaliation.

### Inference

The real policy choice is whether DOE should convert an already-operating non-enforcement policy into formal codified repeal. For commenters, the highest-value input is likely not abstract ideological support or opposition to DEIA, but evidence on whether the current deviation leaves residual uncertainty, how the clause functioned in practice before suspension, and whether the record for formal rescission should rest on temporary policy change, procurement burden, statutory conflict, or some combination of those rationales.
