## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Major claim: allowing non-emitting construction earlier will not reduce environmental protection

**Underlying assumption**
The distinction between “non-emitting” and “pollutant-emitting” components can be drawn clearly enough in practice that earlier site buildout does not functionally constrain later permitting outcomes.

**Failure scenario**
If “concrete pads,” partially enclosed structures, wiring, ductwork, piping, or tie-ins are classified too broadly as non-emitting, permit applicants may complete substantial project-specific construction before the air-permit decision and create practical pressure to approve or soften later permit conditions.

**Procedural implication**
EPA may need to define the boundary more tightly or add clearer anti-sunk-cost guardrails if it wants the “same degree of public health and welfare protection” claim to remain persuasive.

### Major claim: the “at their own risk” concept adequately answers the historical equity concern

**Underlying assumption**
Owners will actually bear the economic risk themselves, and permitting authorities will not be influenced by completed pre-permit construction when deciding whether a permit can lawfully issue or what conditions it should contain.

**Failure scenario**
A permit applicant could argue that a partly built project should not be denied or materially redesigned because substantial site-specific capital is already in the ground, especially where excluded components were tailored to future emitting equipment.

**Procedural implication**
EPA's own Question #7 suggests this risk is real enough to warrant explicit regulatory text, which weakens any suggestion that the issue has already been fully resolved in the proposal.

### Major claim: the proposal is a better reading of the Clean Air Act

**Underlying assumption**
The statute is best read to focus permit timing on construction of pollutant-emitting activities rather than broader project-specific permanent construction.

**Failure scenario**
If courts or commenters conclude the proposal reads “construction” too narrowly or understates the role of integrated source buildout, EPA's legal reversal may look more policy-driven than text-driven.

**Procedural implication**
EPA likely has room to reconsider prior interpretations, but the final rule would be stronger if it more concretely explains why the new line is the best legal fit rather than just a plausible deregulatory preference.

### Major claim: the proposal lacks meaningful federalism or Tribal implications

**Underlying assumption**
Even though some approved plans may need revision, and Tribal NSR definitions are also being revised, those implementation consequences are modest and do not substantially shift regulatory power or burdens.

**Failure scenario**
State, local, and Tribal permitting authorities may face real line-drawing, enforcement, and SIP/TIP revision burdens that are more operationally significant than the notice suggests.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask EPA to be more concrete about transition burdens and program consistency even if the action is not a classic preemption rule.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

EPA proposes to revise NSR preconstruction permitting regulations so that “begin actual construction” generally turns on physical on-site construction of “pollutant-emitting activities” rather than broader permanent on-site construction tied to an emissions unit. The proposal would add a new definition of “pollutant-emitting activities,” revise major NSR rules at 40 CFR 51.165, 51.166, 52.21, and appendix S to part 51, and also revise the Tribal minor NSR definitions at 40 CFR 49.152. The exclusion lists would allow activities such as engineering and design planning, geotechnical investigation, grading, excavation, ordering materials, paving, office buildings, certain storage buildings, some foundations and walls, utility-service wiring and piping, and some “sealed junctions or tie-ins” before permit issuance.

### Inference

This is a meaningful deregulatory reinterpretation of NSR permit timing. EPA is not proposing to remove air-permit requirements altogether. Instead, it is trying to narrow the set of site activities that count as legally significant construction before the permit is in hand, while assuring readers that emissions control requirements and later permit authority remain unchanged.

### Evidence Quotes

“Begin actual construction means, in general, initiation of physical on-site construction of pollutant-emitting activities on a stationary source.”

“Pollutant-emitting activities ... include any equipment or component in a process or operation that emits or has the potential to emit a regulated NSR pollutant.”

“This proposed action would be an Executive Order 14192 deregulatory action.”

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**
Reduce unnecessary preconstruction delay and burden in the NSR program while preserving permit-based protection of air quality.

**Problem definition**
EPA says prior regulatory text and historical interpretations treated too much permanent, non-emitting construction as prohibited pre-permit activity and thereby created uncertainty, delays, and burdens not required by the best reading of the CAA.

**Data used**
Historical EPA memoranda and letters, past requests for comment, statutory text, recent administrative-law cases, and examples of construction activities such as foundations, utility infrastructure, and refinery tie-ins.

**Analytical method**
Redefine the legal trigger around “pollutant-emitting activities,” create non-exhaustive exclusion lists, invoke statutory interpretation and reconsideration authority, and assert that emissions and public-health protection remain unchanged because permitting still must occur before pollutant-emitting construction begins.

**Alternatives considered**
EPA does not present a classic multi-option economic analysis. Instead, it poses targeted questions about placement of exclusions, additional industry-specific activities, explicit anti-sunk-cost text, required state-plan revisions, and whether the agency's legal interpretation is the best one.

**Agency conclusion**
The best reading of the CAA allows more pre-permit construction of non-emitting components at the owner's risk, without changing the permit obligations that apply before construction of the emitting source itself.

### Missing links / unsupported premises

The largest missing link is between EPA's textual reinterpretation and its assurance that practical permit leverage, emissions risk, and public-health protection remain unchanged. The preamble recognizes the sunk-cost issue and the possibility that the new definitions are too general or too specific, but it does not fully close those issues in the reviewed text.

## Step 4 — Analysis Obligations Review

**Adequate data:** Partially satisfied
The proposal is grounded in historical guidance, statutory text, and legal authorities, but the visible record is thin on empirical evidence showing real-world effects of the revised line between allowed and prohibited pre-permit construction.

**Transparent methodology:** Partially satisfied
EPA clearly states the new legal test and provides specific exclusions. The proposal is less transparent about how borderline items will be classified and how permitting authorities should avoid being influenced by completed pre-permit construction.

**Evaluation of reasonable alternatives:** Partially satisfied
EPA asks targeted questions and invites alternatives, but it does not fully analyze middle-ground options such as narrower exclusions, project-specific thresholds, or mandatory anti-sunk-cost provisions as the agency's own preferred baseline.

**Explanation of analytical thresholds:** Partially satisfied
The proposal uses phrases like “specifically and uniquely configured,” “non-emitting,” and “at their own risk,” but the available text does not fully explain how those thresholds will be administered consistently.

**Consideration of foreseeable impacts:** Partially satisfied
EPA discusses burden reduction, small-entity effects, SIP revision timing, and Tribal consultation posture. The available text is thinner on likely disputes at the boundary between non-emitting and pollutant-emitting construction, and on whether early site buildout could practically shape later permit decisions.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

### Regulatory objective: allow earlier non-emitting construction without weakening NSR permitting outcomes

**Alternatives evaluated**
EPA mainly proposes one interpretive rewrite, supplemented by eleven questions for comment.

**Plausible alternatives not fully analyzed in the reviewed text**
Adopt a narrower exclusion list limited to clearly generic site-preparation work.
Require explicit permit-record findings that pre-permit sunk costs cannot influence permit issuance or permit conditions.
Treat some categories, such as tailored foundations, partially enclosed structures, or tie-ins, as presumptively pollutant-emitting unless the applicant makes a documented showing otherwise.
Create a formal safe-harbor process or advisory determination for borderline components before construction begins.

**Evidence suggesting feasibility**
EPA's own Question #7 asks whether explicit anti-equity text should be added, and Question #4 asks whether the revisions are too general or too specific. That suggests these alternatives are operationally plausible rather than invented after the fact.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Fact**
The proposal is internally coherent at a high level. It consistently shifts the regulatory focus away from permanence and project cost and toward whether the thing being built emits or has the potential to emit a regulated NSR pollutant.

**Inference**
The technical weakness is not obvious contradiction. It is line-drawing at the margins. Terms such as “specifically and uniquely configured,” exclusion for certain foundations and walls, and allowance for some “sealed junctions or tie-ins” create room for disagreement about when a component is genuinely generic infrastructure and when it is already part of the emitting source.

**Annex A — EPA technical shortcut**
The proposal assumes that excluding certain foundations, utility-service components, and tie-ins can be done cleanly across industries. Commenters can ask EPA to supply examples or decision criteria showing how that distinction would work in refineries, power plants, cement facilities, and other integrated source configurations.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact**
EPA cites the CAA permitting provisions, including 42 U.S.C. 7475(a), 7502(c)(5), and 7503, and grounds the proposal in statutory text, prior EPA guidance history, and modern administrative-law cases including *Loper Bright*, *FCC v. Fox Television Stations*, *State Farm*, and *Port Hamilton*.

**Inference**
The proposal does not look facially ultra vires. EPA plainly has authority to administer and reconsider NSR rules. The legal vulnerability is narrower: whether the agency has shown that its new interpretation is the best reading of the statute and whether it has adequately justified departing from prior interpretations that were partly aimed at preventing permit-stage leverage from sunk preconstruction investment.

**Unknown**
The available text does not show whether the OMB review material or supporting legal memoranda add materially more analysis on the post-*Loper* statutory question.

## Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

**Classification:** Mostly normal, with a meaningful policy-reinterpretation dynamic

**Observed Fact**
EPA is using a standard NPRM structure, formal comment period, possible public hearing, statutory review sections, and an explicit request-for-comments section with eleven numbered questions.

**Inference**
This is procedurally normal in form, but substantively important because it is a deregulatory reinterpretation of a longstanding permit-timing issue. Several apparent weaknesses are deliberate solicitation points in an NPRM, especially around clarity, state-plan revisions, and additional exclusions. That does not eliminate the issues; it means commenters should treat them as opportunities to improve the record rather than as hidden defects.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

Borderline components may be classified too broadly as non-emitting and allow substantial project-specific buildout before a permit is issued.

Permitting authorities may still feel practical pressure from sunk costs even if EPA says applicants build “at their own risk.”

Different states, local agencies, Tribes, or EPA regions may apply “specifically and uniquely configured” and similar phrases inconsistently.

Facilities may rely on the non-exhaustive exclusion list to push for increasingly aggressive pre-permit construction categories.

The claim that revisions “do not affect emissions” may be attacked if earlier buildout changes permitting behavior or project sequencing in ways the notice does not meaningfully analyze.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Observed Fact**
The proposal expressly seeks to reduce delay and burden and is framed as an Executive Order 14192 deregulatory action.

**Inference**
That framing creates incentives for applicants to maximize the amount of construction they can place on the “non-emitting” side of the line before permit issuance. It also creates incentives for regulators and commenters to contest those classifications more sharply because the timing and project-commitment consequences are real even if the formal emissions unit has not yet been built.

The proposal may also shift negotiation and litigation pressure forward, with more disputes over component classification and fewer disputes over whether permanent-but-non-emitting construction counts under the old guidance.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Overall assessment:** Moderate

**Strengths**
The NPRM is clear about what it is trying to change. It supplies concrete proposed text, identifies historical guidance it is moving away from, acknowledges reliance and sunk-cost concerns, and asks targeted questions where the agency sees residual uncertainty.

**Weaknesses**
The reviewed text is less persuasive on:

the real-world evidence that the prior interpretation imposed unjustified burdens across sectors;

the empirical basis for the claim that earlier non-emitting construction will not affect emissions or public-health protection in practice;

the adequacy of the “at their own risk” concept absent firmer regulatory guardrails; and

the support for the RFA certification and broader benefit claims, especially where EPA separately asks commenters to provide data on expected economic benefits.

## Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

Only calling these gaps where support is incomplete or compressed in the reviewed materials:

1. **No-emissions / no-protection-loss gap**
EPA repeatedly asserts that the revisions should not allow greater emissions and will preserve the same public-health protection, but the visible record does not fully explain how earlier project-specific buildout avoids affecting later permit outcomes in practice.

2. **Boundary-clarity gap**
The proposal includes useful exclusions, but the reviewed text does not fully resolve how to classify tailored foundations, partially enclosed structures, utility-service features, and tie-ins across different industrial settings.

3. **Sunk-cost guardrail gap**
EPA's own Question #7 asks whether explicit regulatory text should prohibit permitting authorities from considering applicants' economic losses. That suggests the proposal does not yet fully solve the very leverage problem it says no longer justifies broader restrictions.

4. **Benefits-support gap**
EPA labels the action deregulatory and asks commenters to provide supporting information on economic benefits, but the reviewed text does not present a substantial visible economic analysis quantifying those benefits.

5. **Implementation-transition gap**
The proposal says it lacks federalism implications even while discussing SIP revisions, possible NNSR revision timing changes, and Tribal-program applicability. The reviewed text leaves the practical implementation burden more compressed than ideal.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

This NPRM does **not** look like an easy pure-authority challenge. The more plausible vulnerabilities are classic reasoned-decisionmaking issues:

incomplete explanation for why the new “pollutant-emitting activities” line is the best statutory reading rather than simply a deregulatory preference;

underdeveloped explanation for why earlier non-emitting buildout does not compromise later permit decisions or public-health protection;

insufficient clarity around borderline exclusions and the use of a non-exhaustive list;

thin support for the no-significant-small-entity-impact and broader economic-benefit posture visible in the reviewed text; and

possible inconsistency between downplaying federalism / Tribal implications and acknowledging plan-revision and implementation questions.

The most credible vulnerability is arbitrary-and-capricious risk through underexplained line-drawing and insufficiently addressed permit-leverage concerns, not a straightforward lack of statutory authority.

## Step 16 — Evidence Requests

A commenter could request:

a more concrete explanation of why the new statutory reading is better than the prior one for integrated source construction, not just more deregulatory;

worked examples showing how EPA would classify borderline foundations, walls, utility-service features, storage structures, and tie-ins in different industrial settings;

an explicit discussion of whether and how permitting authorities should disregard sunk pre-permit investment when deciding permit issuance and permit conditions;

supporting data for the claimed economic benefits, including any expected effects for small entities and local economies;

more detail on how SIP, NNSR, and Tribal implementation transitions would work if different jurisdictions retain stricter standards or revise plans on different timelines.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

### 1. Ask EPA to close the gap between textual reinterpretation and practical permit protection

Constructive angle: accept that EPA may reconsider old guidance, but ask the agency to explain concretely why allowing more project-specific buildout before permit issuance will not weaken permit review in practice.

### 2. Ask EPA to define the exclusion boundary more concretely

The highest-value issue is not abstract opposition to flexibility. It is whether “specifically and uniquely configured” and similar phrases are precise enough to produce predictable, enforceable outcomes across industries.

### 3. Ask EPA to add explicit anti-sunk-cost guardrails

EPA itself asks whether it should bar permitting authorities from considering applicants' economic losses. Commenters can support adding such text or an equivalent safeguard so that “at their own risk” is more than a policy assurance.

### 4. Ask EPA to support the economic and small-entity claims with actual data

EPA calls the action deregulatory and beneficial, but the visible notice text is lighter on quantified benefit support than on legal interpretation. Commenters can press for better evidence, especially where small-business benefits are invoked.

### 5. Ask EPA to explain the state, local, and Tribal implementation consequences more concretely

Even if the action is not classically preemptive, it still changes the practical administration of NSR programs. Comments can request a clearer transition map for SIP revisions, NNSR timing, and Tribal applicability.

## Step 18 — Decision Brief

### Leverage Point 1: the line between non-emitting and pollutant-emitting construction

**Key NPRM sentences**
“Begin actual construction means, in general, initiation of physical on-site construction of pollutant-emitting activities on a stationary source.”

“Pollutant-emitting activities do not include ... concrete pads and building foundations ... that do not have design elements ... specifically and uniquely configured to serve or support” emitting equipment.

**Routine description**
EPA is redrawing the legal trigger around what kind of component is being built. That can be sensible, but the line needs to be clear enough that tailored project buildout does not slip into the “non-emitting” bucket too easily.

**Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy**
This is all three at once: legal fit, operational clarity, and protection against arbitrary application.

**Real-world consequence**
If the line is vague, applicants and regulators will fight about classification project by project, and completed site work may shape later permit outcomes.

**Worth a comment?** High

## Bottom line

This NPRM is a substantive EPA reinterpretation of when NSR-regulated construction begins. The proposal is not thin or sloppy in a generic way, but the available record is notably more developed on legal framing than on operational safeguards. The best comments are likely to focus on:

whether the new exclusions are drawn clearly enough;

whether EPA should add explicit anti-sunk-cost guardrails;

whether the no-emissions / same-protection claim is adequately supported in practice;

and whether the economic, small-entity, and implementation consequences should be documented more concretely before finalization.
