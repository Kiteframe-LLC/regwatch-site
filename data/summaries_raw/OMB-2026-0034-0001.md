### Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Claim: Eliminating fixed amount awards and subawards will improve transparency and oversight.
Underlying assumption: Financial reporting and cost monitoring are necessary and proportionate for all covered award structures.
Failure scenario: Some programs may use fixed amount awards to reduce administrative burden for predictable, low-risk deliverables. Eliminating them could increase process cost without improving outcomes in all contexts.
Procedural implication: Commenters can ask for a risk-based alternative, limited exemption, or transition rule.

Claim: E-Verify can be expanded to assistance recipients as an internal-control safeguard.
Underlying assumption: Recipients and subrecipients can identify covered employees and contractors and implement DHS requirements without major friction.
Failure scenario: Universities, nonprofits, small governments, and pass-through entities may struggle to separate staff working under Federal awards from broader organizational staffing, especially for mixed-funded work.
Procedural implication: Commenters can ask OMB to clarify scope, exceptions, flow-down duties, and transition timing.

Claim: State pre-payment verification checks will prevent improper payments without mandating a specific system.
Underlying assumption: States have available data sources and enough interoperability to run meaningful checks before disbursement.
Failure scenario: Program-specific eligibility files, State systems, and Federal systems may not align on timing or identifiers, causing delay or false positives.
Procedural implication: Commenters can ask for safe harbors, minimum data standards, and correction procedures.

Claim: Discretionary termination and suspension language clarifies existing authority.
Underlying assumption: New text will improve notice without materially changing recipient risk.
Failure scenario: Broad "Federal Government's interest" style language may create uncertainty for recipients planning multi-year projects or hiring staff.
Procedural implication: Commenters can ask for criteria, notice, cure, closeout, and appeal procedures.

Claim: The rule will not have a significant economic impact on a substantial number of small entities.
Underlying assumption: Administrative costs are modest, no new capital system is required, and indirect effects need not be counted under the RFA.
Failure scenario: Cumulative requirements may be meaningful for small nonprofits, small local governments, and territories even if each item is modest alone.
Procedural implication: Commenters can submit cost estimates, staffing impacts, and proposed phased implementation.

### Step 6 — Technical Coherence

The proposal is technically coherent at the high level: it identifies a government-wide assistance-management problem and proposes government-wide rules. The main technical weakness is operational specificity. E-Verify coverage, payment screening, subrecipient reporting for affiliates and related organizations, domestic preference terms where practicable, and foreign-collaboration restrictions all require operational translation by agencies and recipients.

The RIA acknowledges qualitative benefits and costs. That is understandable for a cross-government grants rule, but the final rule would be stronger if OMB provided more concrete examples, implementation scenarios, and cost ranges. The record is not blank; it is broad and partly qualitative.

### Step 9 — Failure Modes

- Small pass-through entities may face higher monitoring and documentation costs if fixed amount subawards are eliminated without a risk-based alternative.
- Payment verification may delay disbursements if data systems are incomplete, stale, or not interoperable.
- E-Verify obligations may be misapplied to workers only tangentially connected to Federal awards unless scope rules are clear.
- Broad termination and suspension language may chill recipient planning for multi-year projects if agencies do not provide criteria and process protections.
- Foreign-collaboration restrictions may over-deter benign or beneficial research and humanitarian activity if "covered" entities and relationships are not clear.
- Domestic preference requirements "where practicable" may produce inconsistent agency practice unless examples and documentation standards are provided.
- Rapid implementation by October 1 may create uneven compliance across agencies and recipients.

### Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

The rule could create incentives to avoid subawards, restructure relationships, or reduce use of smaller subrecipients if monitoring burdens become too high. It could also encourage agencies or recipients to over-screen payments and personnel to avoid audit findings, potentially delaying legitimate work. If termination standards remain broad, recipients may avoid projects that are policy-sensitive or that require long planning horizons. Conversely, clearer payment and subaward controls could reduce incentives for opaque pass-through arrangements and weak internal controls.

### Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

The broad need for better assistance oversight is supported by preamble examples, prior grant-management history, and the RIA. The evidence is weaker where OMB moves from general oversight concerns to categorical or broadly applicable requirements. For example, the record explains why fixed amount awards may reduce cost visibility, but could better quantify how often they cause oversight failures compared with lower-burden alternatives. The RFA analysis identifies affected small entities and acknowledges administrative costs, but its "not significant" conclusion would be stronger with more detailed cost ranges and sensitivity analysis.
