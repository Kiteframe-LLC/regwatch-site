## Phase 1 — Docket Integrity Check

**Classification:** Full analytical spine present

**Observed Fact**

The selected document is the substantive NPRM itself, not an extension, correction, or reopening notice. The title, document type, and rule text all align on a proposal to approve Virginia's partial coal combustion residuals permit program under RCRA section 4005(d).

The available record includes the NPRM text plus a meaningful supporting spine. Cached materials include:

* `EPA-HQ-OLEM-2025-3325-0026` - EPA Technical Support Document for approval of Virginia's CCR permit program.
* `EPA-HQ-OLEM-2025-3325-0021` - EPA's August 2017 interim guidance for state CCR permit programs.
* `EPA-HQ-OLEM-2025-3325-0034` - appendix mapping which federal CCR provisions Virginia would supplant and which would remain federally applicable.
* `EPA-HQ-OLEM-2025-3325-0023` - the Virginia Solid Waste Management Regulations effective January 2025.

The TSD explains the application package, the legal and permitting framework, the comparison between Virginia and federal requirements, the list of federal provisions that would remain directly applicable, and EPA's reasoning for calling several Virginia-specific provisions "at least as protective as" the federal baseline.

**Inference**

This is a stronger record than a thin NPRM that relies only on preamble assertions. The main support is present. The harder question is whether EPA has explained the practical consequences of a partial approval clearly enough for the public, especially where existing Virginia permits would later need major modification and where some post-2020 federal requirements would continue to apply directly.

**Why that matters**

For public comments, the most useful leverage is not to claim that EPA lacks any support. The record is real. The leverage is to ask EPA to sharpen how the split federal/state regime will work in practice, how facilities and communities will know which requirements remain federal, and why the specific omitted amendments do not create transition risk.

## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Major claim: the Virginia program is at least as protective as the federal program for the approved portion

**Underlying assumption**

EPA's line-by-line comparison correctly captures where Virginia is identical to federal law, where it is more protective, and where omitted federal amendments can safely remain federal without undermining the program as a whole.

**Failure scenario**

If the omitted post-2020 federal amendments materially affect current compliance expectations for active units, then describing the approved Virginia portion as sufficiently protective may hide transition complexity that the public and operators need to understand better.

**Procedural implication**

Comments can ask EPA to show the practical compliance map more concretely, not just the legal conclusion.

### Major claim: dual federal/state applicability is manageable

**Underlying assumption**

Facilities, regulators, and the public can reliably determine which provisions have shifted to Virginia permits and which still apply directly under federal law.

**Failure scenario**

Facilities misunderstand the split, communities cannot track which authority applies to a disputed unit or requirement, or existing permits are slow to be modified after approval.

**Procedural implication**

EPA should explain the transition mechanics, timing expectations, and public-facing tools for understanding the split regime.

### Major claim: facial review of the legal and regulatory framework is enough at the approval stage

**Underlying assumption**

EPA does not need extensive facility-specific implementation evidence now because future program reviews and permit actions will address implementation quality later.

**Failure scenario**

If implementation weaknesses are already foreseeable from the transition design, deferring those questions to later reviews may leave a preventable clarity gap in the initial approval record.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask for more explanation of why the record is sufficient now despite the acknowledged need for later permit modification and future program review.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

EPA proposes to approve Virginia's partial CCR permit program under RCRA section 4005(d). Virginia incorporated federal CCR regulations through December 14, 2020 into its solid waste regulations, added state permitting procedures and certain Virginia-specific requirements, and asked EPA to approve only part of that program. EPA's TSD says the approved portion would operate in lieu of federal requirements, while omitted federal provisions would continue to apply directly. Those omitted areas include post-2020 federal amendments, legacy CCR surface impoundments, CCR management units, and several specific provisions such as parts of slope-stability vegetation, groundwater-monitoring suspension, certain groundwater protection standards, updated owner/operator definitions, and some closure-by-removal and post-closure requirements.

### Inference

This is a program-approval NPRM, not a direct technical redesign of coal ash standards. The practical stakes are governance and enforceability: who regulates which units, under which text, at what point existing Virginia permits actually gain permit-shield effect, and whether the split regime is sufficiently legible to support informed public participation and oversight.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**
Allow Virginia to run an EPA-approved partial CCR permit program in lieu of the federal self-implementing program for the covered portion of the rule.

**Problem definition**
RCRA allows states to seek approval of CCR permit programs, and Virginia has submitted a partial program built from its existing waste-management statutes, regulations, and permitting system.

**Data used**
Virginia's application package, the state narrative description, legal certification, state statutes and regulations, the EPA checklist review, the 2017 guidance, and EPA's TSD comparison of federal and Virginia requirements.

**Analytical method**
EPA compares Virginia's submission against 40 CFR part 257 and determines whether each approved element is identical to or at least as protective as the federal program, while leaving omitted or newer federal requirements directly applicable.

**Alternatives considered**
The record clearly contemplates approval in whole or in part, but gives less analysis of other design choices within the partial-approval framework, such as how much transition detail EPA should require before approval or whether additional public-facing compliance mapping is needed for omitted amendments.

**Agency conclusion**
Virginia's partial program meets the statutory approval standard, and EPA should approve it after notice, comments, and a hearing.

### Missing links / unsupported premises / logical jumps

1. The record clearly identifies what remains federal, but it is less concrete about how facilities and the public will navigate that split day to day.
2. EPA says existing permits will later be reviewed and modified, but the record is lighter on timing, prioritization, and what happens before those modifications are complete.
3. "At least as protective" is supported with substantive examples, especially closure-by-removal rules, but some equivalence conclusions still rely on high-level concurrence rather than detailed public-facing discussion of implementation consequences.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

### Regulatory objective: approve a partial state CCR permit program while preserving federal coverage for omitted provisions

**Alternatives evaluated**

The record considers partial approval versus non-approval and explains that omitted elements remain federally applicable.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed in depth**

* Require a clearer public-facing crosswalk keyed to existing Virginia permits before final approval.
* Explain whether priority permit modifications should be issued first for units most affected by omitted federal amendments.
* Describe whether EPA should condition or stage approval around permit-transition milestones.
* Provide a more explicit implementation timeline for when the public can expect Virginia-issued permits to replace federal self-implementation for covered units.

**Evidence suggesting feasibility**

EPA already prepared a state-versus-federal applicability appendix and acknowledges that existing permits will need major modification. That shows more transition detail could be provided without changing the legal theory of the NPRM.

**Stage-aware classification**

**Potential unacknowledged weakness.** Partial approval itself is normal and explicitly authorized. The weaker point is the relative lack of public-facing detail about how the split regime will function before permit modifications are complete.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

Virginia incorporated federal CCR requirements through December 14, 2020, added state permitting machinery and some stricter state requirements, omitted certain later federal amendments, and EPA explains that only the approved portion would operate in lieu of the federal program.

**Inference**

The main vulnerability is not contradiction. It is operational opacity. The legal logic of partial approval is clear, but the record gives less practical detail than a reader may need to understand compliance obligations during the transition period.

**Unknown**

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact**

EPA grounds the proposal in RCRA sections 4005(d) and 7004(b)(1), and the 2017 guidance repeatedly states that a state submission must be compared to the federal CCR requirements and must be at least as protective as those requirements.

**Inference**

The likely vulnerability is not basic statutory authority. It is whether EPA has adequately explained how its partial-approval decision preserves clarity, enforceability, and meaningful public participation where multiple regimes remain in force at once.

**Unknown**

The public-facing record does not fully show how disputes over mixed federal/state applicability would be communicated to affected communities at specific facilities.

## Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

**Classification:** Normal

**Observed Fact**

This is an ordinary NPRM with a 60-day written comment period and a hybrid public hearing. EPA also describes tribal consultation that occurred before proposal and says final action will follow consideration of comments.

**Inference**

The procedural opening is not an unusual notice defect. The opening is to use the comment period to force sharper explanation of implementation, permit transition, and residual federal applicability.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

Facilities may misread which federal provisions remain directly applicable after approval.

Existing Virginia permits may take time to be reviewed and modified, leaving a potentially confusing interim period.

Members of the public may not know whether to evaluate a facility under permit terms, self-implementing federal provisions, or both.

Future federal amendments could widen the divergence between Virginia's approved program text and the current federal baseline unless updates are pursued promptly.

Enhanced state requirements such as closure by removal may be protective in substance while still generating communication challenges when paired with continuing federal requirements for other unit categories.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Observed Fact**

Approval would let Virginia administer the approved portion of the CCR program through its own permitting system, and existing facilities would later seek permit modifications so the permit-shield framework could apply to Commonwealth-issued permits.

**Inference**

That creates an incentive for both the state and operators to move permits into the approved-program framework. It also creates an incentive to treat the remaining federal provisions as background rather than central obligations, which is why the residual-federal map needs to be especially clear.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

The main vulnerability theme is explanation adequacy under a partial-approval structure.

Potential pressure points:

* whether EPA sufficiently explains how the split federal/state regime will function before permit modifications are complete,

## Step 16 — Evidence Requests

A commenter could request:

* a facility-facing summary of which federal provisions remain directly applicable after approval and how that applies to currently permitted Virginia CCR units,
* a clearer explanation of the permit-transition process, including expected sequencing and timing for major permit modifications after approval,
* a more explicit discussion of how EPA concluded that the approved Virginia provisions remain at least as protective where later federal amendments changed definitions, closure, and groundwater-related requirements,
* clarification of how the public will be informed, at each facility, whether a disputed requirement comes from an approved state permit, residual federal self-implementation, or both,
* explanation of how future program reviews will address any lag between federal amendments and later Virginia adoption.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

### 1. Ask EPA to explain the transition from existing Virginia permits to approved-program permits

A strong comment can accept that partial approval is lawful while asking EPA to explain how and when existing permits will actually be modified so the permit shield applies.

### 2. Ask EPA to make the split regime legible to the public

The record would be stronger if EPA added a concise public-facing compliance map for active units, legacy impoundments, and CCR management units.

### 3. Ask EPA to substantiate "at least as protective" conclusions where federal amendments were omitted

Commenters can focus on the practical consequences of omitted post-2020 amendments rather than treating the entire program as unsupported.

### 4. Ask EPA to discuss community-facing consequences of regulatory overlap

Where communities track CCR closure, groundwater monitoring, and permit compliance, a mixed regime can create accountability confusion unless responsibilities are clearly explained.

## Step 18 — Decision Brief

### Strongest leverage point

"For the Commonwealth provisions that are not approved upon finalization, the corresponding federal requirements will continue to apply directly to facilities..."

### Routine description situated in context

EPA plainly has authority to approve state CCR programs in whole or in part, and Virginia has assembled a serious application record. The best comment angle is therefore not abstract opposition to state approval. It is the practical question of whether EPA has explained the compliance consequences of partial approval clearly enough for regulated entities, communities, and later enforcement.

### Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy

The proposal is legally grounded and materially supported, but the administrative record would be more durable if EPA gave the public a clearer explanation of permit transition, residual federal applicability, and how overlapping obligations will be communicated at specific facilities.

### Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage

If commenters press this point effectively, EPA may supplement the final record with a clearer transition explanation or more usable applicability guidance. That would improve public oversight and reduce the risk that approval produces avoidable confusion rather than a clean handoff of authority.
