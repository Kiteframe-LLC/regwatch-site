## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Claim: Paying zero interest plus a Closing Balance Limit will keep Payment Account balances small enough to limit direct balance-sheet effects.
Underlying assumption: Payment Account holders will respond predictably to the zero-interest design and will not find alternative ways to hold meaningfully larger balances.
Failure scenario: If operational needs, stress behavior, or account-holder incentives lead firms to hold larger precautionary balances anyway, zero interest may not do as much work as the Board expects.
Procedural implication: Commenters can request more concrete modeling or scenario analysis showing why both tools together are sufficient and how they interact.

Claim: The risk to rate control from paying zero interest would be minimal.
Underlying assumption: Payment Account balances will remain modest enough that federal funds rate control is not meaningfully affected.
Failure scenario: If adoption is broader than expected or if balances spike in stress periods, the monetary-policy effects may be larger than the notice suggests.
Procedural implication: The Board's confidence would be stronger if supported by sensitivity analysis or balance-sheet ranges rather than assertion alone.

Claim: EBA participation would let Payment Account holders circumvent the proposal.
Underlying assumption: Firms could use EBAs in a way that materially undermines the no-interest rule or the Closing Balance Limit.
Failure scenario: If EBA restrictions already limit that possibility more than the Board suggests, a categorical exclusion may be broader than needed.
Procedural implication: Commenters can ask the Board to explain more concretely how the circumvention would occur and whether narrower guardrails could address it.

Claim: The proposal imposes no meaningful compliance burden because there are no direct reporting or recordkeeping requirements.
Underlying assumption: Formal paperwork burden is the main relevant burden.
Failure scenario: Operational changes, balance-management costs, and opportunity costs may still matter even without new forms or records.
Procedural implication: The record would be stronger if it distinguished paperwork burden from real economic and operational burden.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

Observed Facts:
- The Board proposes to amend `12 CFR 204.10` so that balances in a `Payment Account` would not receive interest.
- The Board proposes to define `payment account` by agreement-based account status rather than by a longer functional test.
- The Board proposes to revise the definition of `master account` so the new `payment account` category is excluded from the interest provisions that apply to master accounts.
- The Board proposes to add `§ 204.10(d)(6)` so a holder of a Payment Account may not participate in an `EBA`.
- The rule is coordinated with a same-day Payment Account Notice that would establish a `Closing Balance Limit` in the PSR Policy.

Inference:
- This NPRM is less about reserve requirements in the ordinary sense and more about shaping the economic design of a new Federal Reserve account type.
- The Board is trying to make Payment Accounts usable for clearing and settlement but unattractive as stores of value.
- The strongest practical question is whether the Board has justified choosing a blanket no-interest rule and EBA ban instead of more targeted alternatives.

Evidence Quotes:
- "The proposed amendments would exclude Payment Accounts from Regulation D's provisions directing Federal Reserve Banks ... to pay interest on balances maintained at a Reserve Bank."
- "As a result, the Reserve Banks would not pay interest on balances maintained in Payment Accounts."
- "A holder of a payment account may not participate in an excess balance account."

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

Regulatory objective:
- Create a new Payment Account structure that supports payments innovation without materially expanding Federal Reserve balance-sheet exposure.

Problem definition:
- If Payment Accounts paid interest or allowed EBA participation, firms might use them as stores of value rather than limited-purpose settlement accounts.

Data used:
- Summary of comments from the December 2025 Payment Account RFI.
- Narrative reasoning about Federal Reserve liabilities, balance-sheet size, monetary-policy implementation, and account-holder incentives.

Analytical method:
- Qualitative policy reasoning, combined with a limited discussion of alternatives and administrative-law impacts.

Alternatives considered:
- Pay interest on Payment Account balances up to the Closing Balance Limit at the `IORB` rate or `ON RRP` rate.
- Leave some related Regulation D provisions unchanged while seeking comment on whether the Closing Balance Limit should be codified here instead of only in the PSR Policy.

Agency conclusion:
- Payment Accounts should earn zero interest and their holders should be excluded from EBAs because that combination better limits balances and keeps the account aligned with a payments-only purpose.

Missing links:
- The notice does not quantify expected balance ranges or explain how sensitive the policy is to different adoption levels.
- The notice does not show in much detail why the Closing Balance Limit alone would be insufficient.
- The notice discusses operational-complexity concerns raised by commenters but does not analyze them deeply.

Unsupported premises:
- That a blanket no-interest rule is superior to threshold-based, phased, or targeted-interest alternatives.
- That EBA exclusion is the best or only way to prevent circumvention.

Logical jumps:
- From acknowledged uncertainty about indirect balance-sheet effects to a fairly confident conclusion that the chosen design is the best balance.
