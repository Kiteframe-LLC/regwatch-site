### Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

#### Claim A

**Claim**
FICU subsidiaries should apply jointly with parent companies rather than requiring each investing FICU to submit its own license application.

**Underlying assumption**
The joint-application model captures the relevant ownership, integrity, and control risks without missing material investors or creating loopholes.

**Failure scenario**
If meaningful control can be exercised by investors that fall short of the proposed thresholds or by complex ownership groupings, the streamlined structure may under-screen some parties while still imposing substantial paperwork on others.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask the NCUA to explain why its chosen thresholds and applicant structure are the right fit for cooperative ownership models and whether narrower or tiered filing approaches were considered.

#### Claim B

**Claim**
Limiting FICUs to investments in NCUA-licensed PPSIs should not materially impede credit unions' ability to facilitate payment stablecoin services.

**Underlying assumption**
A sufficient market of NCUA-licensed issuers will emerge and the restriction will not unduly reduce access to payment stablecoin partnerships or technology options.

**Failure scenario**
If the licensing process is slow, narrow, or operationally demanding, the investment limitation could function as a bottleneck rather than a prudential safeguard.

**Procedural implication**
The record would be stronger if the agency explains expected market entry, transition timing, and whether temporary or conditional pathways were considered.

#### Claim C

**Claim**
The rule will not have a significant economic impact on a substantial number of small credit unions.

**Underlying assumption**
Existing CUSO participation rates are a reliable proxy for the subset of small credit unions likely to face material impact under this rule.

**Failure scenario**
If smaller credit unions are less likely to have existing CUSO investments precisely because barriers are already high, then using current investment patterns may understate the effect of adding a new licensing and investment screen.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask for a fuller explanation of why current CUSO participation is the correct baseline for future stablecoin-related participation.

#### Claim D

**Claim**
The PRA burden estimate of 440 annual hours adequately captures the application and licensing burden.

**Underlying assumption**
The expected number of applicants, the one-time burden estimates, and the treatment of biographical and financial reports are realistic.

**Failure scenario**
If applications involve complex ownership mapping, legal review, AML program certification, or repeated follow-up with the NCUA, the burden estimate may be understated.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can request the assumptions table behind the 10-respondent, 44-hours-per-response estimate and ask for sensitivity analysis.

### Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

#### Observed Facts

The NPRM proposes to add 12 CFR part 706 to implement parts of the GENIUS Act for credit-union-affiliated payment stablecoin issuers. The proposal would:

- establish a licensing process for permitted payment stablecoin issuers that are subsidiaries of federally insured credit unions;
- require applications by the subsidiary together with parent-company-related information and certifications;
- authorize investigations, application review, denial, hearing, reapplication, AML and sanctions certification, and change-in-control procedures;
- impose a prior-notice framework for certain acquiring FICUs; and
- bar FICUs from investing in payment stablecoin issuers unless they are NCUA-licensed PPSIs.

The NPRM expressly says a later proposal will address the standards and restrictions imposed on PPSIs themselves, meaning this rule addresses licensing architecture and investment gating first rather than the full substantive regime.

#### Inference

This is a front-end institutional-design rule. It does not yet set the complete prudential framework for stablecoin issuance, but it could strongly shape who is able to participate and how quickly the credit-union sector can enter the market.

### Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**  
Implement the GENIUS Act's requirement that the NCUA establish a process and framework for licensing, regulating, examining, and supervising payment stablecoin issuers that are FICU subsidiaries.

**Problem definition**  
The Act gives the NCUA licensing and supervisory responsibilities over this subset of issuers, but the agency needs procedural rules explaining who applies, what information is required, how ownership changes are handled, and which issuers FICUs may invest in.

**Data used**  
Primarily statutory text, existing NCUA CUSO concepts, prudential screening considerations, small-credit-union asset-size data, and a short PRA burden estimate.

**Analytical method**  
Interpretive and administrative design reasoning: identify statutory commands, translate them into application procedures, select who must file, define key ownership terms, and create a licensing-only first step ahead of a later substantive PPSI proposal.

**Alternatives considered**  
The NPRM expressly asks whether each investing FICU should apply instead of the subsidiary joint-application model and asks whether the prior notice requirement for acquiring FICUs is unduly burdensome. It does not develop a wide range of concrete alternative procedural structures in depth.

**Agency conclusion**  
The Board concludes that the joint-application model, parent-company and principal-shareholder certifications, change-in-control procedures, and investment limitation best implement the GENIUS Act while minimizing burden on the NCUA and the credit union industry.

**Missing links / logical jumps**

- The NPRM asserts that requiring each investing FICU to apply would be unmanageable, but the public-facing record does not quantify how often that problem is expected or compare other administrable alternatives.
- The proposal claims the investment limitation should not pose a barrier, but the record does not include a concrete market-entry or timing analysis showing how many NCUA-licensed PPSIs are likely to exist.
- The rule is significant under Executive Order 12866, yet the public burden discussion remains relatively thin and mostly limited to the PRA table and RFA certification.
- The first-step / later-proposal sequencing may be sensible, but it also means commenters are asked to assess licensing architecture before the complete substantive obligations for PPSIs are publicly proposed.

### Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

#### Objective: decide who must file and how much ownership information the NCUA needs

**Alternatives evaluated**

- Joint application by the FICU subsidiary and parent-company-related parties.
- Requiring each investing FICU to apply.
- Prior notice and disapproval procedures for acquisitions that create parent-company status.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed in depth**

- A tiered filing regime based on ownership percentage or control rights rather than a more unitary application model.
- A streamlined notice-plus-certification process for passive minority investors.
- Temporary or phased licensing for early entrants pending completion of the later PPSI standards rule.

**Evidence suggesting feasibility**

The NPRM itself acknowledges the possibility of a more investor-by-investor application model and solicits alternatives on the burden imposed by prior notice requirements.

#### Objective: protect safety and soundness without blocking market participation

**Alternatives evaluated**

- Restrict FICU investment to NCUA-licensed PPSIs only.
- Require AML and sanctions certifications and background information for directors, officers, and relevant owners.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed in depth**

- A transition safe harbor allowing limited interim investment relationships while applications are pending.
- Conditional approvals tied to later compliance with the forthcoming substantive PPSI standards rule.
- More explicit criteria for when licensing delays or denials would materially impair access to payment stablecoin services for members.

**Evidence suggesting feasibility**

The NPRM already includes a safe-harbor heading for pending applications and recognizes that another proposal is still forthcoming, which suggests staged or transitional mechanisms were plausible subjects for fuller public explanation.

### Step 6 — Technical Coherence

This is not a scientific or engineering rule, but it still depends on operational coherence across licensing, ownership screening, investment restrictions, and future supervision.

**Observed Fact**
The NPRM consistently tries to separate procedural gatekeeping now from substantive operating standards later. It also repeatedly frames its choices as ways to minimize burden while preserving safety and soundness.

**Inference**
That sequencing is coherent at a high level, but the rule's practical coherence depends on how well the future substantive PPSI proposal aligns with this first-step licensing architecture. A commenter could reasonably ask whether applicants can meaningfully prepare for licensure before all major operating standards are visible.

**Primary coherence risk**
The proposal may create a licensing framework whose real burden and market effect cannot be fully evaluated until the later substantive rule appears.

### Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact**
The NPRM grounds itself in the GENIUS Act's provisions governing approval of subsidiaries of insured depository institutions, broader regulatory authority under the Act, and existing authority under the Federal Credit Union Act.

**Assessment**
The NCUA appears to have clear authority to issue implementing licensing procedures for FICU-subsidiary PPSIs and to establish related supervisory processes.

**Uncertain point**
The strongest legal-risk theme is not the existence of rulemaking authority, but whether the Board has adequately explained why its chosen licensing architecture, ownership-screening scope, and investment limitation are the best interpretations of the GENIUS Act's requirements rather than simply permissible ones.

### Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

**Classification:** Complex but normal

**Observed Fact**
The NPRM follows a conventional structure, includes proposed regulatory text, includes PRA and RFA sections, and expressly solicits comment on core design questions.

**Inference**
This looks like a normal NPRM for a novel statutory program. The main record-strength issue is not procedural irregularity; it is the relative thinness of the public-facing comparative analysis behind several key design choices.

### Step 9 — Failure Modes

- The joint-application model may omit or underweight some forms of practical control in complex ownership structures.
- The investment limitation may slow market entry if few NCUA-licensed PPSIs are approved promptly.
- Small credit unions may face higher practical barriers than the RFA discussion suggests.
- The 440-hour PRA estimate may undercount real burden if applications require extensive legal, governance, or AML remediation work.
- Sequencing licensing now and substantive standards later may reduce commenters' ability to test the full regulatory package as an integrated system.

### Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Potential incentives created by the rule**

- Credit unions may prefer ownership structures that avoid triggering broader parent-company or principal-shareholder review.
- Market participants may consolidate around a smaller set of licensable entities if the approval pathway is perceived as costly or slow.
- FICUs may delay experimentation until the later substantive PPSI rule clarifies what licensed issuers must actually do.

**Potential gaming concerns**

- Parties may try to distribute influence across entities or governance arrangements to reduce apparent control.
- Applicants may frame investors as passive or below threshold even when real-world influence is more substantial than the formal structure suggests.

### Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Overall assessment:** Moderate

The NPRM presents a substantial legal and procedural explanation, but the public-facing support for several burden and implementation claims is limited.

**Observed Fact**
The notice explains the statutory framework, identifies the Board's chosen approach, estimates a 440-hour annual PRA burden, and certifies that the rule would not significantly affect a substantial number of small credit unions.

**Inference**
That is enough to show a serious effort to implement the GENIUS Act, but not enough by itself to establish that the chosen filing architecture and investment limitation are the least burdensome or most workable options.

**Main weakness**
Several important propositions are plausible rather than demonstrated:

- that joint applications are meaningfully more administrable than other screening structures;
- that restricting investment to NCUA-licensed PPSIs will not materially impede access or competition;
- that small-credit-union impacts are limited because current CUSO participation rates are low; and
- that the PRA burden estimate captures the true compliance workload for novel stablecoin licensing.

### Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

This NPRM does not show an obvious headline defect on its face, but three themes could matter if left underexplained in a final rule:

- insufficient explanation for why the joint-application and ownership-screening approach is superior to other administrable alternatives;
- inadequate support for the claim that the investment limitation will not materially impede participation or competition; and
- thin public burden analysis relative to the rule's significant-action status and novel subject matter.

At the NPRM stage, these are best framed as record-strengthening comment opportunities rather than as categorical claims that the rule is unlawful.

Ask the NCUA to explain how many licensed PPSIs it realistically expects, how quickly approvals could occur, and why the investment restriction will not reduce access, competition, or innovation.

The rule is significant under Executive Order 12866, yet the public burden analysis is concise. Commenters can ask for the assumptions underlying the PRA estimate and the RFA certification.
