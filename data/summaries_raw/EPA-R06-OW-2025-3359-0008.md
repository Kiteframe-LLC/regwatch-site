## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim A

**Claim:** Past monitoring shows expansion will not create significant adverse environmental consequences.

**Underlying assumption:** Historic site use is a reliable predictor for materially larger disposal footprints, especially the much larger CCNW expansion.

**Failure scenario:** If the existing monitoring record does not adequately test the conditions at the expanded edges or future cumulative disposal volumes, then the “no significant adverse impacts” conclusion may be overgeneralized.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask EPA to translate the monitoring history into a clearer site-expansion rationale, especially for the larger CCNW footprint.

### Claim B

**Claim:** Expansion provides the best long-term disposal capacity and operational flexibility.

**Underlying assumption:** Ocean disposal at these enlarged footprints remains the most workable option after considering upland confined placement and beneficial use alternatives.

**Failure scenario:** If beneficial use or upland options can absorb more material than the notice suggests, or if the combination approach is not quantified clearly, commenters may argue the record underexplains why this scale of ocean expansion is needed.

**Procedural implication:** EPA may need a clearer public-facing explanation of how much capacity each alternative provides and why the proposed ocean share is appropriate.

### Claim C

**Claim:** The SMMP and project-specific suitability reviews are enough to protect the marine environment.

**Underlying assumption:** Adaptive management, bathymetric monitoring, disposal controls, and later permit-by-permit suitability review will catch problems before they become significant.

**Failure scenario:** If monitoring triggers, thresholds for action, or site-specific closure or adjustment conditions are not sufficiently visible in the public explanation, the safeguards may appear too abstract.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask EPA to make the management thresholds and response steps easier to evaluate before finalizing the site expansion.

### Claim D

**Claim:** Expanding existing sites is preferable to selecting a farther-offshore site.

**Underlying assumption:** A more distant site would be less safe, less economical to monitor, and potentially less environmentally appropriate.

**Failure scenario:** If the record summarizes this earlier siting judgment too briefly, outside readers may not be able to test whether those conclusions still fit current project demands.

**Procedural implication:** EPA would benefit from a clearer explanation of how current dredging volumes and current alternatives interact with the older Zone of Siting Feasibility analysis.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

* EPA proposes to expand two existing ocean dredged material disposal sites offshore of Corpus Christi, Texas.
* The CCSC site would grow from `0.61` square nautical miles to `1.05` square nautical miles.
* The CCNW site would grow from `1.39` square nautical miles to `5.57` square nautical miles.
* EPA says the modified sites are intended to serve long-term disposal needs for suitable dredged material from the Corpus Christi Bay area, including material associated with the Port of Corpus Christi's Channel Deepening Project.
* The notice says the supporting analysis is a draft environmental assessment with a preliminary finding of no significant impact, along with an updated SMMP.
* The notice also says alternatives for dredged-material placement include ocean disposal, upland confined placement, and beneficial use, and that the USACE expects to use a combination of those options.
* EPA states that only material determined to be suitable for ocean disposal under MPRSA testing guidance may be placed at the sites.

### Inference

This is a classic EPA site-management NPRM rather than a direct permit approval for any one dredging project. The main public question is not whether any dredged material may ever go offshore, but whether EPA has explained clearly enough why these particular boundary expansions are sized appropriately, how the alternatives mix works in practice, and why the monitoring and adaptive-management plan is robust enough for a materially larger disposal footprint.

### Evidence Quotes

* “The modified sites will be subject to monitoring and management to ensure continued protection of the marine environment.”
* “Monitoring studies at CCSC and CCNW ODMDSs have confirmed that there have been no significant adverse environmental consequences...”
* “Alternatives for placement of dredged material have been developed as follows: ocean disposal; upland confined placement; and beneficial use.”

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

The proposal is technically coherent at a high level. EPA identifies the governing marine-protection criteria, gives concrete footprint changes, points to historic site use, references dredged-material fate modeling, and relies on a draft SMMP with adaptive-management provisions.

The main weakness is not contradiction. It is compression. The notice repeatedly assures readers that expanded disposal will not create significant adverse impacts, but the public-facing explanation does not do much to translate the underlying monitoring and modeling into concrete, easy-to-test reasons why these larger footprints remain safe.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact:** EPA grounds the proposal in the Marine Protection, Research, and Sanctuaries Act and in the site-selection and management criteria of `40 CFR part 228`, while also explaining that future project-specific ocean disposal still requires material-suitability review and USACE authorization under section 103.

**Observed Fact:** The notice says EPA does not routinely apply NEPA to these site designations because courts have recognized MPRSA procedures as functionally equivalent, but EPA nevertheless documents alternatives analysis as a matter of policy.

**Inference:** The legal foundation for proposing a site modification appears facially strong. The more realistic legal-risk question is administrative-record adequacy: whether EPA has explained the environmental basis for this scale of expansion clearly enough for meaningful comment and eventual finalization.

**Unknown:** Whether the underlying appendices contain more detailed site-specific analyses that would eliminate some of the visible explanatory gaps in the preamble.

## Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

**Classification:** **Complex but normal**

**Observed Fact:** EPA presents the action as a standard proposed rule with a supporting environmental assessment, a preliminary no-significant-impact determination, and a draft SMMP for public review.

**Observed Fact:** The notice repeatedly clarifies that this proposal does not itself authorize dumping from any specific project and that later permits or federal project approvals still require project-specific review.

**Inference:** This looks like a normal EPA MPRSA site-management proceeding, not a procedurally irregular shortcut. The comment opportunity is mainly about explanatory depth and environmental safeguards, not about obvious procedural abnormality.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

* EPA may be relying too heavily on summarized past monitoring without clearly showing why the larger CCNW footprint in particular remains environmentally acceptable.
* The public-facing record may underexplain how much dredged material will go to beneficial use or upland confinement versus the enlarged ocean sites.
* Adaptive management may sound protective in the abstract, but commenters may not be able to assess it well unless EPA makes thresholds for action and corrective responses more visible.
* Assertions about no unique or limited habitats, no significant adverse impacts, and minimal navigation interference may draw criticism if the underlying support remains mostly in appendices.
* The proposal could appear to predetermine reliance on ocean disposal unless EPA better explains why narrower or phased expansion alternatives were not chosen.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

* The proposal creates incentives for port, dredging, and navigation stakeholders to support larger offshore capacity because it increases flexibility and may reduce uncertainty around future disposal planning.
* It creates incentives for environmental commenters to focus on whether the ocean-disposal footprint is larger than necessary once beneficial use and upland options are accounted for.
* It also creates incentives for commenters to ask for clearer monitoring triggers, because adaptive management is the main mechanism EPA offers for managing long-term uncertainty.
