## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim A

**Claim:** Letting AFC systems account for BEL for indoor composite access points will improve indoor performance without materially increasing interference risk.

**Underlying assumption:** AFC systems can reliably distinguish eligible indoor composite devices from outdoor standard-power devices and can apply BEL in a way that still protects incumbents.

**Failure scenario:** If indoor-status verification is weak, FCC ID and equipment-class matching are incomplete, or real-world building attenuation differs materially from the assumed values, the interference margin could be smaller than the notice suggests.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask the FCC to explain what verification, audit, and misuse-detection methods support the assumption that BEL-adjusted calculations will remain confined to genuinely eligible indoor devices.

### Claim B

**Claim:** A `6 dB` BEL allowance is an appropriate threshold for codification.

**Underlying assumption:** The waiver record and engineering basis supporting `6 dB` is transferable from case-specific approvals to generally applicable rule text.

**Failure scenario:** If actual building attenuation varies widely by structure, or if some AFC operators use different modeling choices, a single uniform `6 dB` rule could be over- or under-inclusive.

**Procedural implication:** The FCC should show why `6 dB` is not just administratively convenient but technically justified across expected deployment conditions.

### Claim C

**Claim:** Cruise ships can be carved out from the boat prohibition because they are few in number and have high attenuation from thick metal walls.

**Underlying assumption:** Cruise ships are sufficiently distinct from other boats in both attenuation and usage density to justify a categorical exception.

**Failure scenario:** If attenuation varies meaningfully across vessel designs, or if some other passenger vessels have similar use cases, the chosen cruise-ship boundary may appear arbitrary.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask for a clearer record on vessel characteristics, alternative definitions, and why the proposed `33 CFR 101.105` definition is the best fit for the interference rationale.

### Claim D

**Claim:** The proposal creates little or no public cost.

**Underlying assumption:** Voluntary adoption and producer-surplus framing are enough to avoid counting installation, compliance, and operational costs as meaningful regulatory burdens.

**Failure scenario:** If cruise operators, AFC operators, or smaller equipment makers face real implementation, testing, or compliance costs, the zero-cost framing may understate economically relevant burdens.

**Procedural implication:** This is a useful comment target because the notice quantifies benefits but treats several practical cost categories as effectively non-costs.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

* The FCC proposes two concrete changes to its `6 GHz` unlicensed rules.
* First, the FCC would allow AFC systems to account for up to `6 dB` of building entry loss when responding to spectrum inquiries from composite indoor/standard-power access points.
* Second, the FCC would allow low-power indoor access points to operate on cruise ships, despite the existing general prohibition on boat use.
* The notice also broadly asks whether additional updates to the `6 GHz` unlicensed framework are needed to reflect technological and business developments since `2020`.
* The proposed rule text would amend `47 CFR 15.407`, including explicit language permitting indoor access points on cruise ships and authorizing AFC systems to identify eligible composite access points and apply BEL up to `6 dB`.
* The FCC cites authority under sections `2`, `4(i)`, `302`, and `303` of the Communications Act.

### Inference

This is an NPRM-stage effort to convert targeted FCC and OET experience into more durable rules for indoor `6 GHz` Wi-Fi use. The core comment opportunities are less about whether more `6 GHz` flexibility is desirable in the abstract, and more about whether the FCC has shown enough engineering, threshold, and cost support for the exact boundaries it proposes.

### Evidence Quotes

* “The Commission proposes to update its rules consistent with the waivers OET granted to permit the AFC operators to take BEL into consideration in their calculations.”
* “The Commission proposes to amend its rules to permit LPI access points to operate on cruise ships.”
* “The Commission seeks comment broadly on any changes that could be made to the 6 GHz band unlicensed rules to reflect technological and business developments since the rules were first adopted in 2020.”
* “The Commission tentatively concludes that the proposed rules will yield modest benefits, including a one-time cost savings of $4,800 ... and a recurring annual benefit of $35.6 million.”

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

The proposal is technically coherent at a broad level. Both main rule changes rest on the same basic idea: some indoor `6 GHz` uses may be safely treated more flexibly because building or vessel structure materially attenuates signals and lowers harmful-interference risk. The draft regulatory text is also aligned with that theory by tying BEL use to eligible composite devices and by limiting the maritime exception to cruise ships.

The weaker point is evidentiary specificity. The notice points to thick metal walls, FCC ID matching, and OET waiver experience, but the available export does not contain the engineering detail that would show how often these safeguards fail, how much attenuation varies across conditions, or what monitoring would confirm that modeled assumptions hold in practice.

**Observed Fact:** The FCC proposes that AFC systems must identify composite indoor/standard-power access points based on the FCC ID and equipment-class information from the Equipment Authorization System.

**Inference:** The Commission recognizes that the BEL proposal depends on correct device classification, but the available record only partly explains how verification, auditing, and enforcement would work if devices are misconfigured or used contrary to the indoor assumptions.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

* BEL-based AFC calculations could underprotect incumbents if indoor-only assumptions, device identification, or attenuation estimates perform worse in practice than the notice assumes.
* A uniform `6 dB` limit could be too blunt if the technically appropriate attenuation varies by structure or device context.
* The cruise-ship carveout could create arbitrary line-drawing problems if similarly situated vessels are excluded or if actual vessel attenuation varies materially.
* The economic analysis could overstate net benefits if real deployment, compliance, or transition costs are larger than the notice recognizes.
* A final rule could rely too heavily on prior waiver experience without making the underlying engineering rationale more visible in the public record.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

* AFC operators would have an incentive to support more precise indoor-device classification and to seek design choices that maximize usable indoor power.
* Device makers may have stronger incentives to pursue composite indoor/standard-power certifications if BEL treatment is codified.
* Cruise operators would have an incentive to expand onboard `6 GHz` Wi-Fi capacity where passenger demand supports the installation cost.
* Incumbent spectrum users and public-safety interests have a clear incentive to demand stronger evidence that attenuation and device controls are sufficient.
* Commenters can create the most value by supplying engineering data, vessel-specific attenuation information, and better cost estimates rather than only making abstract arguments for or against flexibility.
