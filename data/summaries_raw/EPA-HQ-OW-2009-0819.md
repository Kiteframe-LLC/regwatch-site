## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

- **C1 baseline flaw:** Plausible and concrete as to omitted capture and pumping costs, but commenters can ask EPA to reconcile the revised baseline with prior 2024 findings and identify which data changed versus which judgments changed.
- **C2 site-specificity:** Strongly supported by the notice's groundwater and leachate discussion. The stress point is whether uncertainty supports BPJ flexibility alone or also requires minimum monitoring and record requirements.
- **C3 Option 1 preference:** Supported by quantified cost savings. The stress point is environmental performance: EPA cannot estimate pollutant loadings for functional-equivalent discharges that would receive BPJ limits.
- **C4 rejection of Options 2 and 3:** Supported by cost estimates and feasibility concerns. The stress point is whether EPA has adequately considered intermediate national floors, phased compliance, or targeted exceptions.
- **C5 net-benefit expectation:** Weakest because benefits and some pollutant loadings are qualitative or omitted; commenters can ask for bounding analysis, sensitivity analysis, or explicit uncertainty treatment.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

EPA proposes to revise the Steam Electric ELG for unmanaged combustion residual leachate (CRL) at existing steam electric power plants. The preferred Option 1 would keep numeric mercury and arsenic limits for pumped unmanaged CRL that is captured and discharged directly, but would let permitting authorities set BAT limits case-by-case using best professional judgment (BPJ) for unmanaged CRL that reaches waters through groundwater as the functional equivalent of a direct discharge.

The practical tradeoff is lower estimated compliance cost and more site-specific permitting flexibility in exchange for less national uniformity and more uncertainty about environmental outcomes.

## Step 4 — Analysis Obligations Review

- **CWA BAT factors:** EPA addresses technology availability, costs, economic achievability, non-water-quality environmental impacts, and implementation issues, but comments can test whether the final rule gives enough support for BPJ outcomes.
- **Costs and economic impacts:** The notice provides cost tables and cost-to-revenue screening. The analysis is stronger on cost than on benefits.
- **Benefits and pollutant loadings:** EPA qualitatively discusses human health and ecological pathways, but key pollutant loading changes are limited to TSS and TDS and do not quantify metals/toxic pollutant changes.
- **Alternatives:** EPA compares Options 1, 2, and 3, but commenters can request intermediate guardrails such as national minimum monitoring, presumptive treatment floors, or structured BPJ criteria.
- **Federalism/permitting:** The proposal relies heavily on state or permitting-authority BPJ; the final rule should clarify documentation and consistency expectations.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

**Alternatives**
- A national minimum BPJ documentation standard for functional-equivalent unmanaged CRL permits.
- A phased approach retaining national numeric limits unless a facility-specific record demonstrates infeasibility or disproportionate cost.
- A monitoring-first option requiring groundwater/leachate characterization before a less stringent BPJ limit is accepted.
- Targeted treatment floors for pollutants with known CRL risk, paired with variance-like site-specific relief.
- A sensitivity analysis that includes retired plants expected to incur unmanaged CRL treatment obligations.

**Comment Use:** These alternatives are useful because they accept EPA concerns about heterogeneity while reducing the risk that uncertainty becomes a reason for under-controlled discharges.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Fact:** EPA explains that unmanaged CRL differs from managed CRL because it mixes with groundwater, can vary by site, may include different chemical forms of pollutants, and may be difficult to capture completely. EPA also says only TSS and TDS were calculated for pollutant loadings due to data limitations.

**Inference:** The technical basis for rejecting one-size-fits-all limits is plausible, but the technical basis for predicting environmental consequences under BPJ is incomplete. A technically coherent final rule would connect BPJ flexibility to required sampling, monitoring, hydrogeologic analysis, and treatment-performance evidence.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact:** EPA cites CWA sections 301, 304(b), 304(g), 307, 402(a), and 501(a), plus cases recognizing agency reconsideration and cost consideration in BAT decisions. The proposal also recognizes Maui functional-equivalent discharge principles.

**Inference:** The basic legal authority appears conventional for a CWA ELG reconsideration. Litigation risk is more likely to center on reasoned explanation, treatment of prior findings and reliance interests, record support for benefits/costs, and the clarity of BPJ implementation rather than absence of statutory authority.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

**Failure Modes**
- Uncertainty ratchet: lack of data supports flexibility but also makes environmental effects harder to evaluate.
- Permit-by-permit drift: BPJ decisions could vary widely without national documentation standards.
- Benefit understatement or omission: metals, toxic pollutants, and community effects may be underrepresented if only TSS/TDS loadings are quantified.
- Retired-facility edge case: excluding retired plants from some analysis may understate continuing CRL obligations or environmental effects.

**Not Observed**
- No apparent statutory-authority substitution.
- No surveillance or eligibility-control mechanism.
- No immediate harm-before-review structure because implementation should occur through NPDES permits.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

Option 1 may incentivize facilities to contest national numeric limits and emphasize site-specific infeasibility, while incentivizing permitting authorities to develop BPJ records for technically complex groundwater-linked discharges. If the final rule lacks minimum documentation standards, cost minimization could dominate pollutant characterization and monitoring. If the final rule requires strong BPJ records, the same flexibility could better match controls to site conditions.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

Potential vulnerabilities include arbitrary-and-capricious challenges to the net-benefit conclusion if forgone benefits remain largely unquantified; inadequate explanation for changing course from the 2024 ELG; insufficient treatment of reliance or environmental consequences for affected communities; and vagueness or inconsistency risk if BPJ guardrails are left too open. EPA has stronger defenses on statutory authority, cost consideration, and the reasonableness of reconsidering a prior BAT judgment when new cost and feasibility information is identified.

## Step 13A — Structural Integrity / Nonsense Detector

The proposal is not structurally nonsensical. Its main structural weakness is a feedback-loop problem: EPA relies on uncertainty to justify BPJ flexibility, but that same uncertainty reduces confidence about pollutant and benefit consequences. The most constructive fix is not necessarily to reject BPJ, but to require minimum data collection, monitoring, reasoned BPJ documentation, and transparent permit records.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

- **Condition BPJ flexibility on minimum data and monitoring requirements.**
  Why it matters: EPA's feasibility concerns are real, but uncertainty should trigger better evidence rather than only looser limits.
  Practical question: What minimum pollutant, flow, hydrogeologic, monitoring, and treatment-performance data must support a BPJ limit?
- **Quantify or bound forgone benefits under Option 1.**
  Why it matters: EPA quantifies cost savings but leaves key health and ecological effects qualitative.
  Practical question: How large would forgone benefits need to be to offset the claimed cost savings?
- **Define the BPJ decision path for functional-equivalent discharges.**
  Why it matters: Similar discharges could receive inconsistent treatment without common record standards.
  Practical question: What findings must a permit record include for technology availability, economic achievability, alternatives, and monitoring?
- **Clarify treatment of retired plants and continuing unmanaged CRL obligations.**
  Why it matters: Retired plants may lack revenue but can still present unmanaged CRL risks.
  Practical question: How are retired facilities handled in final cost, loading, and implementation analyses?

## Step 18 — Decision Brief

- **Ask EPA to close the uncertainty loop.** The proposal is strongest on cost and weakest on environmental consequences. EPA should use uncertainty to require better data and monitoring, not only to justify more flexible limits.
- **Improve benefit-cost transparency.** EPA should show break-even or sensitivity analysis for forgone benefits, especially metals, toxic pollutants, fish-consumption pathways, drinking-water pathways, and ecological effects.
- **Make BPJ permits reviewable and comparable.** EPA should set minimum permit-record criteria and explain how inconsistent BPJ outcomes will be reviewed.
