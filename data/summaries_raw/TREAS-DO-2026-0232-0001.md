### Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

#### Claim A

**Claim**
The best reading of “Federal regulatory framework” includes implementing regulations and interpretations, not just the GENIUS Act's statutory text.

**Underlying assumption**
Congress intended “regulatory framework” to include agency-made implementing law even though the statute does not define the phrase.

**Failure scenario**
If the better reading is that section 4(c) primarily requires comparison to the statute itself, then the proposal may be layering in a broader set of obligations than Congress clearly required for State similarity review.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can request a more explicit explanation of why Treasury's interpretation is the single best reading after *Loper Bright*, especially for provisions where the Federal implementing framework is still unsettled.

#### Claim B

**Claim**
Using the OCC as the default benchmark is the best way to evaluate State-level regimes.

**Underlying assumption**
Most State qualified payment stablecoin issuers are sufficiently analogous to OCC-supervised nonbanks or uninsured national-bank analogues that the OCC baseline is the right federal comparator.

**Failure scenario**
If many realistic State issuers look more like State-chartered depository institutions or otherwise face materially different business models, the OCC-only baseline could distort the comparison and narrow State flexibility more than the statute intended.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask Treasury to explain when, if ever, another Federal regulator's framework should control and whether a single-agency benchmark is overinclusive.

#### Claim C

**Claim**
The proposed split between uniform requirements and State-calibrated requirements faithfully implements the Act.

**Underlying assumption**
Treasury has correctly identified which provisions leave substantive discretion to States and which do not.

**Failure scenario**
If Appendix A misclassifies even a few provisions, the proposal could push States toward over-harmonization or under-harmonization in ways that materially affect licensing, capital, liquidity, or enforcement design.

**Procedural implication**
The classification choices deserve line-item comment because they function as hidden policy decisions inside an ostensibly broad-principles rule.

#### Claim D

**Claim**
Excluding most non-Federal Register guidance from the federal baseline is administrable and consistent with statutory flexibility.

**Underlying assumption**
Published regulations and Federal Register interpretations capture enough of the relevant federal framework to make the comparison meaningful.

**Failure scenario**
If important compliance expectations are embodied in subregulatory materials not published in the Federal Register, then the proposed baseline may be too thin for some topics and too uncertain for others.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can press Treasury either to identify a more determinate subset of controlling materials or to explain why omitted materials do not matter.

### Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

#### Observed Facts

Treasury proposes to add 12 CFR parts 1520 and 1521 to implement section 4(c) of the GENIUS Act. The rule would:

- define the “Federal regulatory framework” largely by reference to the GENIUS Act plus OCC regulations and interpretations published in the Federal Register, with Treasury and FRB materials controlling for specified topics;
- define “State-level regulatory regime” broadly enough to include enforceable State statutes, regulations, and some guidance;
- divide section 4(a) requirements into “uniform requirements” and “State-calibrated requirements”;
- set broad-based principles for reserve assets, redemption, rehypothecation, reporting, capital, liquidity, risk management, activities, licensing, supervision, custody, insolvency, and transition to Federal oversight; and
- allow States to impose additional requirements so long as they do not conflict with Federal law or break substantial similarity.

The NPRM also asks a long series of targeted questions about definitions, scope, the proper benchmark agency, State flexibility, and treatment of particular GENIUS Act provisions.

#### Inference

This is a consequential federalism-and-market-structure rule masquerading as a principles document. It does not directly license stablecoin issuers, but it will shape whether States can build workable parallel stablecoin regimes and how closely they must track future OCC and other Federal actions.

### Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**  
Create a usable framework for deciding when State stablecoin regimes are substantially similar to the Federal framework under the GENIUS Act.

**Problem definition**  
The Act allows some State-level regulation, but only if State regimes are substantially similar and meet or exceed core standards. Treasury says the statute alone does not supply enough detail to make those judgments administrable.

**Data used**  
Primarily statutory text, statutory structure, prior Treasury ANPRM comments, and references to the OCC's proposed implementing regulations.

**Analytical method**  
Interpretive and structural reasoning: define key terms, classify statutory requirements, identify where uniformity is required, and identify where State discretion remains permissible.

**Alternatives considered**  
The NPRM acknowledges at least one important alternative: using Federal regulations for substantial-similarity analysis while using only statutory text for the “meets or exceeds” comparison under section 4(a). It also asks whether the Federal framework should be limited to statutory text, whether multiple agencies' frameworks should count, and whether unpublished guidance should be included.

**Agency conclusion**  
Treasury concludes that a largely OCC-centered baseline, combined with a uniform-versus-State-calibrated split, best carries out the GENIUS Act while preserving some State flexibility.

**Missing links / logical jumps**

- The NPRM explains why the statute alone may be too thin, but it only partly explains why the OCC should be the default benchmark for most topics rather than one comparator among several.
- The proposal relies on parallel or evolving Federal regulatory actions, yet it offers only limited discussion of how moving federal baselines will affect State certainty and certification timing.
- The classification of requirements in Appendix A is presented as a legal consequence of the Act, but several line-drawing choices appear to embed policy judgment as well as interpretation.

### Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

#### Objective: define the federal comparison baseline

**Alternatives evaluated**

- Use the Act plus implementing regulations and interpretations as the federal framework.
- Limit the framework to statutory text only.
- Use a bifurcated approach for “substantial similarity” versus “meets or exceeds.”
- Consider including more agencies' frameworks.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed in depth**

- A rebuttable presumption that the OCC is the default comparator, with a documented process for using another agency's framework when entity type or function differs.
- A fixed cross-agency matrix identifying which provisions track OCC, FRB, FDIC, NCUA, or Treasury rules rather than relying mainly on one agency.
- A transition safe harbor for States when the relevant Federal implementing rule is proposed but not final.

**Evidence suggesting feasibility**

The NPRM itself acknowledges that multiple primary Federal payment stablecoin regulators will issue relevant rules and that some provisions are best handled by Treasury or the FRB instead of the OCC.

#### Objective: preserve State flexibility while preventing regulatory arbitrage

**Alternatives evaluated**

- Distinguish between uniform requirements and State-calibrated requirements.
- Permit additional State requirements if they do not conflict with Federal law or destroy substantial similarity.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed in depth**

- A more granular provision-by-provision standard instead of the binary uniform-versus-State-calibrated classification.
- Illustrative examples showing acceptable and unacceptable State deviations for capital, liquidity, supervision, and licensing.
- A more explicit materiality test for when additional State requirements cease to be “substantially similar.”

**Evidence suggesting feasibility**

The NPRM already classifies provisions in Appendix A and asks whether each classification is correct, which suggests Treasury could provide more concrete examples without changing the overall approach.

### Step 6 — Technical Coherence

This rule does not rely on engineering or scientific modeling, but it does rely on internal coherence across several legal and supervisory concepts.

**Observed Fact**
The NPRM consistently pursues two themes that are in tension: national consistency on core prudential safeguards and meaningful State discretion on implementation details.

**Inference**
The proposal is conceptually coherent because most provisions map back to that tension. The weakest point is operational coherence when the federal baseline is not static. The notice repeatedly relies on current or future OCC, Treasury, and FRB actions while also trying to give States a stable target.

**Primary coherence risk**
The rule says States should not have to chase unpublished federal guidance, but it also adopts a federal framework that may evolve as other agencies finalize their own GENIUS Act rules. That makes the proposal coherent in principle but less precise in practice.

### Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact**
The NPRM grounds itself in sections 4(c) and 13 of the GENIUS Act and repeatedly argues from statutory text, structure, and the transition provisions in section 4(d).

**Assessment**
The proposal appears legally grounded in Treasury's delegated authority to implement the GENIUS Act and to establish broad-based principles for substantial-similarity review.

**Uncertain point**
The strongest legal uncertainty is not whether Treasury may issue a rule, but whether Treasury has sufficiently justified its choice to read “Federal regulatory framework” so broadly and to anchor it primarily in OCC materials for most provisions. That choice likely is defensible, but the record would be stronger with a fuller post-*Loper Bright* explanation of why that is the best interpretation.

### Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

**Classification:** Complex but normal

**Observed Fact**
The NPRM follows a conventional structure, includes detailed proposed regulatory text, cites prior ANPRM comments, and asks dozens of targeted questions on the most contestable interpretive choices.

**Inference**
This looks like a normal NPRM for a complicated statutory implementation rule. The main issue is not procedural irregularity. It is whether the legal and comparative reasoning is sufficiently transparent for a rule that will shape both State regulators and the emerging stablecoin market.

### Step 9 — Failure Modes

- States may face uncertainty if the federal benchmark changes after they build or update a State regime.
- The OCC-centered comparator may fit some issuer types better than others, creating mismatches in certification review.
- Ambiguity around what counts as an “interpretation” within the federal framework could lead to uneven application.
- The uniform-versus-State-calibrated split may invite disputes over whether Treasury has effectively hardened discretionary topics into quasi-mandatory ones.
- Additional State requirements may be judged inconsistently if Treasury does not explain what counts as materially undermining substantial similarity.

### Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Potential incentives created by the rule**

- States may design regimes to hew closely to OCC approaches even where the statute could support more diverse choices.
- Market participants may favor jurisdictions that can predict Treasury's interpretation of “substantial similarity” most reliably.
- Federal agencies may become de facto agenda setters for State stablecoin regulation because changes in their own implementing rules can indirectly reshape State certification prospects.

**Potential gaming concerns**

- States may frame stricter or looser provisions as “additional requirements” rather than deviations from the federal baseline.
- Issuers may structure themselves or choose charters to fit the comparator most favorable to their business model.

### Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Overall assessment:** Moderate

The NPRM's statutory discussion is substantial, but the public-facing support for key design choices is still thinner than the stakes suggest.

**Observed Fact**
Treasury provides extensive legal text and targeted questions. It also cites prior ANPRM comments and parallel OCC proposals.

**Inference**
That is enough to show a serious rulemaking effort, but not enough by itself to prove that the selected benchmark and classification choices are the most workable long-term design.

**Main weakness**
Several central propositions are plausible rather than demonstrated:

- that the OCC is the best default comparator for most State qualified issuers;
- that excluding most unpublished guidance strikes the right balance between administrability and completeness;
- that the Appendix A classifications cleanly track statutory meaning rather than policy preference; and
- that States can absorb a moving federal baseline without substantial uncertainty or transition costs.

### Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

This NPRM does not present an obvious headline APA defect on its face, but three themes could matter if left underexplained in a final rule:

- insufficient explanation for choosing an OCC-centered benchmark over a more explicitly multi-agency framework;
- inadequate support for the line between uniform and State-calibrated requirements; and
- unresolved uncertainty about how changing Federal rules and unpublished guidance affect State notice and administrability.

At the NPRM stage, these are best framed as record-strengthening comment opportunities rather than as primary adversarial attacks.

### Step 16 — Evidence Requests

- A provision-by-provision explanation of why Appendix A classifies each item as uniform or State-calibrated.

### Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

#### 1. Ask Treasury to justify the OCC-centric baseline with more than structural analogy

Request a clearer comparison showing why an OCC-led benchmark is superior to a multi-agency or issuer-type-specific approach.

#### 2. Press for line-item justification of Appendix A

Ask Treasury to explain the legal basis for classifying each section of the GENIUS Act as uniform or State-calibrated, especially where the statute appears to leave mixed discretion.

#### 3. Ask for a transition rule when the federal baseline changes

Request a safe harbor or timing rule so States and applicants know which Federal version controls during certification review.

#### 4. Ask for concrete examples of acceptable State variation

The proposal is framed as broad principles. Commenters can ask Treasury to add worked examples that make substantial-similarity review more predictable.

#### 5. Ask Treasury to clarify the treatment of subregulatory guidance

Request a more precise definition of which materials matter, which do not, and how States should handle important supervisory expectations that fall outside the Federal Register.
