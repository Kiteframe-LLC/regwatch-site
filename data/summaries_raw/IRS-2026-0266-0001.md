## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim A

**Claim**  
Digital-asset customers transact almost exclusively electronically, so paper delivery requirements are poorly matched to this market.

**Underlying assumption**  
Most affected customers reliably maintain digital access sufficient to receive, retain, and retrieve tax statements during filing season and later correction periods.

**Failure scenario**  
If a meaningful subset of customers loses account access, changes devices, changes email addresses, uses kiosks or limited-access platforms, or has accessibility barriers, the proposed all-electronic structure may increase missed statements and tax-filing errors.

**Procedural implication**  
Commenters can ask for the empirical basis for the “almost exclusively” premise and for evidence about account-access failures, bounced notices, and inaccessible delivery modes.

---

### Claim B

**Claim**  
Paper furnishing for non-consenting customers would impose avoidable compliance burdens because many customers have large transaction volumes.

**Underlying assumption**  
The burden from printing and mailing transaction-heavy statements is large enough to justify eliminating the practical paper fallback.

**Failure scenario**  
If brokers can instead use narrower tools such as consolidated statements, transaction thresholds, paper-on-request exceptions, or limited hardship accommodations, the agency’s chosen solution may look overbroad.

**Procedural implication**  
The alternatives analysis may need more visible discussion of intermediate options rather than a binary paper-versus-termination framing.

---

### Claim C

**Claim**  
Enhanced notice, access-period, and disclosure requirements sufficiently offset the risks created by eliminating withdrawal rights and paper delivery.

**Underlying assumption**  
Notice and access protections are adequate substitutes for the consumer-protection function served by paper fallback and revocable consent.

**Failure scenario**  
If customers can still miss notices, lose platform access, or need paper for tax preparation or recordkeeping reasons, the mitigation package may not fully address the new risk profile.

**Procedural implication**  
Commenters can press the agency to explain why these protections are enough and whether less restrictive options were considered.

---

### Claim D

**Claim**  
Brokers may terminate relationships with non-consenting customers rather than offering paper statements.

**Underlying assumption**  
That outcome is consistent with statutory consent requirements, consumer fairness, and tax-administration goals.

**Failure scenario**  
If termination pressures customers into nominal consent or causes disruption for existing customers who still need accurate tax statements for already-effected transactions, the consent may appear less meaningfully voluntary.

**Procedural implication**  
This is a high-leverage comment point on voluntariness, fair notice, and whether the proposed regime indirectly coerces consent.

---

### Claim E

**Claim**  
The carveout should apply to 1099-DA statements but not to other broker statements such as 1099-B.

**Underlying assumption**  
Digital-asset reporting is sufficiently different from other broker reporting to justify a separate consent regime.

**Failure scenario**  
If the same burden logic exists for other high-volume electronically mediated accounts, the rule’s line-drawing may look under-explained.

**Procedural implication**  
The agency may need a clearer explanation of why digital-asset brokerage uniquely warrants this treatment.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

The NPRM would amend Treasury regulations under section 6045 to create an alternative consent framework for furnishing Form 1099-DA statements electronically.  
It would let brokers obtain affirmative consent without being required to offer a paper-delivery alternative.  
It would specifically permit brokers to terminate business relationships with customers who do not consent.  
It would not require brokers to permit withdrawal of previously given consent.  
It would require enhanced disclosure, notice, access, and retention rules, including access through at least October 15 of the following year and retention for seven years.  
It is limited to 1099-DA statements and does not extend the same rule to 1099-B statements.

**Evidence quotes**
- “These proposed regulations would provide brokers with an alternative process for obtaining consent from their customers to receive 1099-DA statements in an electronic format without offering a paper delivery alternative.”
- “These proposed regulations would generally not require brokers to furnish the 1099-DA statements on paper ... but, instead, would specifically permit brokers to terminate their business relationship with these customers.”
- “Additionally, these proposed regulations would not require brokers to give their customers the ability to withdraw a previously provided consent.”

### Inference

This is a targeted deregulatory procedural proposal. The main question is not whether electronic delivery is permissible in general, but whether the agency has adequately justified removing two long-standing safeguards at once: paper fallback for non-consent and the ability to withdraw consent later.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**  
Reduce broker compliance burdens while still preserving tax-administration value from furnishing 1099-DA statements to customers.

**Problem definition**  
Existing consent rules designed for other payee statements are said to be impractical for digital-asset brokers because customer activity is highly electronic and can produce many sale records.

**Data used**  
Stakeholder comments, the 2024 IRSAC report, references to GAO and TIGTA materials on digital-asset tax compliance, and general assumptions about how digital-asset customers transact.

**Analytical method**  
Analogical reasoning from statute, prior electronic-furnishing rules, and consumer-notice concepts; then a policy judgment that enhanced electronic protections can replace paper fallback and withdrawal rights.

**Alternatives considered**  
The notice visibly contrasts current rules with the proposed alternative and adds stronger disclosure and access obligations, but the supplied materials do not show a robust comparative analysis of narrower options.

**Agency conclusion**  
A 1099-DA-specific consent regime without paper fallback is justified, provided brokers meet enhanced disclosure, notice, and access requirements.

**Missing links / unsupported premises / logical jumps**
- The notice asserts that digital-asset transactions are almost exclusively electronic, but the supplied record does not show the size or characteristics of the customer population that may still need paper or flexible withdrawal.
- The notice explains why paper may burden brokers, but the visible record is thinner on why less restrictive alternatives would not sufficiently reduce burden.
- The rule treats enhanced notices as an adequate substitute for withdrawal rights, but the supplied materials do not show comparative evidence that the substitute is equally protective in practice.

**Observed Fact**

- It also includes examples addressing changed software formats and kiosk-based transactions.

**Inference**

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- Customers may consent during onboarding without appreciating that consent may be effectively irreversible.
- Account closures, lost passwords, obsolete contact information, or platform access disputes may prevent retrieval of 1099-DA statements during tax season.
- Brokers may use the termination option as a routine pressure tool, turning “consent” into a take-it-or-leave-it account condition.
- Customers with mixed portfolios may receive 1099-DA electronically while still needing paper or different consent handling for 1099-B, creating confusion.
- Material software changes may require renewed consent, and customers who do not re-consent may face awkward access rules for already-effected transactions.

---

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

The rule may create incentives for:
- brokers to default aggressively toward all-electronic onboarding and make non-consent operationally unattractive;
- customers to give formal consent without meaningful review because refusal may jeopardize account continuity;
- firms to segment digital-asset activity into 1099-DA-covered channels where paper obligations are lighter than for other statement types;
- compliance teams to prioritize formal notice mechanics over whether customers actually receive and understand tax information.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

### 1. Ask for a narrower fallback option

Urge the agency to keep a limited paper-on-request or hardship exception for accessibility, account-lockout, or similar circumstances. This targets the rule’s sharpest edge without rejecting electronic delivery as the default.

### 2. Press on withdrawal rights

Ask the agency to justify why consent must be effectively irrevocable and whether a prospective withdrawal mechanism would preserve most burden reductions while reducing customer lock-in.

### 3. Request visible burden data

Ask for transaction-volume distributions, expected mailing costs, and broker burden segmentation by account type. This directly tests the necessity of the proposal.

### 4. Challenge the 1099-DA-only line drawing

Ask why the same rationale does not apply to at least some other electronically mediated broker statements and what feature of digital-asset reporting makes this market materially different.

### 5. Focus on practical access failures

Ask the agency to address what happens when a customer loses email access, changes devices, closes an account, or needs paper records for tax preparation, audit response, or language/accessibility reasons.

**Key sentences from the NPRM**

**Routine description situated in the context of the rule, law, and research**
The NPRM uses section 401 of the JCWAA and Treasury’s general rulemaking authority to design a different consent framework for 1099-DA statements than the one generally used for other payee statements. The notice reasonably identifies a broker burden problem in a high-volume electronic market, but the visible record is thinner on why the agency must remove both paper fallback and withdrawal rights rather than adopt a narrower accommodation.

**Regulatory effectiveness, statutory compliance, procedural adequacy**
The proposal may improve efficiency for brokers, but the present explanation would be stronger if it showed more clearly that the selected design is necessary, that narrower options were considered, and that the substitute protections adequately preserve meaningful consent and customer access.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**
If commenters press this point with concrete examples, the final rule could become more durable and more workable by adding limited exceptions, prospective withdrawal rights, or a better explanation of why those options were rejected.
