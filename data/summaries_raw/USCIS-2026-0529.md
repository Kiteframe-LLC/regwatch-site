## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

- **Claim:** Removing deference will improve program integrity.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** The higher fraud/non-compliance percentages observed in selected site visits during deference periods are sufficiently comparable to support an inference about deference's causal effect.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** The periods differ in selection practices, enforcement intensity, petition mix, or site-visit targeting, so the observed difference is not caused by deference.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** The agency should disclose denominators, selection rules, subgroup results, and sensitivity analysis before treating the comparison as a basis for the final rule.
- **Claim:** Removing deference will increase RFE rates by 2.9 percentage points.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** The affected and comparison groups provide a valid counterfactual and other policy changes did not differentially affect them.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** Changes in forms, adjudication guidance, workload, or case mix affect the groups differently, or the comparison group is not parallel to the affected group.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Comments can ask USCIS to provide pre-trend checks, subgroup results, and alternative specifications.
- **Claim:** Removing deference does not substantively change eligibility.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** More frequent RFEs, longer processing times, and less predictable extensions do not materially affect employment continuity or worker planning.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** A petition remains pending past an employer's staffing or project deadline, or the record needed to re-establish eligibility is disproportionately difficult for smaller employers to assemble.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** The final rule should address transition, timing, and practical safeguards even if the legal eligibility criteria remain unchanged.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

DHS proposes to remove 8 CFR 214.1(c)(5), ending the required deference posture for Form I-129 extension requests while allowing officers to consider prior approvals where relevant. The notice cites INA sections 103(a), 214, 235(d)(3), 287(b), and 291, plus related Homeland Security Act provisions.

**Observed Facts:** The rule applies to all Form I-129 benefit requests; its principal quantitative evidence concerns deference-eligible petitions and selected H-1B/L-1A data. It estimates 6,044 additional RFEs and $133,987 in annual petitioner opportunity costs, while leaving delay and other costs unquantified.

**Inference:** The practical change concerns the starting posture and intensity of extension review more than the formal eligibility standard.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

**Plausible Alternatives Not Analyzed**

**Evidence Suggesting Feasibility:** The existing rule already identifies material error, material change, and new material information as exceptions, and the notice acknowledges that officers may still consider prior approvals.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Assessment:** Partially coherent

**Observed Facts**
- The notice explains the Table 3 calculation from a 2.9 percentage-point estimate to approximately 6,044 additional RFEs and then to $133,987 in annual petitioner costs.
- It reports a 64-day average difference between petitions with and without RFEs and acknowledges that the site-visit data cover selected H-1B and L-1A cases.

**Inference:** The arithmetic chain is understandable, but external validity and causal attribution are less secure than the presentation of the calculations suggests.

**Commentable Technical Questions**
- Were pre-policy trends parallel for the affected and comparison groups, and how do results change by classification, petitioner size, and fiscal year?
- How were site visits selected, and are fraud/non-compliance rates based on comparable denominators and inspection intensity across periods?
- Why should H-1B employer data be treated as a sufficient proxy for all Form I-129 categories for the final rule's scope?

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- Universal no-deference review could increase RFEs and processing time beyond the estimate, especially in high-volume or complex classifications.
- Officers could apply a broader discretionary review posture inconsistently because the proposed text removes the prior rule without adding a replacement standard for using prior approvals.
- Petitioners may over-document or repeatedly re-prove stable facts, imposing disproportionate burdens on smaller employers and workers with limited planning certainty.
- If the causal evidence is weak, the rule could increase burden without producing the claimed reduction in fraud or erroneous approvals.
- A prior approval may receive too little weight even where the parties and underlying facts genuinely remain stable, creating avoidable inconsistency across adjudications.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Positive Incentives**
- More complete current filings
- Disclosure of changed facts
- Officer attention to current eligibility and fraud indicators

**Risk Incentives**
- Strategic over-documentation and defensive filing practices
- Earlier or more frequent filings to manage uncertainty
- Pressure to use counsel or internal compliance resources even when facts are unchanged

**Assessment:** The proposal plausibly discourages concealment of material changes, but it may also reward excessive documentation and increase the cost of ordinary extensions. The notice should explain how adjudicators will distinguish legitimate scrutiny from repetitive re-litigation.
