## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim A

**Claim**  
Raising the full-coverage and disclosure-statement thresholds to $100 million keeps nearly all currently covered dollars while removing nearly 30 percent of affected entities.

**Underlying assumption**  
The FPDS sample and the Board's use of UEIs as segment proxies are reliable enough to measure current CAS burden and the effects of the threshold shift.

**Failure scenario**  
If some covered segments do not map cleanly to a single UEI, the entity counts may be overstated or understated, and the burden-reduction case for $100 million may rest on a distorted denominator.

**Procedural implication**  
Commenters can ask the Board to publish or describe the segment-identification method more fully and to test alternative assumptions where one entity contains multiple relevant segments or one segment lacks its own UEI.

---

### Claim B

**Claim**  
$100 million is the right regulatory threshold for full CAS coverage and disclosure statements.

**Underlying assumption**  
An inflation-adjusted threshold near $92 million and the 809 Panel's recommendation of $100 million together justify rounding up to $100 million without a more granular alternatives analysis.

**Failure scenario**  
If there are meaningful differences between a pure inflation adjustment, the current $50 million threshold, and the proposed $100 million threshold, the Board may need a clearer explanation of why it chose $100 million rather than a closer-to-inflation or phased option.

**Procedural implication**  
Commenters can ask for comparison of alternatives such as $75 million, $92 million, and $100 million, including effects on disclosure statements, auditability, and new-market entry.

---

### Claim C

**Claim**  
Moving the basic applicability threshold to $35 million and eliminating the $7.5 million trigger contract threshold simplifies CAS and lowers barriers to entry.

**Underlying assumption**  
The new statutory threshold appropriately screens out lower-risk contracts while retaining coverage of the overwhelming share of contract dollars that matter for CAS purposes.

**Failure scenario**  
If the eliminated trigger threshold had been capturing risk patterns not reflected in the new one-threshold model, the simplification could undercut oversight for some contractors with uneven contract sizes.

**Procedural implication**  
This is largely statute-driven, but commenters can still ask the Board to explain implementation effects and any transition risks for contractors near the old thresholds.

---

### Claim D

**Claim**  
Ceiling value is the right measure for determining CAS applicability on single-award IDCs.

**Underlying assumption**  
For single-award IDCs, the ceiling value is both observable at award and a meaningful predictor of actual order volume over the life of the vehicle.

**Failure scenario**  
If agencies routinely set generous ceilings that are never approached, the proposed rule could over-apply CAS to single-award vehicles in ways the multiple-award rule would avoid.

**Procedural implication**  
Commenters can ask for stronger evidence on the distribution of ceiling-to-order realization, including outliers, industry variation, and whether a hybrid or rebuttable rule would better fit the data.

---

### Claim E

**Claim**  
The proposal is deregulatory and should reduce compliance burdens while preserving the most important coverage.

**Underlying assumption**  
Removing entities from full coverage or disclosure-statement requirements will materially reduce burden without creating offsetting clarity, pricing, or oversight costs.

**Failure scenario**  
If reduced disclosure statements, higher waiver authority, and broader exemptions create new ambiguity or reduce useful comparability in cost accounting practices, the deregulatory narrative may be incomplete.

**Procedural implication**  
The strongest comment angle is not opposition to simplification as such, but pressure for clearer burden quantification and a more visible accounting of possible oversight tradeoffs.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

The NPRM would raise the regulatory threshold for full CAS coverage from $50 million to $100 million.  
It would raise the disclosure-statement threshold from $50 million to $100 million and remove the current segment exemption tied to less than 30 percent of segment sales and less than $10 million in CAS-covered awards.  
It would revise CAS applicability rules to implement the 2026 NDAA's statutory increase of the basic CAS applicability threshold to $35 million and eliminate the old $7.5 million trigger-contract concept.  
It would raise executive-agency-head waiver authority from $15 million to $100 million.  
It would clarify that multiple-award IDCs are evaluated for CAS exemptions at the task- or delivery-order level, while single-award IDCs are evaluated at the contract level using the base-and-options value of the IDC.

**Evidence quotes**
- "The Board is proposing to raise the thresholds for full CAS coverage and disclosure statement requirements to $100 million..."
- "Section 1806 of the 2026 NDAA fully codified the OMB legislative proposal..."
- "This proposed rule raises the CAS waiver authority threshold for executive agency heads to $100 million."
- "The exemptions listed in paragraph (b) shall be determined at the time of award of any individual task or delivery order..."
- "The exemptions listed in paragraph (b) shall be determined at the time of award of the indefinite delivery contract..."

### Inference

This is a targeted deregulatory NPRM focused on reducing CAS burden for mid-size and nontraditional contractors while retaining CAS on the largest contracts and clarifying how CAS applies to indefinite-delivery vehicles. The main question is not whether the Board has authority to make these changes, because much of the proposal is clearly statute-linked, but whether the Board has shown enough work to justify the specific threshold choices, the `UEI = segment` proxy, and the choice to use ceiling value for single-award IDCs.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**  
Reduce unnecessary CAS compliance burden, lower barriers to entry, and provide clearer rules for applying CAS to indefinite-delivery contracts.

**Problem definition**  
Current thresholds are said to be outdated relative to inflation and current contracting patterns, and current CAS rules do not clearly address how exemptions should apply to modern indefinite-delivery vehicles.

**Data used**  
FPDS data from FY 2020 through FY 2024, the 809 Panel's recommendation, public comments on the 2024 staff discussion paper, statutory changes in the 2017 and 2026 NDAAs, and the Board's assumption that UEIs approximate covered segments.

**Analytical method**  
The Board uses historical threshold comparisons, inflation reference points, counts of affected entities and dollars in FPDS, and a categorical distinction between multiple-award and single-award IDCs.

**Alternatives considered**  
The notice references six possible approaches in the 2024 staff discussion paper for IDVs and notes the 809 Panel recommendation, but the supplied record does not provide a developed comparative analysis of threshold options such as $75 million or $92 million, nor a transparent comparison of alternate rules for single-award IDCs.

**Agency conclusion**  
CAS should apply less often and more predictably: basic applicability at $35 million, full coverage and disclosure statements at $100 million, agency-head waiver authority up to $100 million, multiple-award IDC analysis at order level, and single-award IDC analysis at contract level.

**Missing links / unsupported premises / logical jumps**
- The notice states the Board has reasoned that UEI represents a covered segment, but the supplied materials do not show testing or error bounds for that proxy.
- The notice gives an inflation-adjusted value of $92 million and proposes $100 million, but the visible record does not show why $100 million is preferable to a closer inflation figure or a lower alternative.
- The single-award IDC rule depends on the claim that ceilings significantly track realized ordering, but the supplied record provides only summary percentages rather than the distribution needed to test edge cases and overinclusion risk.
- The expected-impact section describes the proposal as deregulatory and barrier-reducing, but the supplied materials do not quantify compliance savings or offsetting oversight costs.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- Entities that are covered segments for CAS purposes may not align cleanly with UEIs, weakening the entity-count analysis used to justify threshold changes.
- A $100 million threshold could remove firms from full coverage or disclosure statements even where their accounting complexity or federal-business concentration still warrants more robust CAS treatment.
- Single-award IDCs with high ceilings and low realized orders could be swept into CAS coverage based on projected capacity rather than actual ordering.
- Higher waiver authority may reduce Board-level visibility into waivers and produce more variation across agencies in when CAS gets waived.
- Contractors may structure vehicles, options, or award patterns to remain below the revised thresholds or to take advantage of the clearer multiple-award order-level rule.

---

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

The rule may create incentives for:
- agencies to use higher-value single-award vehicles knowing the ceiling can determine CAS applicability at award;
- contractors near the threshold to structure awards, options, or segmentation practices to stay under full-coverage or disclosure-statement triggers;
- executive agencies to rely more heavily on their own waiver authority instead of escalating waiver requests to the Board;
- firms that recently lost small-business status to enter the federal market sooner because modified CAS or exemption rules remain available for larger awards than before.

**Routine description situated in the context of the rule, law, and research**
This NPRM is partly a statutory implementation rule and partly a discretionary deregulatory rule. The legal basis for the $35 million basic threshold and the $100 million waiver authority is comparatively clear because Congress already acted. The more commentable part of the record concerns the Board's own discretionary decisions: raising the full-coverage and disclosure-statement thresholds to $100 million, removing the separate disclosure-statement carveout, and treating single-award IDCs differently from multiple-award IDCs based on ceiling value. Those choices rest on summarized FPDS analysis and a segment proxy that the notice itself invites commenters to test.

The proposal likely advances simplification goals, but its procedural durability would improve if the Board showed more of its work on threshold selection, segment identification, and single-award IDC overinclusion risk. The strongest comments will likely ask for clearer evidence and narrower comparative reasoning rather than challenge the Board's authority in the abstract.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**
If commenters press for the underlying analysis now, the final rule could become more durable and more targeted by either supplying stronger justification for the Board's choices or narrowing aspects of the proposal that are not as well supported as the notice suggests.
