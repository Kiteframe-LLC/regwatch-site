## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

- **Claim:** The rule does not expand the scope of data that must be reviewed, submitted, or reported.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** Existing provisions already focus on information relevant to human safety rather than on the named method, and FDA has consistently accepted nonanimal methods where appropriate.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** Sponsors and reviewers could treat the broad definition as changing which methods qualify or how much documentation is needed, especially where an assay, model, or human-biology-based method has not yet been qualified for a particular context of use.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Ask FDA to identify representative provisions and prior submissions demonstrating that the proposed language changes terminology rather than the evidentiary threshold.
- **Claim:** The proposed definition can include cell-based assays, organ chips, computer modeling, bioprinting, and animal tests or studies without substantive differences in regulatory effect.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** These heterogeneous methods can be evaluated under the same existing safety-review framework without additional codified distinctions.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** Different methods may have different validation, biological-relevance, reproducibility, and context-of-use limitations, producing uncertainty about when data are adequate to support clinical investigations.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Request a method-neutral explanation of the minimum information FDA will expect for technical characterization and biological relevance.
- **Claim:** The proposal will impose no new costs and no new requirements.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** Sponsors will continue providing the same information and FDA will apply the same review expectations, with only terminology changing.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** If sponsors seek to use new methods or if reviewers request additional validation to determine whether those methods are scientifically valid, transition and documentation costs may arise even without a new testing mandate.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Ask FDA to distinguish legally required submissions from foreseeable validation, consultation, and transition activities that the rule may induce.
- **Claim:** The direct final rule is unlikely to receive significant adverse comments.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** The amendments are noncontroversial conforming changes and commenters can identify any material objection within the defined procedure.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** A commenter may identify a material concern about the definition's scope or the absence of criteria for method adequacy, but the agency could classify a proposed change as outside the significant-adverse-comment standard if the comment does not explain why the rule is ineffective without that change.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Ask FDA to explain how substantive objections to scope, validation, and implementation will be evaluated and preserved for the ordinary notice-and-comment process.
- **Claim:** The changes will foster scientifically valid new testing methodologies while preserving safety.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** Greater terminology flexibility will encourage innovation without reducing the quality or comparability of safety evidence.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** A method may be innovative but not fit for a particular context of use, or may be accepted unevenly across review divisions, creating inconsistent incentives and safety judgments.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Request examples of contexts in which the new terminology is expected to change sponsor behavior and how FDA will monitor safety-relevant outcomes.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

**Regulatory Action:** FDA proposes method-neutral terminology and definitions for selected nonclinical-study provisions in 21 CFR parts 312, 314, 315, 361, and 601.

**Affected Entities**
- Sponsors and applicants for human drugs and biological products
- Developers of new approach methodologies and other nonanimal testing methods
- Patients and the public who depend on reliable preclinical and nonclinical safety evidence

**Statutory Authority:** FDA cites FD&C Act sections 505 and 701, FDORA section 3209, PHS Act section 351, and delegated HHS authority.

**Key Regulatory Change:** The rule replaces animal, preclinical, and in vitro references with nonclinical terminology while retaining existing FDA adequacy review and adding no testing mandate.

**Observed Facts**
- The proposed definition expressly lists cell-based assays, organ chips and microphysiological systems, computer modeling, other nonhuman or human biology-based methods such as bioprinting, and animal tests or studies.
- The notice states that FDA will continue to determine whether data from nonanimal methods adequately support the relevant clinical or safety purpose.

**Inference:** The proposal may lower terminology barriers, but its practical effect depends on how FDA applies adequacy and scientific-validity judgments across methods and review contexts.

**Evidence Quotes**
- FDA will examine any toxicological data from nonanimal nonclinical studies to determine whether they adequately support the clinical studies identified in the IND.
