## Step 4 — Analysis Obligations Review

EPA satisfies the standard NPRM obligations at a high level, but the record is thinner on the visible calculations behind the capacity and cost assumptions. The strongest comment leverage is where the notice relies on summary-level support instead of showing the underlying math.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

EPA clearly solicits comments on alternative contaminants and methods, which makes those points deliberate solicitation rather than hidden defects. The more arguable gap is the lack of a visible side-by-side comparison of phased rollout or alternate small-system sampling options.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

The technical structure is coherent: EPA pairs contaminants with methods, explains why 1,2,3-TCP and chlorpyrifos oxon matter, and connects laboratory approval to reporting. The weak point is not the technical logic itself, but whether the capacity assumption is strong enough to carry the design into practice.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

EPA appears to have a solid statutory hook under SDWA section 1445(a)(2), with AWIA shaping the large-system and representative-sample design. The open question is the scope of discretion EPA has when it discusses petitioned contaminants like microplastics and chooses to defer broader monitoring until later.

## Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

The process looks normal for a major monitoring NPRM: public comment, a separate PRA deadline, public meetings, docketed support materials, and voluntary state partnership language. Any opacity is mostly in the technical attachments, not the procedural frame.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

The realistic failure modes are implementation failures: lab capacity, appropriations, method approval, reporting consistency, and uneven state participation. The rule is not obviously unstable on its face.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

The rule creates incentives to optimize to approved methods and reporting, and to prioritize larger systems if budgets tighten. It does not create an obvious evasion incentive.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

The record is strong on program purpose, statutory framing, and implementation mechanics, but thinner on the visible comparison that led to the final contaminant list and on the full cost/capacity calculations.

## Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

The practical record gaps are lab throughput, contaminant screening, cost inputs, and the smaller-system fallback scenario. Each of those is commentable now and matters to whether the program works as designed.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

If EPA finalizes the rule without fuller capacity math or a clearer comparison set for excluded contaminants, the explanation may be vulnerable as summary-level rather than fully reasoned. The better comments here are record-building comments, not litigation brief comments.

## Step 13A — Structural Integrity / Nonsense Detector

**Overall Assessment:** The proposal is structurally coherent and looks like a normal NPRM, but two pressure points remain: the visible evidence-to-burden match is thinner than ideal, and the rollout still depends on capacity and appropriations assumptions that are not fully shown in the preamble.

**Observed Facts**

- EPA identifies the contaminant package, the laboratory pipeline, and the AWIA population thresholds.

**Inferences**

  Why it matters: if the capacity assumption fails, the rule may shrink or stall before it produces the intended national data.
  Ask agency to show the throughput math, contingency options, and any trigger for reducing the small-system sample.

  Ask agency to publish the screening criteria and comparative ranking that led to the 30-contaminant package.
- Ask EPA to disclose the unit-cost assumptions behind the burden estimate.
  Why it matters: lab and shipping costs are the biggest pieces of the national cost picture, so they should be easy to audit.
