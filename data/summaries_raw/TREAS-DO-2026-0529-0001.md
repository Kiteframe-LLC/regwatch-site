## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

- **Claim:** The proposed definitions will reduce uncertainty and market disruption.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** The definitions are sufficiently objective and administrable across layered ownership structures, portfolio purchases, local tax systems, and exception programs.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** Entities face different land assessments, ambiguous control rights, or inconsistent treatment of written programs, causing transactions to be delayed or structured around uncertain interpretations.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** The final rule should supply examples, fallback methods, and implementation metrics for the definitions most likely to create transaction-level uncertainty.
- **Claim:** The tax-assessed land-value subtraction is a clear and simple method for the renovate-to-rent threshold.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** Land and improvement values are separately stated, current, available, and comparable across jurisdictions, and the subtraction will not produce distorted or negative structure values.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** A jurisdiction does not separately assess land, the assessment is under appeal or capped, or the land value equals or exceeds the transaction price, leaving the investor without a consistent denominator.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Treasury should explain fallback valuation methods and how the 15 percent and 10 percent thresholds operate in those edge cases.
- **Claim:** The exceptions will preserve housing supply and support homeownership without undermining the statutory prohibition.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** The program conditions, safe harbors, transfer rules, and passive-investor definition distinguish genuinely beneficial activity from avoidance structures.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** A nominally written program or contractual consent right qualifies for an exception while producing little owner-occupant benefit, or a transfer between institutional investors preserves the exception without a practical consumer remedy.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** The record should compare safe-harbor thresholds with likely market behavior and explain how Treasury will detect circumvention without imposing arbitrary compliance costs.
- **Claim:** A single purchase involving multiple large institutional investors should count as one violation for maximum civil-penalty purposes.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** A single-violation cap fairly prevents excess penalties while leaving responsibility and deterrence intact.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** Multiple entities intentionally divide control or use affiliates, and the single-violation treatment reduces deterrence or leaves penalty allocation unpredictable.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** The final rule should clarify how the cap interacts with direct participation, affiliate structures, separate transactions, and responsibility allocation.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

Treasury proposes 31 CFR part 36 to implement section 1001 of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act. The proposal defines terms used in the statutory large-institutional-investor prohibition, clarifies program-based and other purchase exceptions, defines passive-investor and property-control concepts, and treats a single prohibited purchase involving multiple large institutional investors as one violation for maximum civil-penalty purposes.

**Observed Facts:** The notice describes an effective date 180 days after enactment, a phased rulemaking approach, 18-month build-to-rent timing, 15 percent and 10 percent renovate-to-rent benchmarks, five- and seven-year homeownership-support tiers, a 30-day first-look period, and a five-business-day matching period. It also states that reliable data are not currently available on the number of affected institutional investors and that records expected under the proposal would be voluntary and generally retained by the entities.

**Inference:** The proposal’s practical effect is to turn broad statutory exceptions and control concepts into operational tests. The central comment leverage is whether those tests are objective enough to prevent both evasion and unnecessary exclusion, and whether the record supports the chosen thresholds and safe harbors.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- **Failure Mode:** Valuation inconsistency
  
  **Mechanism:** Different tax-assessment practices produce different structure denominators for the same percentage threshold.
  
  **Consequence:** Uneven exception eligibility and transaction delay.
  
  **Comment Use:** Request fallback rules, examples, and jurisdictional sensitivity analysis.
- **Failure Mode:** Control-right overreach
  
  **Mechanism:** Broad consent, veto, approval, or covenant language is treated as property control without a clear safe harbor.
  
  **Consequence:** Passive capital or ordinary lender/vendor protections are swept into the covered class.
  
  **Comment Use:** Submit representative agreements and propose neutral exclusions or examples.
- **Failure Mode:** Program formalism
  
  **Mechanism:** A written program satisfies the formal definition but does not deliver the expected housing or consumer benefit in practice.
  
  **Consequence:** Exception use diverges from the statute’s protective purpose.
  
  **Comment Use:** Request outcome metrics and anti-evasion conditions.
- **Failure Mode:** Penalty allocation uncertainty
  
  **Mechanism:** A single-violation cap is clear but responsibility among multiple investors is left for future action.
  
  **Consequence:** Unpredictable enforcement and reduced deterrence for deliberate structuring.
  
  **Comment Use:** Ask Treasury to clarify affiliate, direct-participant, and separate-transaction treatment.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Threshold Gaming:** The 15 percent, 10 percent, 18-month, five-year, seven-year, 30-day, 90-day, and five-business-day thresholds create incentives to time transactions or allocate costs and rights to qualify for an exception.

**Mitigation:** Use examples, anti-circumvention principles, consistent recordkeeping expectations, and public feedback metrics.
