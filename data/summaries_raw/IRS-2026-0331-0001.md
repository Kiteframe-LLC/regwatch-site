# Administrative Rulemaking Structural Review

This review is limited to the available notice text and export metadata for the IRS NPRM on tax-exempt refunding bonds. The docket export identifies no supporting attachments, and the available record is therefore the NPRM itself plus the metadata generated in Step 1.

## Phase 1 — Docket Integrity Check

**Classification:** Thin record

**Observed Fact**
The metadata identifies `IRS-2026-0331-0001` as the substantive proposed rule and reports `supporting_related_material_count: 0`.

**Observed Fact**
The notice text cites prior guidance, including Rev. Proc. 2024-37, Notice 2023-39, and Notice 2024-32, but those materials were not included as supporting attachments in the export package.

**Inference**
For a tax-procedure NPRM that largely codifies prior guidance and clarifies existing regulations, a thin public-facing docket is not unusual. It still limits the ability to test claims about the prevalence of the identified problems, the number of affected issuers, and the practical consequences of the proposed clarifications.

**Unknown**
Whether Treasury or the IRS relied on internal quantitative analyses, field data, or commenter submissions beyond the short characterizations included in the preamble.

**Supporting-material decision**
No attachment fetch appears required for a defensible summary. The docket export lists no supporting materials, and the core reasoning claims can be evaluated directly from the NPRM text.

### Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim A

**Claim**
Most changes are clarifications or codifications of existing guidance rather than materially new policy.

**Underlying assumption**
The proposed text merely aligns regulations with already understood practice and does not alter compliance incentives in major ways.

**Failure scenario**
If the changes meaningfully expand the ability to recover rebate overpayments, enlarge guarantee capacity, or narrow the scope of refunding-issue treatment, the proposal may have larger distributional or compliance effects than the notice suggests.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask the IRS to identify which changes are purely conforming and which materially alter practical outcomes for issuers, borrowers, or investors.

### Claim B

**Claim**
The existing refund-claim deadline was too short in certain post-final-computation situations.

**Underlying assumption**
Those situations occur often enough, or are serious enough, to justify codifying the longer deadline from Rev. Proc. 2024-37.

**Failure scenario**
If late post-final-computation payments are rare or arise from unusual fact patterns, the agency may be making a broader regulatory change without describing the scale of the problem.

**Procedural implication**
The agency should be asked to provide examples, counts, or representative fact patterns showing why the old deadline was practically inadequate.

### Claim C

**Claim**
The transferred-proceeds clarification is needed because a narrower reading could let issuers avoid rebate.

**Underlying assumption**
The identified interpretation gap is real and creates meaningful risk of inconsistent valuation or rebate avoidance.

**Failure scenario**
If the issue was mostly hypothetical, or already controlled by other provisions, the NPRM may be tightening language without showing how often the problem actually arises.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can request examples of the disputed interpretation and ask whether any narrower clarification was considered.

### Claim D

**Claim**
Funds should have to be held on the cash-outlay date to be allocable to an expenditure.

**Underlying assumption**
That rule best reflects section 148 accounting logic and avoids opportunistic retroactive allocations.

**Failure scenario**
If ordinary financing operations routinely involve short timing mismatches that do not create abuse, the clarification could constrain legitimate accounting practices more than the preamble acknowledges.

**Procedural implication**
The notice would be stronger with examples showing the difference between abusive backfilling and ordinary administrative timing.

### Claim E

**Claim**
Raising the guarantee-fund threshold to 500 percent is justified because demand has grown and comments supported the change.

**Underlying assumption**
The 500 percent number is the right level, not just a larger level.

**Failure scenario**
If the record lacks analysis showing why 500 percent is appropriate, the proposal may appear to import Notice 2023-39's interim rule without explaining the boundary choice.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask for the basis for 500 percent, including alternatives considered and any risk analysis for guarantee-fund leverage.

### Claim F

**Claim**
The student-loan refinancing and cross-calling clarifications are needed to prevent unintended tax consequences.

**Underlying assumption**
Existing text creates enough uncertainty to chill legitimate refinancing activity, and the proposed exclusions are properly bounded.

**Failure scenario**
If the two-year expectation rule or proceeds exclusion has edge cases the NPRM does not discuss, issuers may face new uncertainty at the margins even as Treasury resolves one identified problem.

**Procedural implication**
The IRS should be pressed to explain how the rule applies to mixed-purpose financings, partial refinancings, and failed expectations.

### Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

The NPRM proposes amendments under sections 148, 150, and 7805 of the Internal Revenue Code affecting tax-exempt and other tax-advantaged bonds.

The proposal does several things at once:

- removes an obsolete reserve-fund provision tied to former section 148(d)(3);
- codifies a broader deadline for refund claims on rebate overpayments;
- clarifies that transferred-proceeds valuation carries over for all section 148 purposes, not only yield restriction;
- clarifies that funds allocated to an expenditure must be held on the cash-outlay date;
- raises the permitted guarantee capacity for certain State perpetual trust funds to 500 percent of asset cost;
- treats special 90-day SLGS certificates as tax-exempt bonds for section 148 purposes;
- adds student-loan refinancing and cross-calling clarifications to the refunding-issue rules; and
- updates the filing-address rule so the IRS can designate addresses through the Internal Revenue Bulletin or its website.

The notice states that most provisions would apply to bonds sold on or after 90 days after publication of final regulations, with some earlier applicability or reliance rules for specified provisions.

### Inference

This is a legal-procedural NPRM rather than a large economic regulatory program. Its main administrative-law question is not whether Treasury has authority to regulate these bond rules, but whether the notice gives enough public-facing explanation for the chosen thresholds and carveouts when it converts prior notices and operational fixes into formal regulations.

### Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**  
Update the arbitrage and refunding regulations so they better reflect statutory changes, prior guidance, and current administrative needs.

**Problem definition**  
Several existing regulations are outdated, incomplete, or unclear. The current text can produce timing problems for rebate-refund claims, confusion about transferred-proceeds valuation and expenditure allocations, capacity limits for school-bond guarantees, debt-limit complications for SLGS holdings, uncertainty around student-loan refinancing, and outdated filing instructions.

**Data used**  
The notice relies mainly on narrative descriptions of identified problems, cited prior guidance, asserted questions from issuers, and a statement that comments on Notice 2023-39 supported the guarantee-fund revision.

**Analytical method**  
Mostly interpretive and practical. Treasury identifies a rule text problem, explains the compliance consequence it sees, then inserts a clarifying or codifying amendment. The method is legally coherent but only lightly quantified.

**Alternatives considered**  
The visible notice does not present a robust alternatives analysis. It generally moves from identified issue to preferred fix, often by incorporating a prior notice or revenue procedure.

**Agency conclusion**  
The proposal will modernize and clarify the rules without imposing significant new economic burdens.

### Missing links / logical jumps

- The notice often states that questions have arisen or that certain outcomes may occur, but it rarely describes how often those issues occur in practice.
- The 500 percent guarantee-fund threshold is presented as supported by comments, but the public-facing explanation of why that level is appropriate is thin.
- The small-entity discussion says many small governmental jurisdictions may be affected, yet still concludes impact is not significant without much visible supporting detail.

### Step 4 — Analysis Obligations Review

### Adequate data

**Classification:** Partially satisfied

The NPRM provides enough narrative detail to understand each proposed amendment, but limited empirical support for the scale or frequency of the underlying problems.

### Transparent methodology

**Classification:** Partially satisfied

Treasury's interpretive logic is usually clear, but the notice does not show much methodology for selecting specific boundaries such as the 500 percent guarantee-fund cap or the two-year student-loan expectation rule beyond cross-reference to prior guidance.

### Evaluation of reasonable alternatives

**Classification:** Not demonstrated

The public-facing NPRM does not materially discuss alternative ways to solve the identified issues, narrower options, or reasons for rejecting different thresholds.

### Explanation of analytical thresholds

**Classification:** Partially satisfied

Some thresholds are inherited from prior guidance, but the notice still gives limited fresh explanation for why the proposed lines are the right ones to codify in regulation.

### Consideration of foreseeable impacts

**Classification:** Partially satisfied

The NPRM addresses legal and administrative effects and includes standard RFA, UMRA, and federalism statements, but it provides only limited discussion of practical effects on smaller issuers or on financing structures that may sit near the edges of the clarified rules.

### Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

### Alternatives evaluated

Unknown from available text. The NPRM does not visibly present a structured alternatives discussion for the principal amendments.

### Plausible alternatives not analyzed

- A shorter or more conditional extension for rebate-refund claims rather than the proposed codification of the Rev. Proc. 2024-37 approach.
- A more explicit anti-abuse example for transferred proceeds instead of a broader clarification to "all purposes" under section 148.
- A safe harbor or example set for allocation-to-expenditure timing questions rather than a single bright-line sentence.
- Alternative guarantee-fund caps below or above 500 percent, with supporting risk rationale.
- A narrower proceeds exclusion or additional examples for student-loan refinancing and cross-calling.

### Evidence suggesting feasibility

The notice already identifies the specific problems each amendment is trying to solve. That means Treasury likely could have discussed competing thresholds or narrower drafting options without changing the overall structure of the NPRM.

### Step 6 — Technical Coherence

The proposal is technically coherent in the sense that each amendment addresses a recognizable tax-bond administration problem.

The strongest parts are the conforming updates:

- removing an obsolete rule tied to a statutory repeal;
- updating the filing-address rule; and
- treating special 90-day SLGS certificates as covered instruments during debt-limit contingencies.

The weaker parts are the threshold-dependent or boundary-setting changes. The NPRM says the 500 percent guarantee-fund revision is warranted and that the student-loan refinancing clarification is needed, but it offers limited public-facing analysis of why those chosen lines best address the problems.

**Assessment:** Coherent overall, but some key line-drawing choices are only partly explained.

### Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact**
The notice cites section 148(i) and section 7805(a) as express delegations of rulemaking authority and grounds the amendments in sections 148 and 150 of the Code.

**Assessment**
The agency's legal authority to issue the rule is clear from the face of the NPRM.

**Potential legal tension**
The most plausible legal vulnerability is not lack of authority. It is whether Treasury has adequately explained certain chosen thresholds and exclusions when codifying prior guidance into formal regulations.

**Unknown**
Whether comments on the cited prior notices raised narrower objections that Treasury considered but did not summarize in the NPRM.

### Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

### Observed Fact

The notice includes standard sections on authority, background, proposed applicability dates, Regulatory Flexibility Act certification, UMRA, federalism, and comment procedures.

### Opacity

The docket is procedurally normal for an IRS NPRM, but analytically thin in the sense that the public package does not include the comments or support materials that the notice references when justifying several specific choices.

### Step 9 — Failure Modes

- Issuers may remain uncertain about edge cases for the new student-loan refinancing rule if refinancing expectations change after issuance.
- The clarified expenditure-allocation rule may be applied rigidly in situations involving ordinary administrative timing rather than abusive retroactive allocations.
- The 500 percent guarantee-fund cap could prove either too high or too low if Treasury has not publicly explained the basis for that number.
- Cross-calling and proceeds-definition clarifications may still leave mixed-purpose or hybrid financing structures open to interpretive disputes.

### Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

The proposal creates several incentives:

- issuers may be more willing to seek rebate refunds if the filing deadline becomes clearer and more forgiving;
- bond counsel may structure student-loan refinancings with greater confidence where the proposed exclusions apply;
- State guarantee programs may expand guaranteed bond volume up to the new threshold if final rules adopt the 500 percent cap;
- issuers may prefer bright-line readings of the allocation and transferred-proceeds clarifications, which makes examples and edge-case discussion important.

### Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Stronger parts**

- The obsolete-rule removal is straightforward.
- The filing-address change is operationally self-explanatory.
- The debt-limit contingency fix for special 90-day SLGS certificates is easy to understand and tied to a defined regulatory problem.

**Weaker parts**

- The notice gives only a thin explanation for why 500 percent is the correct guarantee-fund threshold.
- The student-loan refinancing and cross-calling provisions are plausible but would benefit from more examples and edge-case discussion.
- The RFA certification is asserted with limited visible support, especially after acknowledging that a substantial number of small jurisdictions may be affected.

### Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

Likely gaps in the available record include:

- examples or counts showing how often rebate overpayment claims were blocked by the older deadline;
- a clearer explanation for choosing 500 percent as the guarantee-fund limit;
- more developed examples for the student-loan refinancing and cross-calling clarifications;
- practical illustrations distinguishing impermissible retroactive allocations from ordinary timing issues under the cash-outlay rule;
- supporting detail for the claim that the rule will not significantly affect a substantial number of small entities.

### Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

Likely APA-relevant vulnerabilities are modest rather than headline-grabbing.

The clearest concerns are:

- incomplete explanation for selected thresholds and exclusions, especially the 500 percent guarantee-fund cap;
- limited discussion of reasonable alternatives or narrower options;
- a sparse public-facing basis for the small-entity certification.

This looks more like a "show more work" NPRM than a rule with an obvious statutory-authority defect.

## Phase 3 — Comment Generation

### Step 14 — Commentable Sentences

1. The statement that the proposal merely "update[s]" the arbitrage rules and definitions.
2. The statement that the older rebate-refund deadline "may not provide an adequate opportunity" in certain circumstances.
3. The statement that Treasury disagrees with a narrow reading of "arbitrage restrictions" because it could allow rebate avoidance.
4. The statement that comments on Notice 2023-39 "supported the revision" to the guarantee-fund rule.
5. The statement that special 90-day certificates may cause section 148 failures during debt-limit contingencies.
6. The statement that the proposal would not have a significant economic impact on a substantial number of small entities.

### Step 15 — Procedural Concern Score

**Score: 4/10**

### Why

The NPRM is legally grounded and mostly coherent, but the public-facing record is thin where Treasury codifies prior notices and chooses specific thresholds. The best comments will likely push for explanation and examples, not attack the rule as facially unauthorized.

### Step 16 — Evidence Requests

Commenters could request:

- examples or statistics showing how the prior rebate-refund deadline failed in practice;
- the analysis or comments supporting the 500 percent guarantee-fund threshold;
- examples illustrating the intended reach of the student-loan refinancing and cross-calling clarifications;
- examples showing how the expenditure-allocation clarification applies to ordinary financing operations;
- the basis for concluding that the proposal will not significantly affect a substantial number of small governmental jurisdictions.

### Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

### 1. Press for support behind the 500 percent guarantee-fund cap

Ask the IRS to explain why 500 percent is the correct ceiling, what alternatives were considered, and whether any risk or leverage analysis supports that number.

### 2. Ask for examples around student-loan refinancing and cross-calling

The proposal likely solves a real interpretive problem, but commenters can ask for examples covering mixed-purpose financings, changed expectations, and partial refinancings.

### 3. Ask the IRS to distinguish anti-abuse clarifications from ordinary timing administration

For the expenditure-allocation rule, commenters can ask whether the amendment targets abuse, ordinary bookkeeping delays, or both, and request examples that cabin the rule's reach.

### 4. Test the small-entity certification

Because the notice says a substantial number of small jurisdictions may be affected, commenters can ask the IRS to show more clearly why the effect is still not significant.

### Step 18 — Decision Brief

### Leverage Point 1

**Key NPRM sentence**  
Comments on Notice 2023-39 "supported the revision" to the guarantee-fund rule.

**Routine description**  
This sentence carries much of the justification for a concrete numerical threshold. The NPRM does not provide much public-facing explanation for why support for change also supports this exact number.

**Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy**  
Procedural adequacy is the main issue. Treasury can likely make this change, but the record would be stronger if it showed why 500 percent is the right line.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**  
The IRS may add explanation, examples, or a more developed response to comments before finalizing the threshold.

**Worth a comment?** High

### Leverage Point 2

**Key NPRM sentence**  
The older rebate-refund deadline "may not provide an adequate opportunity" in certain circumstances.

**Routine description**  
The proposed extension is plausible, but the notice gives little information about how often the problem happens and what cases it is trying to reach.

**Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy**  
This is an evidence-and-examples issue rather than a major legal defect.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**  
More examples could narrow interpretive uncertainty and help commenters evaluate whether the codified deadline is calibrated correctly.

**Worth a comment?** Medium to High

### Leverage Point 3

**Key NPRM sentence**  
The proposal would not have a significant economic impact on a substantial number of small entities.

**Routine description**  
The NPRM acknowledges that many small governmental jurisdictions may be affected, but gives limited public-facing support for the conclusion that the impact will not be significant.

**Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy**  
This is a classic request-for-more-support issue under the notice's own RFA discussion.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**  
Treasury may provide more granular explanation of who is affected and why the compliance burden remains modest.

**Worth a comment?** Medium

## Bottom Line

This NPRM is best understood as a package of tax-bond clarifications, codifications, and operational fixes rather than a sweeping substantive rewrite. Its strongest features are clear authority and a generally coherent description of the problems being addressed. Its weaker feature is the thin public-facing explanation for certain line-drawing choices, especially the 500 percent guarantee-fund threshold, the contours of the student-loan refinancing carveout, and the small-entity certification. Those are the most productive areas for constructive public comment.
