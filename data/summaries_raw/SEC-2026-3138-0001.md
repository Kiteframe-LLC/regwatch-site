## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Claim: Simplifying the framework into two primary filer categories will better balance burdens and investor protection.
Underlying assumption: The current mix of LAF, AF, NAF, SRC, and EGC interactions is more costly and confusing than useful.
Failure scenario: The simplification removes disclosure and assurance requirements that investors actually rely on, while leaving major complexity in place through parallel EGC and other form-specific rules.
Procedural implication: Commenters can ask the Commission to identify which complexity costs are actually being eliminated and which remain.

Claim: A `$2 billion` public-float threshold and `60-calendar-month` seasoning period are appropriate for LAF status.
Underlying assumption: Only the very largest and more seasoned issuers should carry the full non-scaled disclosure and timing burden.
Failure scenario: The threshold is too high or the seasoning period too long, causing a large shift out of accelerated treatment without enough justification for investors who lose information or assurance.
Procedural implication: The record would be stronger with a more explicit threshold rationale, sensitivity analysis, and explanation of why a shorter seasoning period would be inadequate.

Claim: Newly exempting `1,596` registrants from ICFR auditor attestation will generate material compliance savings.
Underlying assumption: The proposal's newly eligible NAFs will actually reduce attestation spending and related compliance work.
Failure scenario: Many issuers continue obtaining attestation voluntarily, or the reduction in audit scope is smaller than assumed, weakening the claimed savings.
Procedural implication: Commenters have leverage to request clearer assumptions, ranges, and evidence about real section `404(b)` cost savings.

Claim: The smallest-NAF accommodation targets issuers for which reporting burdens are heaviest.
Underlying assumption: Total assets at or below `$35 million` over two second-fiscal-quarter measurement points is a sensible proxy for the filers most in need of deadline relief.
Failure scenario: The asset threshold misses issuers with thin resources but somewhat higher asset balances, or creates arbitrary cutoffs that shift timing burdens rather than matching actual reporting capacity.
Procedural implication: Commenters can ask for comparative analysis of asset, revenue, and public-float thresholds.

Claim: The proposal will help more companies go and stay public.
Underlying assumption: Disclosure burden is a meaningful marginal driver of IPO and public-market retention decisions for the affected registrants.
Failure scenario: Firms stay private for reasons mostly unrelated to filer-status obligations, so the proposal reduces investor information without materially improving public-market participation.
Procedural implication: Commenters can ask the Commission to distinguish between broad public-market trends and evidence tied to these specific accommodations.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

Observed Facts:
- The notice proposes to collapse Exchange Act reporting-company filer statuses into two primary categories: `large accelerated filers` and `non-accelerated filers`.
- The proposed LAF threshold is framed around a much higher public-float cut point, and the notice asks whether a proposed `$2 billion` threshold is appropriate.
- The proposal would expand the LAF seasoning period from `12 months` to `60 months`.
- The Commission would eliminate AF and SRC filer statuses as separate filer classifications while extending many existing SRC and certain EGC accommodations to all NAFs.
- The proposal would exempt NAFs from section `404(b)` ICFR auditor attestation, while management's own ICFR assessment would remain.
- The proposal would create a smallest-non-accelerated-filer subcategory using a `$35 million` total-assets threshold and would extend filing deadlines for that subcategory.
- The proposal would also update the SEC's small-entity definitions for RFA purposes.

Inference:
- This is a materially deregulatory securities-disclosure proposal aimed at lowering reporting and assurance burdens for a much larger share of public companies.
- The Commission is trying to re-center the heaviest periodic-reporting requirements on the largest and most seasoned public issuers, while treating many smaller registrants as needing more time, more scaled disclosure, and less audit-related cost.
- The proposal relies heavily on a capital-formation thesis: reducing compliance burden may encourage market entry and retention, but that thesis is not fully pinned down with issuer-specific causal evidence in the available text.

Evidence Quotes:
- "The Securities and Exchange Commission (“Commission”) proposes amendments to streamline filer statuses for Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (“Exchange Act”) reporting companies into two primary categories: large accelerated filers and non-accelerated filers."
- "Is the proposed LAF threshold of $2 billion in public float, which would capture approximately 93.5 percent of the total market public float and would result in approximately 20 percent of existing public companies being classified as LAFs, appropriate?"
- "The proposed amendments would both amend the threshold ... and expand the seasoning period to qualify as an LAF from 12 months to 60 months."

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

Regulatory objective:
- Make public-company reporting more workable for smaller and newer issuers while preserving investor protection through tailored rather than one-size-fits-all obligations.

Problem definition:
- The current filer-status framework is layered and complex.
- Public-company reporting burdens may contribute to fewer domestic public companies and discourage companies from entering or remaining in public markets.
- Section `404(b)` auditor attestation and accelerated disclosure timelines may impose especially high relative costs on smaller issuers.

Data used:
- Historical counts of domestic Exchange Act reporting companies.
- Public-float, asset, and filer-status estimates based on recent filing data.
- Research and GAO materials on `404(b)` compliance costs and benefits.
- Estimates of affected registrant counts and monetized benefits/costs.

Analytical method:
- Threshold-based redesign of filer categories.
- Quantified estimates where the Commission believes data are available.
- Qualitative discussion where it says public data are too limited for precise measurement.

Alternatives considered:
- Alternative LAF thresholds.
- Shorter or no LAF seasoning period.
- Alternative SNF thresholds and definitions.
- Different accommodations for SPACs and other issuers.

Agency conclusion:
- The revised framework would meaningfully reduce unnecessary burdens, encourage public-market participation, and still preserve appropriate investor protections.

Missing links:
- The notice does not fully connect the broad trend of fewer public companies to the specific filer-status changes proposed here.
- The economic analysis monetizes large benefits while acknowledging major data limitations, which makes the numerical confidence level hard to assess.
- The record does not fully explain why the chosen `$2 billion` / `60-month` structure is preferable to narrower intermediate options.

Unsupported premises:
- That most affected issuers will respond to burden relief in ways that measurably improve capital formation.
- That investors will not lose meaningful value from reduced attestation, reduced disclosure, or slower filings for the affected registrants.

Logical jumps:
- From evidence of compliance burden to a conclusion that this specific package of threshold, timing, and accommodation changes is the right calibration.
- From quantified benefits to near-zero annualized costs, despite acknowledged uncertainty and potential information-loss tradeoffs.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

1. The final rule could over-correct by shifting too many issuers out of accelerated treatment, reducing disclosure timeliness or assurance more than investors expect.
2. Issuers may voluntarily preserve some existing practices, making projected savings smaller than expected.
3. The small-filer asset threshold may produce arbitrary border effects for similarly situated companies just above and below the line.
4. Capital-formation benefits may prove modest if going-public decisions are driven more by market structure than reporting obligations.
5. Reduced attestation and scaled disclosure may create comparability issues across firms that are not fully discussed in the NPRM.
