# Phase 2 — Claim-Centered Analysis

## Step 0 — Extract Agency Reasoning Claims

1. **TIG is presented as an outdated safeguard.** OPM says the 52-week rule no longer serves the same purpose it once did and now blocks merit-based promotion decisions.
2. **Qualification standards and merit principles are said to do the real work.** The notice says current statutory protections, prohibited personnel practices, and qualification standards already constrain abusive promotions.
3. **Agency flexibility is the operational payoff.** OPM argues that removing TIG will let agencies promote qualified employees sooner and reduce administrative burdens tied to waiver approvals and training agreements.
4. **The rule is framed as consistent with skills-based hiring.** The proposal ties the change to current hiring philosophy and says promotions should depend on demonstrated ability, not elapsed time alone.
5. **The rule is sold as broadly workable.** OPM points to alternative pay systems, demonstration projects, and a China Lake example to suggest that rapid or excessive promotions are not a widespread problem.

**Bottom line:** the theory is coherent, but the record is stronger on policy framing than on proof that TIG removal will preserve merit in practice.

## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim: TIG is unnecessary to protect merit
**Assumption:** merit principles and qualification standards can catch the same problems TIG was meant to prevent.
**Failure scenario:** agencies may lose a uniform guardrail and rely on uneven local judgment instead.
**Procedural implication:** the agency needs a better explanation for why the replacement safeguards are enough.

### Claim: agencies will make better promotions decisions without TIG
**Assumption:** supervisors can reliably identify when an employee is ready for a higher grade.
**Failure scenario:** readiness judgments may vary by office, occupation, or manager, producing inconsistent promotion timing.
**Procedural implication:** the proposal should explain how OPM will keep discretion from becoming inconsistency.

### Claim: the change will improve recruitment and retention
**Assumption:** time-based promotion eligibility is a meaningful barrier in the current labor market.
**Failure scenario:** recruitment and retention may depend more on pay, mission, supervision, and local staffing than on the TIG rule alone.
**Procedural implication:** the market-effect claim needs more than broad assertion.

### Claim: rapid promotions are not likely to become widespread
**Assumption:** existing systems and agency practices will continue to constrain overpromotion.
**Failure scenario:** the notice does not quantify how often TIG currently prevents abuse or how quickly agencies might tighten standards after repeal.
**Procedural implication:** the anti-abuse assurance reads as plausible but under-supported.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

**Regulatory Action:** Eliminate the 52-week time-in-grade restriction for competitive service General Schedule promotions.

**Affected Entities**
- Competitive service GS employees at grades 5 and above
- OPM and agency staffing offices
- Managers making promotion decisions
- Employees seeking promotions or career-ladder movement

**Statutory Authority:** 5 U.S.C. 3301, 3302, and E.O. 10577; conforming amendments in parts 315, 316, 352, and 410.

**Key Regulatory Change:** Remove part 300 subpart F and conform related cross-references so TIG is no longer a prerequisite for promotion.

**Observed Facts**
- The notice says TIG was originally adopted to prevent excessively rapid promotions.
- The proposal says qualification standards, merit principles, and prohibited personnel practices remain in place.
- The proposal cites prior TIG repeal efforts, a China Lake demonstration project, and labor-market pressures.

**Inference:** The practical shift is from a single governmentwide time gate to agency-specific judgment tied to qualification standards and job-related criteria.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Summary:**
OPM's logic is: the old TIG rule is an outdated time gate, current merit protections are enough to stop abuse, and agencies should regain flexibility to promote qualified employees sooner.

**Nodes:**
- `n1` Problem claim: TIG is an outdated non-merit gate.
- `n2` Legal premise: OPM has authority to revise competitive-service regulations and the Whitten Amendment no longer supplies the old policy basis.
- `n3` Safeguard premise: merit system principles, qualification standards, and prohibited personnel practices already protect against abuse.
- `n4` Operational premise: agencies can add job-related promotion criteria and still manage promotions responsibly.
- `n5` Implementation premise: conforming changes in parts 315, 316, 352, and 410 can keep the system aligned.
- `n6` Conclusion: eliminate TIG as a prerequisite for promotion.

**Edges:**
- `n1 -> n6` because the alleged problem is the reason for repeal.
- `n2 -> n6` because authority is the legal basis for the change.
- `n3 -> n6` because the agency says the replacement safeguards are sufficient.
- `n4 -> n6` because local job-related criteria are supposed to replace the fixed time rule.
- `n5 -> n6` because conforming edits are needed to make repeal operational.

**Missing links:**
- No quantified estimate of how often TIG blocks otherwise qualified employees.
- No side-by-side comparison of promotion outcomes with and without TIG across occupations or agencies.
- No worked example showing how a specific agency would replace TIG with a consistent internal standard.

**Logical jumps:**
- The notice moves from "merit principles exist" to "TIG is unnecessary" without a comparative performance record.
- The notice treats flexibility as a proxy for better merit, but the causal chain is asserted more than demonstrated.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

**Alternatives that deserve more analysis:**
- A phased repeal with pilot agencies or pilot occupations.
- A targeted repeal only for specified grades, occupations, or promotion tracks.
- A waiver or safe-harbor framework for agencies that want to keep a time-based screen.
- A governmentwide transition guide showing how to replace TIG with calibration, documentation, and review steps.

**What the notice does instead:**
- It says agencies may keep their own internal requirements, but it does not map out how those choices would be standardized or audited.
- It treats broad flexibility as the solution, without comparing that approach to narrower or staged options.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

1. **Inconsistent local standards.** Agencies may replace one governmentwide time rule with many different local promotion thresholds.
2. **Informal gatekeeping.** If TIG disappears, promotion decisions may move into less visible supervisory judgments that are harder to audit.
3. **Grade inflation or faster progression.** Agencies may feel pressure to promote sooner without a uniform time-based check.
4. **Documentation drift.** The record may become harder to reconstruct if each agency designs its own job-related criteria without shared templates or examples.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Agency incentives**
- More freedom to promote employees when managers think they are ready.
- Less paperwork tied to TIG approvals and training agreements.
- More responsibility for agencies to design internal standards that actually protect merit.

**Manager incentives**
- Faster promotion decisions and more discretion.
- Greater pressure to defend subjective judgments about readiness.

**Employee incentives**
- A shorter path to promotion for employees already performing above grade.
- Less certainty that a uniform time requirement will apply across offices and occupations.

**System incentive**
- The rule encourages a shift from a uniform governmentwide gate to locally managed calibration.

**Bottom line**
