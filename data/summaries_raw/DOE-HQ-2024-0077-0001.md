The NPRM itself cites statutes, DOE Order 522.1A, and various existing regulations, but the export does not include a separate regulatory impact analysis, technical support document, or memorandum explaining the proposed 1 percent Federal Administrative Charge or the expected $4 million annual cost reduction.

**Inference**
This is a workable record for reviewing the public-facing proposal, but it is thin as an analytical record. The notice is not empty; it includes section-by-section explanations and legal citations. Still, the proposal makes several policy and cost claims without attaching deeper support in the available docket materials.

**Why that matters**
The strongest comment leverage is not that DOE lacks statutory authority to revise part 1009. It is narrower:

- whether DOE has adequately explained why a 1 percent Federal Administrative Charge is the right public-facing rate rather than simply a deregulatory policy choice carried over from internal policy;
- whether the expanded exclusions and special-pricing carveouts are explained with enough criteria to let the public evaluate future application; and
- whether the rule's transparency claims are supported by operational changes that would actually make prices and charging practices easier for outside parties to understand.

## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Major claim: the 1980 rule is outdated enough to justify a broad rewrite

**Underlying assumption**
Post-1980 legal and programmatic changes are extensive enough that a broad modernization, not targeted amendments, is warranted.

**Failure scenario**
If many proposed changes merely restate already-existing DOE practice without showing where the old regulation caused public confusion or pricing error, the rewrite may be harder to evaluate because it mixes clarification with substantive policy choices.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask DOE to identify which revisions are purely conforming, which are policy changes, and where the prior text created actual administrative problems.

### Major claim: lowering the Federal Administrative Charge from 3 percent to 1 percent is justified

**Underlying assumption**
The 1 percent rate still recovers appropriate federal overhead while better matching DOE policy goals and reducing unnecessary cost burdens.

**Failure scenario**
If the 1 percent rate is driven mainly by internal deregulatory policy rather than a public explanation of actual overhead recovery needs, the public record may not show why 1 percent is the right rate rather than simply a preferred lower rate.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask DOE to provide the calculation basis for the 1 percent rate, including whether it reflects measured administrative costs, a policy judgment, or both.

### Major claim: the expanded exclusions improve transparency

**Underlying assumption**
A longer and more explicit exclusion list makes it easier for outside parties to understand when part 1009 does and does not apply.

**Failure scenario**
A larger list can also make the rule harder to navigate if DOE does not explain how complete the list is, how future exclusions will be identified, or how borderline cases should be resolved.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask DOE to provide criteria or a decision framework for classifying activities as excluded rather than relying on a static list alone.

### Major claim: special-pricing activities are appropriate because statutes allow flexibility

**Underlying assumption**
The cited statutes give DOE enough discretion that negotiated, reduced-cost, or no-fee pricing can be codified without additional guardrails.

**Failure scenario**
If the rule gives broad discretion but little explanation of how that discretion will be exercised consistently, similarly situated outside parties may face unpredictable pricing outcomes.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask DOE to explain how it will document, publish, or review special-pricing decisions to avoid arbitrary application.

### Major claim: the proposal meaningfully enhances public transparency

**Underlying assumption**
Outside parties mainly need a clearer rule text and statutory cross-references, not centralized publication of actual prices or pricing methods.

**Failure scenario**
If most prices remain facility-specific, unpublished, or only estimable on request, then the rule may clarify legal categories while doing less to improve practical price transparency.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask DOE whether it should add publication, reporting, or website-listing commitments for recurring charges and exemptions.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

**Observed Facts**

- The proposal would revise 10 CFR part 1009, DOE's general pricing regulation for materials, goods, and services sold outside the Federal Government.
- DOE proposes updated definitions, a revised full-cost policy, a new 1 percent Federal Administrative Charge provision, an expanded exclusions list, and a new special-pricing-activities section.
- The NPRM states that the rule has not been updated since 1980 and needs to reflect later statutes, Executive orders, regulations, and program changes.
- The proposal says the Federal Administrative Charge for non-federal reimbursable work is scheduled to fall from 3 percent to 1 percent beginning October 1, 2025.
- The proposal estimates that lowering that charge would reduce aggregate costs for non-federal entities by about $4 million per year based on 2022 work volumes.

**Inference**

- This is a governance-and-pricing NPRM rather than a narrow editorial cleanup. It would affect who pays full cost, which transactions are excluded, when DOE can negotiate or waive charges, and how outside users interact with DOE facilities and services.
- The central analytical question is not whether DOE can modernize part 1009 at all. It is whether DOE has provided enough public explanation for the most consequential pricing choices embedded in that modernization, especially the lower administrative charge and the breadth of the exclusions and special-pricing carveouts.

**Evidence Quotes**

> "The proposed rule will provide greater clarity and enhance transparency by providing a more comprehensive list of prices and charges not governed by part 1009."

> "The Federal Administrative Charge paid by non-Federal entities is scheduled to fall from 3 percent to 1 percent on October 1, 2025..."

> "The reduction in the Federal Administrative Charge is estimated to reduce aggregate costs for non-Federal entities by approximately $4 million per year..."

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**
Modernize DOE's general pricing regulation so it reflects current statutes, current DOE practice, and DOE's view of appropriate public-facing pricing rules for non-federal transactions.

**Problem definition**
DOE says the 1980 rule is outdated, does not reflect later legal developments, and does not clearly identify exclusions, special-pricing activities, or current pricing concepts such as the statutory definition of full cost.

**Data used**

- statutory citations enacted after 1980;
- DOE's internal pricing policy, including DOE Order 522.1A;
- a 2022 non-federal reimbursable-work volume estimate used for the $4 million savings figure; and
- qualitative descriptions of existing DOE and NNSA pricing practices.

**Analytical method**

- align regulatory text with cited statutes and existing DOE practice;
- codify a lower Federal Administrative Charge for non-federal reimbursable work;
- reorganize exceptions into exclusions and special-pricing activities; and
- rely on qualitative legal and policy explanation with one quantified savings estimate.

**Alternatives considered**

- no explicit alternative rate for the Federal Administrative Charge is discussed in the available NPRM text;
- no explicit alternative structures for the exclusions list or special-pricing section are meaningfully compared in the available NPRM text;
- the proposal generally presents each change as the needed update.

**Agency conclusion**
DOE proposes a revised part 1009 that sets the default rule at full cost, lowers the Federal Administrative Charge to 1 percent for covered non-federal reimbursable work, lists numerous exclusions, and codifies several special-pricing activities.

**Missing links / logical jumps**

- The NPRM gives a quantified savings estimate for the 1 percent charge but does not explain the underlying calculation or why 1 percent, rather than another rate up to the statutory 3 percent cap, is the correct public-facing choice.
- The notice says the exclusions list improves transparency, but it does not explain whether the list is exhaustive, how future exclusions will be handled, or what principles distinguish exclusions from special-pricing activities.
- The rule repeatedly describes certain practices as current DOE practice, but it does not consistently explain why those practices should now be codified in the precise form proposed.
- The proposal centralizes many legal cross-references while leaving actual pricing dissemination decentralized at the facility or office level, which weakens the transparency rationale unless further operational disclosure is added.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

**Alternatives evaluated**

- update the rule as proposed with a 1 percent Federal Administrative Charge and expanded exclusions;
- maintain default full-cost recovery while carving out specific categories.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed**

- retain a higher administrative charge, such as 2 percent or case-specific rates, while publishing the cost basis for any reduction;
- phase in the reduction from 3 percent to 1 percent rather than adopting the lower rate across covered non-federal reimbursable work at once;
- add a criteria-based appendix or decision tree for exclusions and special-pricing activities;
- require centralized public posting of recurring prices, common charges, or exemption determinations;
- require periodic reporting on how often DOE uses discretionary exemptions or negotiated pricing arrangements.

**Evidence suggesting feasibility**

- DOE already distinguishes between exclusions, special-pricing activities, and default full-cost transactions, so a criteria-based framework is conceptually available.
- DOE already relies on internal policy and facility-level implementation, suggesting it has existing operational data that could support a more explicit public justification for the 1 percent rate.
- Proposed Sec. 1009.6 already identifies dissemination channels, which means additional publication obligations could have been considered without changing the rule's core structure.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

The proposal is internally coherent at a high level: it defines full cost, moves many exceptions into explicit categories, and uses separate sections for exclusions and special-pricing activities.

The weaker point is coherence between the rule's stated transparency goal and its operational design.

**Observed Fact**
The NPRM says the rule will enhance transparency, but proposed Sec. 1009.6 still says pricing information is available from the facility or office providing the material, good, or service, and that DOE often can provide only an estimate rather than a fixed price.

**Inference**
The proposal appears stronger on legal taxonomy than on practical transparency. It may make the categories of pricing more legible while leaving outside parties to navigate decentralized, estimate-based pricing in much the same way as before.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

### Failure mode 1: underexplained rate reduction

DOE could finalize the 1 percent Federal Administrative Charge without showing why that rate accurately reflects recoverable federal overhead or why a different rate would be inferior.

### Failure mode 2: opaque boundary lines between exclusions and special pricing

If the rule does not explain how borderline activities are classified, outside parties may struggle to predict which pricing framework applies.

### Failure mode 3: uneven discretionary application

Broad references to negotiated pricing, laboratory approval, or retained exemptions could produce inconsistent pricing outcomes if DOE does not document the governing criteria.

### Failure mode 4: transparency gains remain mostly formal

If pricing information remains decentralized and often estimate-based, the claimed transparency improvement may be more legal than practical.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

The proposal creates several incentives worth watching:

- **Incentive for non-federal users:** lower administrative charges may increase demand for reimbursable work or outside use of DOE capabilities.
- **Incentive for DOE program offices and facilities:** classification decisions about exclusions, special pricing, and incremental costs may materially affect the price charged to outside parties.
- **Incentive for laboratories:** the distinction between non-proprietary and proprietary research, and between NNSA and non-NNSA facilities, creates incentives to characterize work in ways that affect pricing.
- **Incentive for DOE headquarters:** a lower published administrative charge may advance deregulatory goals even if the public record does not fully demonstrate the cost basis.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

### 1. Ask DOE to justify the 1 percent rate with actual cost methodology

The most concrete economic change in the NPRM is the lower Federal Administrative Charge. A useful comment can ask DOE to publish the cost basis, assumptions, and alternatives considered rather than treating the lower rate as self-justifying.

### 2. Press DOE to convert formal transparency into practical transparency

The NPRM improves the legal map of exclusions and special pricing, but commenters can ask DOE to add website publication, annual reporting, or decision criteria so outside users can actually predict pricing outcomes.

### 3. Ask for clearer guardrails on discretionary pricing

Negotiated fees, facility-manager discretion, and CFO clarification authority may be reasonable, but commenters can ask DOE to define the standards and documentation practices that will prevent inconsistent application.

### 4. Ask DOE to explain the exclusion framework, not just the exclusion list

Instead of commenting on isolated subsections only, commenters can ask for a principle-based explanation of how DOE decides whether an activity belongs in the default full-cost rule, an exclusion, or a special-pricing carveout.

## Step 18 — Decision Brief

### Leverage point 1: DOE has not fully explained why 1 percent is the correct Federal Administrative Charge

**Key sentences from the NPRM**

> "The Federal Administrative Charge paid by non-Federal entities is scheduled to fall from 3 percent to 1 percent on October 1, 2025..."

> "The reduction in the Federal Administrative Charge is estimated to reduce aggregate costs for non-Federal entities by approximately $4 million per year..."

**Routine description situated in context**
DOE clearly has authority to charge up to the statutory cap and to revise its regulation. The public-facing weakness is narrower: the notice states the new rate and the resulting savings without showing how DOE determined that 1 percent, specifically, is the appropriate rate.

**Regulatory effectiveness, statutory compliance, procedural adequacy**
This is mainly a procedural-adequacy and reasoned-explanation issue, not an obvious authority defect. A stronger record would explain the methodology behind the selected rate and why alternatives were rejected.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**
If commenters press this point effectively, DOE may need to disclose the analytical basis for the 1 percent rate, adjust the rate, or provide a more detailed justification before finalizing.

**Worth a comment?** High

**Comment posture:** evidence request

### Leverage point 2: the proposal promises transparency, but the operational transparency gain is underdeveloped

**Key sentences from the NPRM**

> "The proposed rule will provide greater clarity and enhance transparency by providing a more comprehensive list of prices and charges not governed by part 1009."

> "Pricing information for specific materials, goods and services is available from the DOE facility or office providing the material, good, or service..."

**Routine description situated in context**
The NPRM clearly improves the legal structure of part 1009. But outside users may still need to contact individual offices and often receive estimates rather than fixed prices. That creates a gap between formal regulatory clarity and practical transparency.

**Regulatory effectiveness, statutory compliance, procedural adequacy**
The point is not that DOE must publish every price. It is that DOE's stated rationale would be stronger if the final rule included operational transparency measures that match the rhetoric of increased clarity.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**
Constructive comments could push DOE toward centralized publication, recurring charge schedules, guidance documents, or annual reports on exclusions and special-pricing use.

**Worth a comment?** High

**Comment posture:** improvement request

### Leverage point 3: discretionary special-pricing provisions need clearer public guardrails

**Key sentences from the NPRM**

> "Certain statutes provide DOE leeway to charge less than full cost recovery or negotiate the pricing for items without more specific pricing instructions."

> "At the discretion of the facility manager, a user engaged in non-proprietary research at a user facility should be charged for incremental costs incurred over and above normal facility costs..."

**Routine description situated in context**
DOE can reasonably preserve flexibility where statutes allow it. But negotiated pricing and discretionary charges can be difficult for outside parties to predict unless the rule or record explains the governing standards.

**Regulatory effectiveness, statutory compliance, procedural adequacy**
This is mainly an implementation-clarity issue. Better guardrails would improve consistency and reduce the risk that similarly situated outside users are treated differently without a visible basis.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**
If commenters focus here, DOE may clarify the factors that govern negotiated fees, incremental charges, and exemptions, which would improve public predictability even if the basic structure remains unchanged.

**Worth a comment?** Medium to high

**Comment posture:** criteria request
