## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim A

**Claim**
Prescribed-fire fireline duties expose employees to hazards sufficiently similar to wildfire fireline duties to justify the same 25 percent differential.

**Underlying assumption**
The relevant hazard comparison is based on active-fire exposure, not on broader program purpose or the fact that prescribed fire is planned rather than emergency response.

**Failure scenario**
If exposure intensity, duration, or controllability is materially lower for many prescribed-fire assignments than for wildfire fireline assignments, the proposal may overgeneralize from the riskiest prescribed-fire examples.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask for a clearer comparison framework showing why parity at 25 percent is appropriate rather than a lower rate, narrower trigger, or phased approach.

### Claim B

**Claim**
Existing mitigation practices cannot reduce prescribed-fire hazards below a significant level.

**Underlying assumption**
Administrative controls, PPE, planning, and contingency actions meaningfully reduce risk but do not sufficiently change the residual hazard for fireline workers.

**Failure scenario**
If some prescribed-fire settings are highly controlled and consistently lower-risk, the rule may need finer distinctions by assignment, location, or operational phase.

**Procedural implication**
The record would be stronger with more explicit discussion of when the residual risk is high enough to warrant the differential and when it is not.

### Claim C

**Claim**
Excluding “preparation” activities while covering implementation and control is a workable line.

**Underlying assumption**
Agencies can distinguish preparation from active-fireline work consistently in payroll administration.

**Failure scenario**
Crew assignments often blend preparation, ignition, patrol, mop-up, and snag-felling duties within the same operational period, creating classification disputes.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can press OPM to clarify edge cases and payroll triggers to reduce inconsistent application across agencies.

### Claim D

**Claim**
The cost estimates are modest enough that the proposal imposes no more than de minimis costs.

**Underlying assumption**
Agency hour estimates, participation rates, and burn targets are reasonably accurate.

**Failure scenario**
If burn activity scales up materially, or if eligible hours are broader than expected, agency cost exposure could exceed the simple FY2026 estimates.

**Procedural implication**
The cost discussion would benefit from sensitivity ranges and clearer assumptions, especially because the rule itself says prescribed-fire activity is expected to grow over time.

### Claim E

**Claim**
FPRAC consensus and USDA/DOI submissions sufficiently support the FWS portion of the proposal.

**Underlying assumption**
Consensus committee support reflects adequate vetting of the risk basis and payment design.

**Failure scenario**
If the committee considered only a high-level narrative and not underlying data, the consensus label may overstate the depth of scrutiny.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask OPM to summarize the data and reasoning presented to FPRAC, not just the fact of committee agreement.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

* OPM proposes to add a new hazardous-duty / environmental-differential category for prescribed wildland fire activities.
* The proposal would authorize a **25 percent differential** for eligible GS and FWS employees.
* Coverage is limited to employees “participating as a member of a firefighting crew” and engaged in activities on the fireline directly involving implementation and control of prescribed fires.
* OPM also proposes to amend `5 CFR 550.904` to clarify that GS-0456 wildland firefighters may receive hazardous duty pay for this work notwithstanding classification rules.
* The rule text estimates affected employees at roughly **10,000 GS** and **2,500 FWS** employees.
* OPM reports estimated FY2026 costs of **$20 million for USDA** and **$12.5 million for DOI**.

### Inference

The rule is best understood as a parity proposal: OPM is trying to treat active prescribed-fire fireline work more like existing wildfire fireline work for pay-differential purposes, while still drawing a narrower boundary around covered duties than the agencies originally requested.

### Evidence Quotes

> “This rulemaking would authorize a 25 percent differential.”

> “The Committee recommended this change by consensus.”

> “The language approved by OPM in this proposed regulation replaces ‘related to’ with ‘directly involves’ and does not include activities of ‘preparation’ for a prescribed fire...”

> “OPM expects this rulemaking to impact approximately 10,000 GS employees and 2,500 FWS employees at USDA and DOI.”

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

### Alternatives actually visible in the record

* OPM narrowed the agencies' requested wording by excluding “preparation” activities.
* OPM clarified that coverage is tied to work directly involving implementation and control of a prescribed fire on the fireline.

### Plausible alternatives not visibly analyzed

* A lower differential rate than 25 percent.
* Coverage limited to certain operational phases or hazard conditions.
* A pilot or sunset approach with post-implementation review.
* More explicit eligibility criteria keyed to hours, assignment codes, or incident status.

### Why this matters

Because the rule is not rejecting the concept of differential pay, the most useful public comments are less about whether to compensate hazardous work at all and more about whether OPM chose the right scope, rate, and administrative trigger.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

### Inference

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

### Observed Fact

### Inference

### Unknown

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

* Agencies apply the covered-duty standard differently across units, causing pay inequities.
* Employees performing mixed preparation / ignition / patrol assignments face inconsistent eligibility decisions.
* Cost estimates understate future obligations if prescribed-fire operations expand.
* The final rule relies too heavily on high-level incident counts without clarifying the comparative logic behind the 25 percent rate.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

### Observed Fact

The rule increases compensation for qualifying prescribed-fire duties and may affect agencies with large prescribed-fire programs.

### Inference

The proposal creates incentives to classify tasks as covered fireline implementation/control work. That is not inherently improper, but it raises a practical need for precise eligibility guidance to avoid over- or under-payment.

### Weaker parts of the record

* No robust discussion of alternative rates or narrower triggers.

* Limited administrative detail on how agencies will decide whether an employee's work “directly involves” implementation and control.

## Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

### Gap 1

Provide a clearer side-by-side comparison between wildfire fireline duties already receiving a 25 percent differential and the specific prescribed-fire duties proposed for equivalent treatment.

### Gap 2

Publish or summarize the main assumptions behind the USDA and DOI cost estimates, including hours, participation rates, and expected growth in prescribed-fire activity.

### Gap 3

Clarify eligibility edge cases for mixed assignments, especially where a work period includes both preparation and active-fire implementation/control duties.

### Gap 4

Explain whether OPM considered lower-rate, phased, or more task-specific alternatives and why those options were rejected.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

### Potential unacknowledged weakness

The strongest possible challenge from the available record would be that OPM has shown hazards exist but has only partially explained why the selected **scope** and **25 percent rate** are the right regulatory choices.

### Why this is not stronger

* OPM does have authority to set these categories.
* The record is not empty; it includes a meaningful supporting attachment and concrete incident references.
* The notice gives a real explanation for narrowing the requested scope by excluding preparation activities.

### Practical takeaway

The most constructive comments are likely to target comparative justification, rate selection, cost assumptions, and administrative clarity rather than arguing the entire proposal lacks any basis.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

### Angle 1 — Comparative justification for the 25 percent rate

Commenters can accept that prescribed-fire duties are hazardous while still asking OPM to show more explicitly why the existing wildfire 25 percent benchmark is the right comparator.

### Angle 2 — Scope boundary between preparation and covered fireline work

Commenters can press OPM to define the operational trigger with enough clarity that payroll treatment does not vary unpredictably across agencies and field units.

### Angle 3 — Cost and growth assumptions

Commenters can ask OPM to show sensitivity ranges, because the notice itself says prescribed-fire use is expected to continue and likely grow.

### Angle 4 — Administrative implementation and auditability

Commenters can ask what records, coding practices, or supervisory certifications will support consistent eligibility decisions.

## Step 18 — Decision Brief

### Leverage Point 1 — Rate selection

**Context:** OPM proposes a 25 percent differential for prescribed-fire implementation and control duties on the fireline.

**Procedural adequacy:** **Partially shown.** The record explains hazard similarity but gives only a limited public explanation for why the correct rate is 25 percent specifically.

**Real-world consequence:** If the rate is set too broadly or without a clear comparative basis, the final rule may be vulnerable to criticism for overgeneralization.

**Worth a comment?:** **Yes.** This is one of the highest-leverage places to request more analysis.

### Leverage Point 2 — Exclusion of preparation activities

**Context:** OPM explicitly removed “preparation” from the agencies' earlier proposed wording.

**Procedural adequacy:** **Moderately shown.** OPM explains that the hazards justifying coverage are associated with management of an active fire and its aftermath, not pre-ignition preparation.

**Real-world consequence:** Without added examples or payroll guidance, mixed-duty assignments may generate inconsistent application.

**Worth a comment?:** **Yes.** This is a practical implementation issue likely to matter immediately if the rule is finalized.

### Leverage Point 3 — Cost estimates and participation assumptions

**Context:** OPM reports roughly $20 million in USDA FY2026 costs and $12.5 million in DOI FY2026 costs.

**Procedural adequacy:** **Partially shown.** The notice provides some assumptions, but not the full analytical basis or sensitivity analysis.

**Real-world consequence:** Stakeholders may have difficulty assessing fiscal impacts if prescribed-fire activity expands or if eligible hours are broader than expected.

**Worth a comment?:** **Yes.** This is a straightforward request for analytical transparency.

### Leverage Point 4 — Administrative proof of eligibility

**Context:** The notice says employing agencies will determine whether employees meet the criteria for the prescribed-fire differentials.

**Procedural adequacy:** **Only partially shown.** The notice does not provide much detail on documentation, coding, or audit mechanisms.

**Real-world consequence:** Ambiguity here could create disputes across crews, agencies, and duty periods.

**Worth a comment?:** **Yes.** Clearer implementation standards would strengthen the final rule and reduce inconsistent treatment.
