## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

**P1 — Threat prevalence.** Underlying assumption: observed device compromises are sufficiently common and tied to commercial supply chains to justify general intervention. Negation test: isolated incidents would weaken the case for market-wide requirements, while still supporting targeted investigation. Counterfactual: a low-cost independent device could be free of malware; a licensed product could later be compromised. Request representative samples, firmware versions, acquisition channels, and reproducible indicators rather than equating price or certification status with infection.

**P2 — Remedy effectiveness.** Underlying assumption: boot verifiability and DRM integrity address covert proxy operation. Negation test: if proxy software can run through legitimate signed components, those requirements alone may not solve the harm. Counterfactual: legitimate user-authorized proxy services or independently maintained firmware could trigger broad capability-based language. Adversarial interpretation: a vendor might invoke one-time opt-in while obscuring continuing bandwidth use or third-party traffic. These are technical hypotheses to test, not established exploits or present obligations.

**P3 — Need for a new rule.** Underlying assumption: existing tools and narrower duties are insufficient. No enforcement comparison or alternatives assessment is supplied. The procedural implication is a request for evidence before deciding whether to initiate rulemaking; it is not a finding that the FTC has failed a proposal-stage obligation.
