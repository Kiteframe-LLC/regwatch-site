## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim: Food-delivery fees and markups commonly harm consumers through late or incomplete disclosure
- **Underlying assumption:** The cited studies, complaints, and enforcement matters are representative of current platform practices rather than isolated platform-specific episodes.
- **Failure scenario:** If the most severe examples come from a few firms, old practices, or atypical order configurations, a broad trade regulation rule could sweep beyond the best-supported conduct.
- **Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask the FTC to distinguish widespread conduct from platform-specific episodes and to specify which disclosures or fee practices are most consistently harmful.

### Claim: Consumers do not adequately understand markups, service fees, and other mandatory charges before purchase
- **Underlying assumption:** Existing interface design and disclosures leave reasonable consumers unable to estimate total price or compare options effectively.
- **Failure scenario:** If platforms can show that consumers usually see fee information early enough or that shopping behavior already incorporates those costs, the Commission would need a more targeted theory of deception.
- **Procedural implication:** The later NPRM will need clear evidence on timing, placement, and salience of disclosures, not just examples of high prices.

### Claim: Sector-specific regulation may be appropriate because delivery platforms act as intermediaries and use variable pricing
- **Underlying assumption:** General total-price or unfair-fee principles are not sufficient by themselves to address the operational realities of online food and grocery delivery.
- **Failure scenario:** If the same consumer-protection concerns can be solved through general mandatory-fee disclosure standards, a special rule for delivery platforms could add complexity without clear necessity.
- **Procedural implication:** Commenters can push the FTC to explain what platform-specific features require bespoke treatment and what general rules should still apply.

### Claim: State and local laws provide a workable analogue for Federal rule design
- **Underlying assumption:** The cited State approaches are effective enough, administrable enough, and similar enough to national platform operations to inform a Federal rule.
- **Failure scenario:** If State laws materially differ in scope, definitions, enforcement structures, or treatment of variable fees, analogizing from them without careful comparison could be misleading.
- **Procedural implication:** A stronger record would compare which State-law elements actually worked and which produced compliance uncertainty.

### Claim: The Commission can translate prior enforcement actions into a broader rulemaking standard
- **Underlying assumption:** The Grubhub, Instacart, Amazon, Walmart, DoorDash, and D.C. matters reveal recurring patterns rather than one-off fact patterns tied to particular representations.
- **Failure scenario:** If those matters turned on unusually specific representations, subscriptions, or tipping mechanics, a general rule may need narrower elements than the ANPRM currently implies.
- **Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask the FTC to identify which factual predicates are essential to the theory and which are only illustrative.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

**Observed Facts**
- This document is an ANPRM, not a proposed rule with draft regulatory text.
- The FTC is considering whether to adopt a trade regulation rule addressing allegedly unfair or deceptive fee and pricing practices on online food and grocery delivery platforms.
- The notice focuses on several recurring practices: item-price markups relative to in-store or restaurant prices, mandatory fees disclosed late in the checkout flow, "free delivery" or "$0 delivery" claims paired with other required charges, confusion about the nature and purpose of service fees, and tip representations that may mislead consumers about worker compensation.
- The Commission grounds the inquiry in Section 18 of the FTC Act and references prior FTC enforcement actions, State and local laws, and private or public litigation involving platform fee practices.
- The notice repeatedly asks for public input on prevalence, consumer understanding, platform operations, disclosure timing, and possible regulatory alternatives.

**Evidence quotes**
- "The Commission proposes to commence a rulemaking proceeding to address certain unfair or deceptive acts or practices relating to fees and charges for food and grocery items ordered through online delivery platforms."
- "Often, the existence and cost of these fees are not disclosed until checkout, if at all, meaning that consumers frequently do not learn the true cost of getting their food delivered until the very end of the transaction."
- "Many food and grocery delivery platforms currently charge variable fees, and the total price of a transaction typically varies based on a consumer's selections, as well as on other factors such as the size of an order or the distance and time it will take to deliver the order."

**Inference**
- The main policy question is not whether food delivery is expensive. It is whether the FTC can define a workable Federal transparency baseline for a market where both the menu price and the delivery-related charges may vary by seller, platform, order size, location, timing, and subscription status.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

- **Alternatives evaluated:**
  - Require upfront disclosure of mandatory fees.
  - Require itemized cost breakdowns before checkout.
  - Address "free delivery" or nominal-fee advertising that omits other required charges.
  - Apply special rules to variable-fee or price-variable transactions.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- The FTC could write a future rule that is too broad, sweeping in pricing differences or variable delivery charges that are not inherently deceptive when disclosed clearly.
- A rule could be too narrow if it targets only nominal "delivery fees" while platforms shift revenue collection into differently named mandatory charges.
- The record could overemphasize consumer-facing disclosure while underanalyzing effects on restaurants, grocers, drivers, or subscription pricing structures.
- If the Commission does not distinguish among marketplace, grocery, and restaurant-delivery models, the final rule could apply awkwardly across materially different transaction types.
- If variable-fee disclosures are required without workable safe harbors, platforms may argue that compliance is uncertain whenever time, distance, or basket contents shift during checkout.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

- Stronger upfront disclosure rules could reduce incentives to advertise artificially low entry prices and then recover revenue through later mandatory charges.
- If the rule focuses only on named fees, platforms may relabel charges or shift more revenue into marked-up item prices.
- A well-designed itemization rule could improve comparison shopping across platforms, but it could also encourage platforms to restructure pricing in harder-to-compare ways unless total-price presentation is addressed too.
- If tipping representations are regulated more tightly, platforms may need clearer separation between optional gratuities and mandatory delivery-related compensation charges.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

- At the ANPRM stage, immediate litigation vulnerability is low because the document is exploratory and does not itself impose obligations.
- The larger future risk is line-drawing: if the FTC later adopts categorical disclosure or advertising rules without showing why those thresholds fit variable-price delivery transactions, the rule could face arbitrary-and-capricious challenges.
- Another future risk is overgeneralization from settlements and complaints. If the rule relies heavily on fact-specific enforcement matters without demonstrating broader market prevalence, challengers could argue that the Commission moved from anecdote to industry-wide regulation too quickly.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

1. Ask the FTC to separate item-price markup issues from hidden-fee issues and explain whether both belong in one rule or require different legal and disclosure treatment.
2. Ask the FTC to define a staged disclosure framework for variable-fee transactions, identifying what must be shown at each point in the ordering process rather than using only a vague "upfront" standard.
3. Ask the FTC to compare State-law models explicitly and identify which features it views as effective, especially for maximum-fee disclosures, itemized breakdowns, and "free delivery" advertising.
4. Ask the FTC to distinguish among third-party marketplace delivery, first-party merchant delivery, grocery-delivery intermediaries, and subscription-linked ordering because the pricing architecture may differ materially across those models.
5. Ask the FTC to publish or summarize complaint and prevalence data so the eventual NPRM can target recurring market practices rather than a collection of notable enforcement examples.

## Step 18 — Decision Brief

### Leverage Point 1: The FTC should define a stage-based disclosure architecture for variable pricing
- **Key sentence from the notice:** "Many food and grocery delivery platforms currently charge variable fees, and the total price of a transaction typically varies based on a consumer's selections, as well as on other factors such as the size of an order or the distance and time it will take to deliver the order."
- **Routine description situated in context:** The ANPRM already recognizes that some charges vary. That makes it especially important to define what information must appear at search, item-selection, cart, and checkout stages.
- **Regulatory effectiveness, statutory compliance, procedural adequacy:** A staged approach would help the FTC avoid writing a rule that sounds simple but proves operationally indeterminate.
- **Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage:** Better comments here could shape a future safe-harbor disclosure structure that is easier to enforce and harder to evade through interface design.
- **Worth a comment?** High

### Leverage Point 2: The record should distinguish item-price markups from platform fee practices
- **Key sentence from the notice:** "Perhaps most importantly, consumers are not always made aware that prices are marked up or that they would need to parse through disclosures to understand the various ways in which prices may vary between a restaurant or grocery store and a delivery platform."
- **Routine description situated in context:** The ANPRM treats markups and fees as related transparency problems, but they are not the same thing. One concerns the underlying item price; the other concerns additional charges layered on top.
- **Regulatory effectiveness, statutory compliance, procedural adequacy:** Asking the FTC to separate these concepts can sharpen the eventual legal theory and reduce the risk of an overbroad or confusing proposal.
- **Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage:** A more precise rule could improve transparency without forcing awkward one-size-fits-all disclosures across different pricing components.
- **Worth a comment?** High

### Leverage Point 3: The FTC should show why any platform-specific rule is preferable to a general mandatory-fee framework
- **Key sentence from the notice:** "Despite slight variations across jurisdictions, these approaches highlight that tailored pricing and disclosure requirements may be appropriate for third-party food and grocery delivery platforms given that such platforms typically serve as intermediaries between consumers and the primary seller or maker of the ordered goods."

- **Regulatory effectiveness, statutory compliance, procedural adequacy:** This is a constructive request for clearer problem definition, not a rejection of the rulemaking.
- **Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage:** A stronger record on platform-specific necessity could make the eventual rule both narrower and more defensible.
- **Worth a comment?** High
