## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Major claim: Colorado already meets federal Class VI primacy requirements

**Underlying assumption**

EPA's legal and technical review was sufficiently searching to support an approval proposal rather than merely acknowledging that Colorado filed a complete package.

**Failure scenario**

If EPA's “comprehensive evaluation” relies too heavily on high-level equivalency statements without showing how Colorado will execute the federal program in practice, commenters and later reviewers may be left with a conclusion that feels asserted rather than demonstrated.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask EPA to tie the approval finding more concretely to staffing levels, permit-review workflow, contractor use, enforcement independence, and how EPA tested the state's readiness for real Class VI applications.

### Major claim: the Indian-lands carveout cleanly preserves federal authority where needed

**Underlying assumption**

The jurisdictional line between Colorado-administered wells and EPA-retained Indian-lands wells is clear enough to prevent confusion during project siting, permitting, and later enforcement.

**Failure scenario**

Boundary disputes, cross-boundary plume questions, or project components near tribal lands could create uncertainty over which sovereign is responsible for permit review or enforcement action.

**Procedural implication**

The record would benefit from clearer discussion of how EPA and Colorado will handle edge cases, consultation triggers, and shared fact patterns that cross jurisdictional lines.

### Major claim: Colorado has enough technical capacity to evaluate complex Class VI applications

**Underlying assumption**

The listed in-house staff plus possible contractor support are enough to review modeling, site characterization, financial responsibility, and long-term monitoring for carbon sequestration wells.

**Failure scenario**

If staffing remains dependent on vacancies, contractor availability, or informal coordination across units, the state may face bottlenecks or uneven review quality as project complexity rises.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask EPA to document expected workload, minimum staffing assumptions, and how contractor use will preserve accountability rather than substituting for core agency capacity.

### Major claim: EPA oversight is a sufficient backstop after primacy transfer

**Underlying assumption**

Post-approval federal oversight mechanisms will reliably catch state under-enforcement or technical drift before risks to underground sources of drinking water worsen.

**Failure scenario**

If oversight is described only in broad terms, the final record may not show how quickly EPA would detect or respond to state-level permitting or enforcement problems.

**Procedural implication**

The strongest comments here would request more concrete descriptions of permit review, audit frequency, intervention triggers, and information-sharing expectations.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

EPA proposes to approve Colorado's request for primacy over Class VI underground injection wells used for geologic carbon sequestration under SDWA section 1422. The proposed approval would allow Colorado's Energy and Carbon Management Commission to issue and enforce Class VI permits within the State, while “the EPA retains primacy” on Indian lands. The record indicates that Colorado submitted a primacy package including a program description, statutes and regulations, public-participation materials, an Attorney General statement, and an MOA with EPA Region 8. The program description says Colorado adopted a new 1400 Series of Rules for Class VI wells, requires a public Commission hearing on permits, and plans to use both in-house staff and contractor expertise for technically complex reviews.

### Inference

This is a governance-transfer NPRM more than a classic cost-benefit regulation. The principal public-comment leverage is whether EPA has shown that Colorado's legal authorities, staffing model, and federal oversight arrangements are concrete enough for a high-stakes permitting program that will affect carbon sequestration deployment and groundwater protection for years.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**
Allow Colorado to administer the Class VI UIC program itself while preserving protection of underground sources of drinking water.

**Problem definition**
Colorado has adopted a Class VI legal framework and wants EPA to recognize it as meeting federal primacy standards.

**Data used**
Colorado's primacy application, program description, statutes and regulations, public-participation materials, Attorney General statement, and MOA; EPA's technical evaluation of those materials.

**Analytical method**
EPA compares Colorado's authorities and program design to SDWA section 1422 and 40 CFR parts 144, 145, and 146, then proposes codification in part 147 with continued federal oversight.

**Alternatives considered**
The visible record mainly presents approval versus non-approval. It says much less, at least in the surfaced materials, about narrower conditional approval paths, staged approvals, or approval contingent on fully staffed state implementation.

**Agency conclusion**
Colorado's Class VI program is at least as stringent as the federal baseline, has adequate administrative and technical capacity, and can receive primacy except on Indian lands where EPA remains the permitting authority.

### Missing links / unsupported premises / logical jumps

The main jumps are:

1. from completeness review to practical readiness for complex Class VI project review,
2. from described staffing and future recruitment to demonstrated implementation capacity,
3. from a general oversight promise to an operationally clear federal backstop,
4. from a jurisdictional carveout to confidence that border and consultation questions will be managed smoothly.

   A clearer explanation of what evidence convinced EPA that Colorado is operationally ready now, not just legally eligible in theory.

3. **Oversight gap**
   More concrete description of audit cadence, permit-review touchpoints, escalation triggers, and how EPA would respond if Colorado underperforms.

4. **Jurisdictional-edge-case gap**
   More direct treatment of projects near Indian lands, plume migration questions, and consultation or enforcement coordination where authority lines may be sensitive.

5. **Alternative-design gap**
   Public-facing analysis of whether conditional approval, phased transition, or enhanced early reporting would better manage implementation risk.

A commenter could request:

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

### 1. Ask EPA to make the “comprehensive evaluation” more visible

Constructive angle: request a clearer public-facing crosswalk between federal Class VI requirements and Colorado's actual authorities, staffing, and review procedures.

### 2. Ask EPA to explain why current staffing is enough

Commenters can focus on workload, vacancies, and how as-needed contractors fit into a program that may involve technically demanding geologic and modeling questions.

### 3. Ask EPA to operationalize the oversight promise

The proposal says EPA retains oversight, but commenters can ask what that means in practice for permit review, inspections, and intervention.

### 4. Ask EPA to address Indian-lands edge cases more concretely

This is a high-value clarification request because the proposal expressly preserves EPA primacy on Indian lands while moving most in-state permitting to Colorado.

### 5. Ask EPA to discuss transitional alternatives

A phased or conditional approval framework may not be necessary, but the record would be stronger if EPA explained why full primacy now is preferable to more incremental transition tools.

### 6. Ask EPA to commit to transparent early-years reporting

Public reporting on permitting throughput, enforcement activity, and coordination with EPA would strengthen accountability without rejecting the primacy model itself.
