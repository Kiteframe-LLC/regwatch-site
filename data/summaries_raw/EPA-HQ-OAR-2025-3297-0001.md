## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Major claim: lower BEV sales projections make the MY 2027-2028 Tier 4 phase-in infeasible on the original schedule

**Underlying assumption**
Manufacturers materially depended on high BEV sales to satisfy fleet-average NMOG+NOx and per-vehicle PM obligations, and cannot realistically substitute gasoline-vehicle hardware changes quickly enough for MYs 2027 and 2028.

**Failure scenario**
If manufacturers could comply through other tools, such as more aggressive use of ABT, targeted deployment of GPFs, or a narrower redesign of specific high-volume models, the case for a two-year blanket delay weakens.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask EPA to show how much of the claimed feasibility problem remains after accounting for ABT flexibility, already-certified Tier 4 vehicles, and manufacturer-specific rather than sector-wide compliance pathways.

### Major claim: the cost-savings estimate is directionally reliable enough to support deregulation

**Underlying assumption**
The catalyst-loadings memo and the one-case DRIA approach capture the main incremental costs that would be avoided, even though some compliance routes and noncompliance costs are not monetized.

**Failure scenario**
If the unmodeled alternatives materially lower compliance costs under the original Tier 4 schedule, or if the omitted noncompliance and sunk-cost categories run in both directions, the headline savings figure may be overstated or incomplete.

**Procedural implication**
A useful comment would not merely attack the presence of uncertainty. It would ask EPA to show sensitivity ranges for alternative ABT use, partial GPF deployment, and sunk Tier 4 investments already made for MYs 2027 and 2028.

### Major claim: emissions and air-quality impacts are small enough that new air-quality modeling is unnecessary

**Underlying assumption**
National inventory increases of up to 0.2 percent for NOx and up to 1.6 percent for onroad PM2.5 are small enough that the absence of new air-quality modeling does not materially undermine the decision record.

**Failure scenario**
Small national percentages could still mask localized near-road or corridor effects, especially because the proposal itself notes that people living or working near transportation corridors are especially affected by motor-vehicle pollution.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask for a more explicit explanation of why national-scale inventory deltas are an adequate screening tool for a proposal that delays standards nationwide but may have concentrated local consequences.

### Major claim: the change in position is lawful despite prior Tier 4 planning and investment

**Underlying assumption**
Intervening policy and market changes are strong enough that previously incurred research, development, and manufacturing investments do not outweigh the need to revisit the MY 2027-2028 schedule.

**Failure scenario**
If a substantial share of manufacturers already engineered or certified Tier 4-compliant vehicles for the affected model years, the delay may redistribute competitive burdens rather than simply relieve infeasible compliance pressure.

**Procedural implication**
The reliance-interest question is a live administrative-law issue, and the final record will need more than generalized statements that the landscape changed.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

EPA proposes to keep Tier 3 criteria-pollutant standards in place for light-duty vehicles, light-duty trucks, medium-duty passenger vehicles, and medium-duty vehicles during model years 2027 and 2028, delaying the initial Tier 4 criteria-pollutant phase-in until MY 2029. The proposal also delays associated certification-test and on-board-diagnostics changes and requests comment on related implementation details, including whether to extend Tier 3 for three model years instead of two, whether to import additional Tier 4 NMOG+NOx bins into the Tier 3 program for MYs 2027 and 2028, how ABT should affect the cost analysis, how already-certified Tier 4 vehicles should be treated, and whether reliance interests are significant. EPA estimates present-value cost savings of about $1.77 billion at a 3 percent discount rate and $1.66 billion at a 7 percent discount rate, while also projecting small increases in VOC, NOx, PM, and air-toxics emissions relative to the current Tier 4 baseline.

### Inference

This is a deregulatory timing proposal rather than a full reconsideration of the substantive Tier 4 limits. EPA is not yet proposing a new final long-term pollution-control architecture. Instead, it is arguing that short-term compliance assumptions collapsed because expected BEV penetration fell and the industry lacks enough lead time to redesign, source, certify, and install additional controls such as GPFs and upgraded catalyst systems on the original schedule.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**
Avoid near-term noncompliance and feasibility failures by delaying the criteria-pollutant portion of the Tier 4 phase-in for MYs 2027 and 2028 while EPA prepares a broader Part 2 reconsideration.

**Problem definition**
The 2024 Tier 4 schedule assumed relatively high BEV sales that would help manufacturers meet fleet-average NMOG+NOx and per-vehicle PM requirements with less near-term redesign of internal-combustion vehicles. EPA now says that assumption no longer fits the market, legal, and policy landscape.

**Data used**
EPA relies on revised BEV projections, automaker product-plan developments, a DRIA, a catalyst-loadings cost memo, MOVES-based national emissions inventory modeling, and previously developed health-effects technical support documents.

**Analytical method**
EPA compares an action case that extends Tier 3 for MYs 2027 and 2028 with a no-action case that retains the 2024 Tier 4 phase-in, then estimates cost savings and emissions deltas. For health and air-quality consequences, EPA does not do new air-quality modeling and instead infers that ambient impacts are likely small because projected emissions increases are relatively small at the national onroad-inventory level.

**Alternatives considered**
The visible notice discusses a narrow menu of alternatives rather than a full alternatives matrix. It invites comment on a three-year extension through MY 2029, additional Tier 4 bins inside the Tier 3 framework, ABT assumptions, OBD modifications, and treatment of already-certified Tier 4 vehicles.

**Agency conclusion**
EPA concludes that changed conditions and lead-time limits justify postponing the near-term criteria-pollutant phase-in without reopening the post-MY-2029 elements of the program in this rulemaking.

### Missing links / unsupported premises

The largest missing link is the jump from “BEV sales are now projected to be lower” to “a broad two-year delay is the best response for the whole regulated fleet.” The notice gives examples and cost logic, but the public-facing explanation still leaves room to ask why narrower tools, manufacturer-specific flexibility, or a shorter delay would not work. Another gap is the decision to skip new air-quality modeling while still making a nationwide delay proposal that will increase emissions by design. EPA explains why it thinks the impacts are likely small, but the visible record does not quantify localized concentration changes or near-road distributional effects.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Fact**
The technical story is coherent at a high level. The NPRM explains that BEVs count at zero grams per mile for fleet-average NMOG+NOx and help satisfy PM phase-in obligations without extra tailpipe hardware. Lower projected BEV penetration therefore increases the amount of catalyst enhancement, GPF integration, OBD work, supplier coordination, packaging changes, and certification lead time required for conventional vehicles. The catalyst-loadings memorandum gives a more specific cost-method foundation by describing how EPA linked catalyst loading and Tier 3 certification bins.

**Inference**
The main weakness is not contradiction; it is confidence calibration. EPA’s own record shows that several key parts of the technical and economic story remain uncertain enough to warrant targeted requests for comment. The proposal may still be technically plausible, but it is not presented as a locked-down engineering certainty.

**Annex A — EPA technical shortcut**

Affected vehicles: nationwide light-duty and medium-duty program vehicles covered by Tier 3 and the delayed Tier 4 criteria-pollutant schedule.

Emission reduction potential from the proposal itself: negative relative to the current Tier 4 baseline, because EPA projects increases in VOC, NOx, PM, and toxics compared with keeping the original phase-in.

Cost direction: substantial near-term savings to manufacturers, with present-value savings of about $1.66 billion to $1.77 billion.

That shortcut frames the rule as a classic trade: immediate feasibility and cost relief in exchange for delayed pollution reductions.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact**
EPA grounds the proposal in Clean Air Act section 202(a)(2), emphasizing that standards may take effect only after a period necessary to permit development and application of the requisite technology while giving appropriate consideration to compliance cost. The notice also expressly addresses change-in-position doctrine by acknowledging reliance interests and citing the principle that agencies may revise prior actions if they acknowledge the shift, explain it reasonably, and consider legitimate reliance.

**Inference**
The legal footing is not obviously outside EPA’s statutory lane. The more vulnerable legal question is whether the record adequately supports the factual predicates for reopening the schedule so soon after the 2024 rule. That means the strongest challenge angle is likely reasoned explanation and reliance analysis, not basic statutory authority.

**Uncertain interpretation**
The notice treats changed BEV projections, the June 2025 CRA resolution on California’s ACC II waiver, the July 2025 OBBB, and automaker announcements as enough to reopen the near-term schedule. The visible record gives that theory a plausible basis, but commenters can still ask whether those developments justify a full two-year nationwide delay rather than a narrower response.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

One failure mode is forecast error. If BEV sales or manufacturer adaptation capabilities improve materially before MY 2027, EPA may end up delaying controls more than necessary based on an overly pessimistic snapshot.

Another failure mode is asymmetry in burden relief. Firms that already invested heavily in Tier 4 compliance for MYs 2027 and 2028 could be disadvantaged relative to firms that delayed investment and now benefit more from the postponement.

A third failure mode is localized impact blindness. Even small national inventory increases can matter more in high-traffic corridors or communities already burdened by transportation pollution, yet the proposal does not model ambient concentration changes from the delay.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

The proposal creates incentives for manufacturers to emphasize lower-BEV scenarios and near-term hardware constraints because those showings support schedule relief. It also reduces immediate pressure to accelerate GPF integration and deeper catalyst upgrades on conventional vehicles for MYs 2027 and 2028.

At the same time, the structure preserves an incentive to keep some Tier 4-compliant product plans alive, because EPA proposes to honor existing Tier 4 certificates and contemplates mixing Tier 3 and Tier 4 sales-weighted values within a model year.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

The record is strongest on the existence of an analytical foundation. EPA did not proceed with only industry anecdotes. It assembled a DRIA, a cost-support memorandum, MOVES-based emissions modeling, and reference health documents.

The weaker point is the decision logic that converts those materials into a specific two-year nationwide delay. EPA quantifies cost savings and acknowledges uncertainty, but it also says it modeled only one cost case, did not monetize some potential noncompliance effects, and did not conduct new air-quality modeling. That makes the evidence real but not fully closed.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

The most plausible vulnerability is not that EPA lacks statutory power to revise a schedule under section 202(a)(2). A secondary vulnerability is reliance analysis. EPA acknowledges prior investment and invites comment, but the final record will need to show that it weighed those interests against feasibility concerns in a more concrete way than generalized references to a changed landscape.
