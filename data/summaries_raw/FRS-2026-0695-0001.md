## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Claim: Safety and soundness concerns formerly addressed through reputation risk are adequately covered by other risk categories.
Underlying assumption: Existing credit, market, liquidity, operational, and legal risk frameworks are sufficient substitutes in all relevant supervisory settings.
Failure scenario: Examiners encounter conduct that is difficult to classify under those categories, producing inconsistent treatment or a supervisory gap.
Procedural implication: Commenters have leverage to request examples, boundary rules, and implementation guidance showing how those substitute categories will work in practice.

Claim: Benefits and costs after June 2025 are de minimis because the Board already removed reputation risk from supervision.
Underlying assumption: Internal practice changed completely and consistently after the June 2025 announcement.
Failure scenario: Supervisory materials, examiner habits, or institution-facing expectations still contain residual references or proxy concepts.
Procedural implication: The record would be stronger if the Board described the cleanup status, timelines, and residual implementation work rather than assuming full transition.

Claim: The proposal will increase clarity and objectivity.
Underlying assumption: The prohibition's operative terms are clearer than the supervisory concept it removes.
Failure scenario: Terms such as "encourage," "compel," and "politically disfavored but lawful business activities" create new line-drawing disputes.
Procedural implication: Commenters can press for definitions, examples, or safe-harbor language to reduce ambiguity.

Claim: Market access may expand because banks may maintain profitable relationships previously discouraged by reputation-risk concerns.
Underlying assumption: Reputation risk meaningfully constrained customer relationships in a way the rule will relieve.
Failure scenario: Firms continue making the same account and lending decisions for ordinary business, legal, or credit reasons, so the rule changes little in customer access.
Procedural implication: The claimed benefit would be more credible with concrete examples or limiting assumptions.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

Observed Facts:
- The Board proposes to add `12 CFR 262.9`.
- The rule would prohibit the Board from using "reputation risk" in examination programs or in supervisory materials.
- The rule would also state that the Board may not encourage or compel supervised banking organizations to deny or condition financial services based on constitutionally protected political or religious beliefs, associations, speech, or conduct, or based on involvement in politically disfavored but lawful business activities perceived to present reputation risk.
- Covered entities include bank holding companies, savings and loan holding companies, state member banks, subsidiaries, and combined U.S. operations of foreign banking organizations.
- The rule of construction says the Board retains authority to enforce applicable law, including the Bank Secrecy Act, OFAC sanctions, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, and the Fair Housing Act.

Inference:
- The proposal is both a codification of a June 2025 supervisory-policy change and a forward-looking effort to cabin examiner discretion in politically sensitive "debanking" disputes.
- The Board is trying to separate prudential supervision from viewpoint-based or political considerations while preserving conventional safety-and-soundness and compliance oversight.

Evidence Quotes:
- "The Board shall not use reputation risk as a component of its examination programs or in materials used for the supervision of banking organizations."
- "The Board shall not encourage or compel banking organizations to deny or condition the provision of banking or other financial products or services" for the specified protected reasons.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

Regulatory objective:
- Codify the removal of reputation risk from Board supervision and prevent supervisory pressure that could drive politically or religiously discriminatory banking outcomes.

Problem definition:
- Reputation risk is difficult to quantify and communicate.
- Concerns have arisen that reputation risk and similar supervisory tools have been misused.

Data used:
- Historical search of MRAs/MRIAs mentioning the word "reputation."
- General statement that the Board ended use of reputation risk in June 2025.

Analytical method:
- Qualitative reasoning plus a simple historical count of supervisory findings containing the word "reputation."

Alternatives considered:
- The notice asks what alternatives might better achieve the Board's objectives, but the preamble does not meaningfully analyze alternatives itself.

Agency conclusion:
- Codification will improve clarity, reduce burden, preserve effective supervision, and yield benefits that outweigh costs.

Missing links:
- The notice does not show why word-search frequency is a reliable proxy for the real supervisory role of reputation risk.
- The notice does not explain why codifying a broad anti-discrimination policy statement is the best means of implementing the June 2025 policy change.
- The notice does not connect claimed benefits for market access to identified real-world examples or measured effects.

Unsupported premises:
- That all legitimate prudential concerns previously associated with reputation risk fit cleanly into other risk buckets.
- That the rule's anti-discrimination language is sufficiently clear for examiners and institutions without further elaboration.

Logical jumps:
- From concern about potential misuse to a conclusion that the proposed text solves the problem without meaningful collateral ambiguity.
