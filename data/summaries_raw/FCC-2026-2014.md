### Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

#### Claim

Basic KYC collection from new and renewing customers will deter scammers and improve traceability.

Underlying assumption: Illegal callers either cannot or will not easily adapt to stronger customer-identification checks, and providers can verify the collected information reliably enough to make a practical difference.

Failure scenario: If bad actors can still use stolen identities, shell entities, foreign intermediaries, or rapidly changing IP-based tools, the burden on legitimate customers may rise without proportionate deterrence gains.

Procedural implication: Commenters can ask the FCC to provide evidence on which KYC elements actually improve detection and which only add paperwork.

#### Claim

Risk-tiered requirements for high-volume or foreign customers are workable.

Underlying assumption: The FCC can define `high-volume`, higher-risk traffic, and foreign-customer triggers clearly enough that providers can apply them consistently.

Failure scenario: If the thresholds remain vague, providers may over-collect data, under-enforce, or apply inconsistent screening across similarly situated customers.

Procedural implication: The final record would be stronger if the FCC disclosed concrete thresholds, examples, and expected false-positive tradeoffs.

#### Claim

Periodic re-verification is essential.

Underlying assumption: Existing one-time onboarding checks materially degrade over time and re-verification meaningfully improves enforcement outcomes.

Failure scenario: If customer information rarely changes in ways relevant to illegal-call prevention, routine re-verification may add recurring burden without much additional value.

Procedural implication: Commenters can ask for evidence on when and how often re-verification has prevented abuse or improved tracing.

#### Claim

Incremental compliance costs will be minimal because many providers already do some KYC.

Underlying assumption: Current industry practice overlaps substantially with the FCC's possible requirements and small or non-nationwide providers will not need major new systems.

Failure scenario: If many providers, especially smaller or prepaid-focused providers, lack document-verification systems, storage controls, or foreign-customer review capacity, incremental costs may be significant.

Procedural implication: The final rule record would be stronger with provider-size segmentation and implementation-cost estimates by business model.

#### Claim

Per-call KYC penalties will improve compliance and reduce illegal calls.

Underlying assumption: A per-call forfeiture schedule will produce stronger deterrence without creating unfair notice problems or disproportionate exposure for ambiguous violations.

Failure scenario: If the underlying KYC duties remain open-ended, a per-call penalty could magnify uncertainty and create pressure for defensive over-compliance.

Procedural implication: Commenters can ask the FCC to pair any per-call penalty with clearer trigger rules, safe harbors, and penalty-calculation examples.

### Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

#### Observed Facts

- The FCC seeks comment on requiring originating providers to collect baseline customer information from new and renewing customers before granting access to voice services.
- The notice identifies possible baseline data points such as name, physical address, government-issued identification number, and an alternate telephone number.
- For some customers, especially high-volume, business, or foreign customers, the FCC asks whether providers should collect additional information such as intended use of service and the IP address from which calls will be placed.
- The FCC also asks about verification, retention, re-verification, prepaid-service treatment, AI-assisted KYC tools, foreign-customer controls, and whether alternative information sources such as credit reports should sometimes substitute for direct collection.
- The notice proposes to codify a forfeiture amount of `$2,500` per call for KYC violations in the FCC forfeiture table.
- The legal-basis and IRFA sections indicate the rule could affect a large number of telecommunications firms, many of them small entities.

#### Inference

This is a meaningful NPRM-stage anti-robocall proposal, but the document is more developed on direction than on calibration. The FCC has a clear enforcement theory: stop illegal traffic earlier by making originating providers know more about customers and by raising the stakes for noncompliance. The most comment-productive weaknesses are not that the FCC lacks any rationale, but that the visible record is thin on how particular KYC elements were selected, how high-risk categories will be defined, how privacy and data-retention burdens will be managed, and why incremental cost is expected to be minimal.

### Step 9 — Failure Modes

- Providers may collect more personal data than necessary because the final standard remains vague about what is required for different customer types.
- Smaller or prepaid-service providers may face nontrivial system, staffing, and retention costs if the FCC assumes existing KYC sophistication that is not universal.
- Bad actors may adapt by using proxies, stolen identities, or layered intermediaries, reducing the benefit of simple document collection alone.
- Per-call penalties may create severe exposure if KYC duties remain unclear or if violation attribution is not carefully bounded.
- Customers with legitimate high-volume use cases may face delayed service or over-screening if thresholds are blunt.

### Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

- The proposal would incentivize originating providers to screen more aggressively and document customer onboarding more thoroughly.
- It may also encourage defensive over-collection and longer retention of personal information if providers fear per-call liability but lack clear safe harbors.
- A vague `high-risk` framework could encourage providers to classify uncertain customers conservatively, potentially shifting burden onto legitimate business, foreign, or prepaid users.

- Another potential weakness is fair-notice pressure if the FCC combines open-ended KYC duties with per-call forfeitures before clarifying what counts as compliance.
