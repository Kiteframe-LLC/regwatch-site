## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Claim: Excluding all Payment Account holders from discount window credit mitigates risk to Reserve Banks in a consistent and transparent way.
Underlying assumption: The shared features of Payment Account holders justify a categorical exclusion even though their business models and supervisory profiles may vary.
Failure scenario: Some Payment Account holders may be operationally narrow, well-controlled, or otherwise low-risk enough that a blanket exclusion sacrifices useful flexibility without materially improving safety.
Procedural implication: Commenters can press the Board to explain why a categorical ban is superior to a narrower or conditional approach.

Claim: Uninsured status, varied insolvency regimes, and limited supervisory data justify denying access.
Underlying assumption: Those features create material credit-risk or operational-risk concerns that cannot be addressed through collateral, haircut, eligibility screening, or stress-specific conditions.
Failure scenario: If those concerns can be addressed with more targeted controls, the record may not support the need for a full categorical bar.
Procedural implication: The Board may need to explain why less restrictive safeguards were not sufficient.

Claim: Liquidity provision to Payment Account holders could incentivize risk-taking behavior.
Underlying assumption: The marginal availability of discount window credit would materially change incentives for these institutions.
Failure scenario: If Payment Accounts are already tightly constrained and pre-funded, moral-hazard effects may be smaller than the notice suggests.
Procedural implication: Commenters have leverage to ask for more concrete causal support rather than generalized prudential intuition.

Claim: Leaving current eligibility rules unchanged could support financial stability and monetary policy implementation, but exclusion is still preferable.
Underlying assumption: The downside risks of access outweigh the potential stability or implementation benefits in most realistic scenarios.
Failure scenario: If stress events or operational incidents are precisely when liquidity backstops matter most, the categorical exclusion may prove overbroad.
Procedural implication: The Board should explain more clearly why limited emergency access was not chosen.

Claim: The proposal imposes no mandatory requirements on small entities because Payment Accounts are optional.
Underlying assumption: The relevant baseline is that firms may simply not choose a Payment Account.
Failure scenario: If the Board is simultaneously creating Payment Accounts as a practical route for some institutions to access Reserve Bank services, the loss of discount window eligibility may still shape business choices and funding costs.
Procedural implication: The RFA discussion may understate the practical tradeoffs for smaller eligible firms.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

Observed Facts:
- The Board proposes to amend `12 CFR 201.2` to define `payment account`.
- The Board proposes to amend `12 CFR 201.3(a)(1)` and add `§ 201.3(a)(4)` to specify that a Reserve Bank may not lend under the primary, secondary, or seasonal credit programs to a depository institution that holds a Payment Account.
- The proposal is tied to a broader Payment Account initiative described in a separate same-day notice concerning account access and payment system risk.
- The Board says the proposal would not affect discount window eligibility for other depository institutions using master accounts or correspondent arrangements.
- The Board expressly requests comment on whether it has correctly identified the risks and benefits, whether existing eligibility rules should remain unchanged, and whether narrower restrictions or limited access in stress periods would be better.

Inference:
- The proposal is less about altering discount window mechanics generally and more about drawing a policy boundary around a new type of special-purpose Reserve Bank account.
- The Board is trying to support payments innovation while denying one of the most sensitive forms of Federal Reserve support to entities it expects to be newer, less supervised, or less legible from a credit-risk perspective.

Evidence Quotes:
- "The Board proposes to amend Regulation A to specify that a holder of a Payment Account would not be eligible for discount window credit."
- "A Federal Reserve Bank may not lend under § 201.4(a), (b), or (c) to a depository institution that holds a payment account."
- "The Board could, alternatively, leave unchanged the current discount window eligibility requirements in Regulation A."

Data used:
- Summary of comments received on the prior Payment Account RFI.

Analytical method:
- Qualitative risk reasoning with some reliance on prior comments and related policy proposals.

Alternatives considered:
- Keep existing Regulation A eligibility rules unchanged.
- Consider narrower restrictions or limited access in unusual or exigent circumstances.

Agency conclusion:
- A categorical exclusion for Payment Account holders is the best balance because it mitigates Reserve Bank, financial-sector, and economy-wide risk in a consistent and transparent way.

Missing links:

- The notice does not explain why collateral requirements, supervisory conditions, or stress-only access would be inadequate substitutes for a categorical ban.

Unsupported premises:

- That lack of existing supervisory information necessarily warrants zero access rather than staged eligibility or enhanced information requirements.

Logical jumps:
- From uncertainty and heterogeneity to a conclusion that categorical exclusion is the right design, without much intermediate analysis of partial alternatives.
