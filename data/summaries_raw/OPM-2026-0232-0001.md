## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim: Executive Order 13415 already supplies the written approval needed for rates above `EX-I`
- **Underlying assumption:** The delegation of presidential functions to the Director of OPM is legally sufficient to satisfy `5 U.S.C. 5377(d)(2)` without any additional case-specific presidential document.
- **Failure scenario:** If the delegation is read as authorizing OPM to exercise the function but not as permanently substituting for the statute's written-approval requirement in every case, commenters or later reviewers could argue the rule collapses a safeguard Congress expected to apply case by case.
- **Procedural implication:** A high-value comment can ask OPM to spell out the legal reasoning in more detail and explain why the delegated function supports this exact regulatory rewrite.

### Claim: Additional approval layers and undefined criteria meaningfully suppress agency use of critical pay
- **Underlying assumption:** OPM's experience reflects more than anecdotal impression and captures a real deterrent effect from the current regulatory framework.
- **Failure scenario:** If limited use instead reflects budget constraints, cultural reluctance, or the adequacy of other flexibilities, then removing the criteria may not solve the identified problem and could simply expand discretion with limited demonstrated need.
- **Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask for utilization history, examples of denied or abandoned requests, and evidence showing that the current criteria rather than other constraints are the main bottleneck.

### Claim: Removing `rare circumstances` and `exceptional circumstances` improves transparency
- **Underlying assumption:** Undefined standards create more uncertainty than discipline, and the statute's baseline criteria are enough to constrain decisions.
- **Failure scenario:** If those phrases have functioned as meaningful caution signals even without formal definitions, eliminating them may reduce restraint without replacing them with clearer operational criteria.
- **Procedural implication:** The record would be stronger if OPM explained what review factors will actually govern approvals once those phrases disappear.

### Claim: Employees should have no grievance or appeal right when critical pay is reduced or terminated under the proposed conditions
- **Underlying assumption:** These determinations are closer to discretionary continuation of a temporary pay-setting authority than to a classic reduction-in-pay action.
- **Failure scenario:** If employees reasonably rely on critical pay over time, or if agencies use notice language broadly without consistent standards, the rule could reduce procedural protection in circumstances that feel practically similar to a compensation cut.
- **Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask whether narrower procedural safeguards, minimum notice content, or review criteria would preserve flexibility while reducing arbitrariness concerns.

### Claim: Government costs from increased use are illustrative transfers that will remain limited
- **Underlying assumption:** The statutory cap, OPM/OMB review, and the necessity standard will keep the authority narrowly used even after the regulatory loosening.
- **Failure scenario:** If agencies begin using the authority more aggressively for AI, cyber, or other technical hiring priorities, the low-utilization baseline cited today may be a weak guide to future use.
- **Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask for sensitivity analysis, historical utilization tables, and discussion of whether the rule could reallocate pay budgets or create internal equity pressures.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

**Observed Facts**
- The NPRM would revise `5 CFR part 535` to make `EX-I` the default maximum critical pay rate and allow pay above `EX-I` with written approval by the Director of OPM rather than separate presidential approval.
- It would remove the regulatory phrases `exceptional circumstances` and `rare circumstances`.
- It would allow agencies to require written service agreements and allow OPM to require service-agreement or notice terms as a condition of granting or continuing critical pay authority.
- It would clarify that certain reductions, non-increases, or terminations of critical position pay are not grievances, appeals, or chapter 75 adverse actions when the employee was informed the rate was time-limited, subject to annual review, and could be reduced or terminated if no longer needed.
- It would clarify how critical pay is treated as basic pay for some purposes while not being a General Schedule basic-pay rate for the `5 CFR part 531` GS administration rules, except as referenced in the maximum payable rate rule.

**Evidence quotes**
- "the rate payable for EX-I would serve as the default maximum rate for critical position pay"
- "OPM proposes to remove the 'rare circumstances' criterion"
- "An agency may require an employee to sign a written service agreement"
- "An employee has no right to grieve or appeal a decision to reduce, not increase, or terminate a critical position pay rate."
- "if up to one-half of the authorized positions ... were approved at rates above EX-I ... the resulting incremental cost ... could range from approximately $20 million to $40 million per year"

**Inference**
- This NPRM is a legal-procedural simplification rule with real practical effects. OPM is trying to make a rarely used pay flexibility easier to deploy for mission-critical hiring, especially at very high pay levels, while also making it easier to reduce or end that pay later without triggering standard employee-protection channels.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- Agencies could use the broadened flexibility more aggressively than OPM predicts, creating compensation disparities that are difficult to compare across agencies or occupations.
- Director-level approval above `EX-I` could become easier in practice without the notice explaining what concrete standards will govern that discretion.
- Employees could experience large pay reductions or non-renewals under service agreements and notice provisions with limited recourse, leading to disputes about notice sufficiency and fairness.
- Agencies may interpret `necessary to recruit or retain` inconsistently if OPM does not provide clearer implementation guidance after deleting the current regulatory caution signals.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

- The proposal increases agencies' incentive to consider critical pay for hard-to-fill technical, scientific, professional, or administrative positions because the approval path becomes simpler and potentially faster.
- It also increases the incentive to use time-limited arrangements and service agreements, which may give agencies more leverage over continuation decisions.
- At the same time, employees may face stronger incentives to negotiate initial terms carefully because the rule would reduce later grievance or appeal options if pay is reduced or not renewed under the stated conditions.

## Step 18 — Decision Brief

**Bottom line**
