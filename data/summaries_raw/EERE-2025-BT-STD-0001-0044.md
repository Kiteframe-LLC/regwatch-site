## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Classification:** Mostly coherent, with one recurring tension.

- The proposal is technically coherent in the sense that it uses familiar DOE process-rule tools: thresholds, comparative analysis, comment periods, and a webinar.
- The main technical tension is class variation. A single threshold or one restored analytic sequence may not fit consumer appliances, commercial equipment, and ASHRAE equipment equally well.
- The notice partly addresses this by inviting comments on alternative thresholds, but it does not yet give a final design for how the rule will adapt across categories.
- Overall, the mechanism is understandable; the open question is whether it is sufficiently tailored to the underlying product mix.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- If the threshold is too high, DOE may discourage standards rulemakings that should have proceeded on a fuller record.
- If the threshold is too low, the gate becomes ornamental and adds process without genuine screening value.
- If the comparative-analysis requirement is not documented clearly, future decision records may become harder to reconstruct.
- If Appendix A becomes too rigid, DOE may need repeated departures or ad hoc fixes for unusual product classes.
- If the ASHRAE evidence language is reinstated without operational guidance, it may become a slogan rather than a usable rule.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

- The most obvious incentive problem is threshold gaming: parties may try to frame savings estimates or product classes to clear or avoid the gate.
- The proposal can also incentivize strategic class-splitting if different thresholds are applied by category.
- A binding process rule can encourage parties to litigate process details instead of the substance of later standards.
- On the positive side, the restored comparative-analysis language may incentivize more complete documentation of trial standard levels.
