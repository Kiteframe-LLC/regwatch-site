## Phase 1 — Docket Integrity Check

**Classification:** Partial analytical spine

**Observed Fact**

The selected document is the substantive NPRM itself, not an extension, reopening, or correction notice. The metadata identifies it as a "Proposed Rule," and the notice proposes to rescind EPA's regulatory determinations, MCLGs, MCLs, and related compliance provisions for PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA (GenX), and the hazard-index mixture built from those substances plus PFBS.

The available record includes the full notice text, metadata listing ten supporting materials, and cached older technical/economic attachments from the 2024 PFAS rulemaking, including the final PFAS NPDWR and its economic analysis. The newer rule-specific cost overview memorandum and ICR statement listed in metadata were not cached for this run, but the preamble itself contains the central legal and cost-benefit assertions EPA relies on.

The notice text identifies a recognizable analytical spine for a major NPRM: statutory background, procedural history, basis for proposal, economic analysis, primacy requirements, and statutory/executive-order reviews. It also points to supporting materials such as the cost-benefit overview memorandum and OMB review documentation.

**Inference**

This is not a thin record in the sense of a bare notice with no explanation. But it is only a partial spine for this run because some rule-specific supporting materials cited by the notice were not available in cache, and the proposal's core claims still lean heavily on EPA's own legal reinterpretation and summary-level cost figures in the preamble.

**Why that matters**

The strongest public-comment leverage is not to say the docket has no analysis. It is to ask EPA to show more of the legal and policy basis for rescinding health-protective standards, especially where the proposal quantifies savings and foregone benefits only at a high level and does not fully test narrower alternatives.

## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Major claim: the 2024 standards for these PFAS were promulgated through an unlawful sequence

**Underlying assumption**

SDWA section 1412 is best read to require a final determination to regulate before EPA may even propose the NPDWR for the same contaminant.

**Failure scenario**

If the statute can reasonably bear EPA's earlier reading, or if the relevant defect affects only the timing of proposal rather than the validity of the final standards, the case for rescinding already-promulgated health protections becomes materially weaker.

**Procedural implication**

Comments can ask EPA to show why rescission is legally required rather than simply preferred, and to explain how this proposal fits with SDWA's separate instruction that revised regulations must maintain or provide greater protection of health.

### Major claim: full rescission is the right corrective response

**Underlying assumption**

The best remedy for the alleged sequencing defect is removing the PFHxS/PFNA/HFPO-DA/Index-PFAS standards now, rather than preserving them temporarily while EPA conducts a new sequential rulemaking.

**Failure scenario**

If EPA could cure the process prospectively while keeping existing protections in place, commenters may argue the agency has not justified why an immediate rollback is the least disruptive lawful option.

**Procedural implication**

Comments can press EPA to compare full rescission with stay-plus-reproposal, severance, phased replacement, or interim continuation alternatives.

### Major claim: the cost savings and foregone benefits are adequately explained

**Underlying assumption**

Summary figures in the preamble are enough for commenters to evaluate the balance between reduced compliance obligations and reduced health protection.

**Failure scenario**

If the notice quantifies savings and foregone benefits only selectively, without clearly showing assumptions, affected systems, distribution, or uncertainty, the public record may be too opaque for meaningful scrutiny.

**Procedural implication**

Comments can ask EPA to disclose the underlying modeling, affected-system counts, and distribution of both savings and lost protections across communities.

### Major claim: leaving PFOA and PFOS in place while rescinding the other four pollutant actions is a coherent line

**Underlying assumption**

The legal defect is limited to the contaminants for which EPA combined the regulatory determination and rulemaking process, and does not spill over into the retained PFOA/PFOS standards.

**Failure scenario**

If the retained and rescinded components are operationally intertwined in monitoring, treatment, communications, or compliance planning, the line may be administratively workable only with further explanation.

**Procedural implication**

Comments can ask EPA to explain transition effects for systems that planned treatment around the integrated 2024 PFAS framework.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

EPA proposes to rescind regulatory determinations and associated drinking-water standards for PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and the hazard-index mixture of those PFAS plus PFBS. The proposal would remove the related MCLGs, MCLs, and associated provisions in `40 CFR parts 141 and 142`, while leaving the 2024 PFOA and PFOS standards intact.

EPA's stated rationale is legal, not scientific. The notice says the 2024 rulemaking used an unlawful procedure by proposing and finalizing regulatory determinations and standards for the affected PFAS simultaneously, rather than in sequence. EPA says SDWA requires a preliminary determination, public comment, final determination, then a separate proposal/finalization sequence for the NPDWR.

EPA also states that the rollback would reduce previously expected compliance costs and reduce previously expected health benefits. The preamble gives one annualized savings figure tied to PFHxS and a separate summary figure for foregone benefits, while acknowledging additional nonquantifiable costs and benefits.

### Inference

This is a major deregulatory NPRM disguised less as a policy rebalancing than as a legal correction. The key commentable issues are therefore not whether PFAS matter, but whether EPA has shown that rescission is the necessary legal remedy, whether it has adequately justified its chosen scope, and whether it has transparently accounted for the practical and distributional consequences of removing standards that were only recently finalized.

### Evidence Quotes

- "The EPA is also proposing to rescind all associated regulatory provisions currently codified in the EPA's 2024 PFAS National Primary Drinking Water Regulations."
- "The Act thus requires ... a proposed substantive regulation no earlier than the final regulatory determination."
- "Removing the PFHxS NPDWR is estimated to save $11.6 million ... in annualized quantified costs per year."
- "This proposed deregulatory action is expected to yield a reduction in previously expected benefits of approximately $6.7 million."

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**  
Correct what EPA describes as an unlawful rulemaking sequence under SDWA while reducing associated compliance burdens.

**Problem definition**  
EPA says the 2024 PFHxS/PFNA/HFPO-DA/Index-PFAS actions were finalized through a procedure that unlawfully merged the determination-to-regulate stage with the standard-setting stage.

**Data used**  
Statutory text, procedural history of the 2023 proposal and 2024 final rule, prior PFAS rulemaking materials, and summary cost/benefit estimates.

**Analytical method**  
Legal reinterpretation of SDWA sequencing requirements, paired with a summary deregulatory cost-benefit description.

**Alternatives considered**  
EPA acknowledges alternatives exist but says it is not proposing them because rescission is, in EPA's view, the best reading of the statute and the best way to correct the prior action.

**Agency conclusion**  
Rescind the affected regulatory determinations and standards now, retain PFOA/PFOS, and invite comment on the proposal.

### Missing links / unsupported premises / logical jumps

1. The notice strongly argues that the prior procedure was unlawful, but it is less clear on why that conclusion compels immediate rollback rather than another corrective path.
2. The cost and benefit discussion is summary-level and does not fully surface the assumptions or distribution behind the figures used.
3. The proposal does not thoroughly explain how rescission interacts with SDWA's instruction that revisions maintain or provide greater health protection.
4. The retained-versus-rescinded split is legally motivated, but the notice gives only limited discussion of how water systems should understand the operational transition.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

### Regulatory objective: correct the alleged SDWA sequencing defect while addressing the status of the 2024 PFAS standards

**Alternatives evaluated**

The notice effectively evaluates full rescission of the affected PFAS actions while retaining PFOA/PFOS.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed**

- Maintain the current standards temporarily while conducting a new sequential determination-and-rule process.
- Stay enforcement or deadlines for the affected PFAS while preserving the standards pending reproposal.
- Rescind only the regulatory determinations while keeping some implementation framework in place pending correction.
- Phase the rollback to avoid abrupt planning disruption for water systems already investing in treatment or monitoring.

**Evidence suggesting feasibility**

The notice itself says EPA considered alternatives. The retained PFOA/PFOS structure also shows EPA is not treating the entire 2024 PFAS rule as indivisible, which suggests additional tailoring options were at least possible to discuss.

**Stage-aware classification**

**Potential unacknowledged weakness.** EPA is not merely seeking open-ended input about what to do. It is already proposing a specific deregulatory remedy and should explain more concretely why narrower or more protective corrective options were rejected.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Fact**

The proposal is internally coherent as a legal rollback. It identifies the affected contaminants, distinguishes them from retained PFOA/PFOS provisions, and states that the rollback would remove associated monitoring, treatment, and reporting obligations for the rescinded PFAS standards.

**Inference**

The main technical coherence risk is transitional rather than scientific. Water systems may have been planning compliance around a six-PFAS framework, and the notice does not fully spell out how mixed compliance planning should be re-scoped if only part of that framework remains.

**Unknown**

Unknown from available text whether EPA quantified how many systems had already altered capital planning, treatment design, or procurement decisions specifically because of the now-targeted PFHxS/PFNA/HFPO-DA/Index-PFAS requirements.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact**

EPA grounds the proposal in SDWA section 1412's sequencing requirements and emphasizes the phrase permitting a proposed regulation "concurrent with the determination to regulate." The notice interprets "determination to regulate" to mean the final determination, not the preliminary one. EPA also notes that the 2024 rule is the subject of pending D.C. Circuit litigation.

**Inference**

EPA's legal argument is serious and developed, but it is not self-proving. The notice must carry two legal burdens at once: first, that the 2024 procedure was unlawful; and second, that rescission of already-promulgated health standards is lawful and appropriately reasoned despite SDWA language and case law suggesting revised NPDWRs cannot reduce health protection.

**Unknown**

Unknown from available text whether EPA has docketed a fuller legal memorandum explaining why rescission is compatible with the "maintain, or provide for greater, protection" language for NPDWR revisions.

## Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

**Observed Fact**

EPA is proceeding through ordinary NPRM procedures: notice, comment period, public hearing, and statutory/executive-order review sections. The notice asks for public comment on the proposal and identifies the hearing date and comment deadline.

**Inference**

Procedurally, this is normal in form but unusual in substance because it proposes to withdraw recently finalized drinking-water protections based on a later legal reinterpretation rather than new health science.

**Comment relevance**

That makes it especially important for commenters to ask for the underlying legal and remedial analysis, not just the policy conclusion.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

1. **Legal-remedy mismatch:** EPA may persuade readers that the original sequence was flawed but still fail to show that full rescission is the correct remedy.
2. **Opaque transition effects:** Public water systems may face planning uncertainty if the retained and rescinded PFAS obligations are not operationally translated clearly.
3. **Underexplained distributional loss:** National savings figures may obscure which communities lose expected health protection.
4. **Thin alternatives record:** A court or commenter may view the notice as having skipped plausible middle-ground options.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Observed Fact**

The proposal reduces obligations tied to the rescinded PFAS standards while retaining PFOA/PFOS obligations.

**Inference**

The incentive effect is straightforward: systems and states would have less reason to monitor, treat, and plan around PFHxS/PFNA/HFPO-DA/Index-PFAS compliance, at least under SDWA drinking-water standards. That may reduce near-term burdens, but it also reduces pressure to generate implementation experience and data for those contaminants during the period before any future sequential rulemaking.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Strongest-supported points**

- EPA clearly identifies the procedural history of the 2023 proposal and 2024 final rule.
- EPA clearly states the legal interpretation it now adopts.
- EPA clearly states that the proposal is deregulatory and will reduce some previously expected compliance costs.

**Moderately supported points**

- The general claim that rescission will reduce burdens on systems is plausible, but the public-facing explanation is only partly quantified in the available text.
- The partial-retention approach for PFOA/PFOS appears reasoned, but the operational implications are not fully spelled out.

**Weakest-supported points**

- Why full rescission is preferable to narrower corrective options.
- How the foregone health-protection impacts fall across affected communities.
- How rescission fits with SDWA's health-protection floor for revised drinking-water regulations.

## Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

1. A clearer remedial analysis comparing rescission with other legally available corrective options.
2. Better public-facing explanation of the cost and benefit calculations, affected-system counts, and uncertainty bounds.
3. More explicit treatment of distributional and environmental-justice consequences from foregone PFAS protections.
4. Clearer transition guidance for systems that have already planned treatment, monitoring, or procurement around the 2024 integrated PFAS rule.
5. A direct explanation of how EPA reads the NPDWR revision cases and statutory text in relation to this rollback.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

**Potential vulnerability 1: rescission remedy may be underexplained**

Even if EPA is correct that the 2024 sequencing was unlawful, the notice still needs to explain why rescinding standards now is the appropriate remedy instead of taking a narrower corrective step.

**Potential vulnerability 2: health-protection floor tension**

The notice itself cites authority suggesting revised NPDWRs may not reduce health protections. EPA may believe the unlawfulness of the original promulgation changes that analysis, but the proposal needs to explain that bridge clearly.

**Potential vulnerability 3: alternatives discussion is thin**

The notice says alternatives were considered but does not appear to provide a robust comparison in the available text.

**Potential vulnerability 4: cost-benefit transparency is partial**

Because the proposal quantifies savings and foregone benefits only selectively in the preamble, commenters can argue that the public needs more of the underlying record to test the agency's balancing and assumptions.

## Phase 3 — Comment Generation

## Step 14 — Commentable Sentences

1. EPA should explain why full rescission, rather than temporary preservation plus a new sequential rulemaking, is the legally required or best-supported remedy.
2. EPA should directly address how this proposal is consistent with SDWA language and case law stating that revised drinking-water regulations must maintain or provide greater health protection.
3. EPA should publish the underlying assumptions and affected-system counts behind the cited cost-savings and foregone-benefit figures.
4. EPA should explain how many systems have already planned or invested around the 2024 six-PFAS framework and what transition effects this rollback would create.
5. EPA should explain the distributional consequences of foregone protections, including whether affected burdens fall disproportionately on smaller or more contaminated systems and the communities they serve.
6. EPA should disclose the alternatives it considered and why they were rejected.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

### 1. Ask EPA to justify rescission as a remedy, not just a legal interpretation

EPA spends substantial effort explaining why it now reads SDWA differently. A strong comment should ask the next question: why does that interpretation require or warrant removing the standards immediately rather than repairing the process while preserving protections?

### 2. Ask EPA to reconcile the proposal with SDWA's health-protection floor

The notice itself cites authority stating revised NPDWRs may not decrease health protections. Commenters can ask EPA to explain precisely why this proposal is outside or consistent with that principle.

### 3. Ask EPA to open the black box on savings and foregone benefits

The public-facing record provides high-level figures, but commenters need the underlying modeling, assumptions, and affected-system distribution to test whether the proposal is proportionate and whether important harms are being hidden inside "nonquantifiable" categories.

### 4. Ask EPA to explain transitional impacts on real systems

Many systems may already have planned around the 2024 PFAS framework. Commenters can ask how rescission affects capital planning, treatment procurement, public communication, and state primacy administration.

### 5. Ask EPA to analyze narrower alternatives

A strong administrative-record comment should press EPA to compare full rescission with more tailored corrective options that would reduce legal risk without fully withdrawing the protections.

## Step 18 — Decision Brief

### Leverage Point 1: EPA's legal theory does not automatically resolve the remedial question

**Observed Fact**

EPA argues the 2024 sequence was unlawful because the proposal and finalization merged stages that SDWA requires to occur in sequence.

**Inference**

That argument may support some corrective action, but it does not by itself establish that full rescission is the only or best option.

**Useful comment question**

What legal analysis shows that EPA cannot preserve the current standards temporarily while conducting a new, sequential determination-and-rule process?

### Leverage Point 2: The proposal needs a clearer answer on the health-protection floor

**Observed Fact**

The notice cites authority indicating revised NPDWRs must "maintain, or provide for greater, protection of the health of persons."

**Inference**

EPA likely believes the original procedural defect changes the analysis, but the proposal should explain that explicitly because the rule is undeniably deregulatory in practical effect.

**Useful comment question**

How does EPA reconcile this rescission with the statutory and case-law principle that revisions may not reduce protection?

### Leverage Point 3: The public record is still too summary-level on costs, benefits, and distribution

**Observed Fact**

EPA cites savings and foregone-benefit figures, plus additional nonquantifiable effects, but the available text does not fully unpack them.

**Inference**

Commenters have a strong basis to demand the underlying methodology, system counts, uncertainty treatment, and community-level consequences before EPA finalizes the rollback.

**Useful comment question**

Please place in the docket the full modeling and assumptions supporting the cited savings, foregone benefits, and any distributional consequences across public water systems and populations served.

### Leverage Point 4: Alternatives analysis appears underdeveloped

**Observed Fact**

EPA says it considered alternatives but is not proposing them.

**Inference**

That leaves the record open to criticism that EPA selected the most deregulatory option without enough public-facing comparison against narrower remedies.

**Useful comment question**

What alternative corrective pathways did EPA evaluate, and why were they rejected in favor of full rescission?

## Bottom line

The proposal presents a substantial legal argument that EPA mishandled the sequencing of the 2024 PFAS rule for PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and the hazard-index mixture. The more vulnerable part of the record is not the existence of a legal theory; it is the underexplained leap from that theory to immediate rescission of recently finalized health protections. High-value comments should press EPA on remedy, alternatives, transparency of the cost-benefit record, and the practical consequences for systems and communities that were already preparing to comply.
