## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Claim: Faster SDP spending growth justifies tighter payment caps and fiscal guardrails.
Underlying assumption: The growth CMS observes mainly reflects problematic financing incentives rather than benign coverage, wage, or access-related policy choices.
Failure scenario: Spending increased for mixed reasons, including legitimate access support, and the new caps cut too broadly across both high-risk and low-risk arrangements.
Procedural implication: Commenters can ask CMS to separate suspected abusive growth from access-supporting growth using clearer service- and provider-specific evidence.

Claim: Medicare is the right benchmark for the new payment caps.
Underlying assumption: Medicare rates are a sufficiently neutral and administrable ceiling for Medicaid SDPs and targeted practitioner payments.
Failure scenario: Medicare rates may understate appropriate Medicaid payment levels for some services, providers, or geographies, especially where Medicaid patient mix or safety-net financing differs.
Procedural implication: Commenters can request more service-by-service justification for why Medicare is the right benchmark and when State-plan rates are a better fallback.

Claim: The 110 percent cap for non-expansion States is an appropriate statutory implementation choice.
Underlying assumption: The statute's special treatment for non-expansion States should be read as a narrow numeric exception rather than as evidence of broader contextual differences.
Failure scenario: The distinction may be under- or over-inclusive relative to actual provider payment conditions and access risks.
Procedural implication: Commenters can press for clearer explanation of what practical conditions the expansion / non-expansion split is meant to capture.

Claim: The State-plan-rate fallback is appropriate where no published Medicare rate exists.
Underlying assumption: State plan approved rates are a sufficiently reviewed and defensible proxy when Medicare lacks a comparable rate.
Failure scenario: Some State plan rates may themselves be weak proxies for access-supporting payment levels, especially if those rates lag market conditions or differ sharply across States.
Procedural implication: Commenters can ask CMS to explain when the fallback may understate access needs and whether alternative comparators were considered.

Claim: Narrowing prior approval for some SDPs after 2028 will still preserve adequate oversight.
Underlying assumption: Documentation requirements in contracts, rate certifications, and post-submission materials can substitute for some ex ante review.
Failure scenario: Less pre-approval review could reduce early detection of problematic payment structures or one-provider classes.
Procedural implication: Commenters can ask CMS what monitoring triggers, audit routines, or retrospective checks will replace the foregone up-front scrutiny.

Claim: The rule will improve fiscal integrity without materially harming access to care.
Underlying assumption: Reducing payment ceilings mainly removes excessive or distorted payments rather than necessary support for fragile provider networks.
Failure scenario: Hospitals, AMCs, nursing facilities, and practitioners in some States may treat the payment reductions as a real access shock.
Procedural implication: Commenters can request more explicit access-risk analysis and State / provider-type sensitivity ranges.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

- The proposal would replace the 2024 managed-care SDP ceiling at the average commercial rate for key services with caps pegged to Medicare or, where Medicare lacks a rate, the Medicaid State plan rate.
- For the four services identified by the 2025 WFTC legislation, the proposed cap is `100 percent` of the total published Medicare payment rate in expansion States and `110 percent` in non-expansion States.
- The rule would also extend a Medicare-anchored payment-limit framework beyond those four services as part of CMS's broader fiscal-integrity policy.
- CMS proposes to create payment-limit rules for certain targeted Medicaid practitioner payments in fee-for-service, not only for managed care SDPs.
- The proposal would revise which SDPs require written prior approval and would leave some provider-class questions for later rulemaking.
- The notice says SDP spending was projected at `$97.8 billion` in FY 2024, `$124.3 billion` in FY 2025, and `$144.6 billion` in FY 2026 absent further change.

### Inference

- This is a materially consequential Medicaid payment-policy proposal, not a narrow technical cleanup.
- CMS is trying to move the system away from payment ceilings tied to commercial rates and toward a more standardized Medicare-based benchmark, while also tightening the policy justification for supplemental payment structures.
- The proposal uses fiscal integrity as its primary frame, but its real-world effects would likely be felt through changed provider revenue expectations and State financing choices.

Evidence Quotes:
- "This proposed rule describes alternatives to modify the limit on the total payment rate and other requirements for State directed payments in Medicaid managed care."
- "the total payment rate limit specified in section 71116(a) of the WFTC legislation for SDPs ... is 100 percent of the total published Medicare payment rate for an expansion State, or 110 percent ... for a non-expansion State."
- "we estimate that absent any changes to existing regulations ... SDP spending is projected to be $97.8 billion ... $124.3 billion ... and $144.6 billion"

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

Regulatory objective:
- Improve fiscal integrity and reduce payment arrangements that CMS believes can reward financing tactics instead of beneficiary access or quality.

Problem definition:
- SDP spending has grown rapidly.
- Some arrangements may use provider taxes or intergovernmental transfers in ways CMS sees as distorting State incentives.
- The existing average-commercial-rate framework may allow payment ceilings that CMS now views as too high or too weakly connected to statutory objectives.

Data used:
- CMS Office of the Actuary spending projections for SDPs.
- Prior SDP preprints and CMS review experience.
- Medicare payment schedules and State plan approved rates.
- Supplemental payment expenditure reporting that CMS acknowledges is incomplete for some provider categories.

Analytical method:
- Use statutory direction from the 2025 WFTC legislation for the four named services.
- Extend similar Medicare-based logic to other managed-care SDPs and certain FFS targeted practitioner payments.
- Use documentation, prior-approval rules, and definitional changes to shape which arrangements receive closer review.

Alternatives considered:
- Different ways to define provider classes.
- Whether and how far to require prior approval for various fee schedule SDPs.
- Continued future rulemaking rather than immediate resolution for some provider-class issues.

Agency conclusion:
- Medicare-based caps plus narrower pathways for some high-payment structures will improve transparency, stewardship, and consistency across Medicaid payment arrangements.

Missing links:
- The notice does not fully separate abusive or distorted arrangements from access-supporting arrangements before applying broad benchmark changes.
- CMS relies on growth in aggregate SDP spending as a justification signal, but the record is less clear on how much of that growth reflects harmful rather than policy-driven expansion.
- The proposal acknowledges downstream provider-impact uncertainty while still making broad fiscal-integrity claims.

Unsupported premises:
- That Medicare is generally the right limiting benchmark across heterogeneous Medicaid provider and service contexts.
- That post-2028 oversight changes will still catch problematic one-provider or otherwise distorted arrangements with comparable effectiveness.

Logical jumps:
- From aggregate SDP growth to the conclusion that lower or more standardized caps are the appropriate remedy across very different services and States.
- From an RFA conclusion focused on direct regulation of States to a practical assumption that indirect provider impacts need not be disaggregated more rigorously.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

### Observed Fact

- The rule is technically complex in an administrative and payment-policy sense rather than a scientific or engineering sense.
- It links managed-care actuarial-soundness rules, State plan rates, Medicare payment schedules, SDP preprints, and FFS targeted practitioner payments into one benchmark structure.
- CMS also proposes timing rules to align State plan rate years with Medicare rate years for certain targeted practitioner payments.

### Inference

- The proposal is internally coherent at a high level: cap payments more tightly, standardize the benchmark, and preserve documentation pathways.
- The most difficult coherence issue is not the legal architecture itself, but whether one benchmark family can fairly represent very different provider classes and service types.

Potential technical weak points:
- Differences between Medicare pricing and Medicaid network realities may be more consequential than the summary narrative suggests.
- The provider-class issue remains underdeveloped for a rule that is partly motivated by concern over narrow or distorted provider classes.

Unknown:

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

1. The Medicare-based caps could reduce payments for some providers that States were using to maintain access or specialized service availability.
2. The non-expansion-State distinction could become a rough proxy that does not map well onto actual service-specific access conditions.
3. Narrower prior approval could reduce early detection of problematic SDP structures, especially those involving very small provider classes.
4. The State-plan fallback could lock in historically low or outdated State rates for services lacking a Medicare comparator.
5. Fiscal savings may be easier to estimate than access losses, causing the record to overweight stewardship benefits relative to care-delivery consequences.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

Potential incentives:
- States may redesign SDPs to fit within Medicare-based caps rather than using commercial-rate benchmarks.
- Providers and State partners may have stronger incentives to frame payment proposals around documented access or quality goals rather than broader financing objectives.
- States may shift more effort toward services or structures that avoid prior approval while still complying with documentation rules.
- Hospitals, AMCs, nursing facilities, and practitioners may press for narrower application or exceptions where Medicare is viewed as an inadequate benchmark.

Inference:
- The proposal reallocates bargaining power and documentation incentives, not just payment ceilings.
- It may also encourage more strategic use of provider classification and rate-design choices unless CMS's final oversight structure stays clear and credible.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

Inference:
