# Phase 1 — Docket Integrity Check

**Observed Fact**

The selected document is a notice of proposed rulemaking, not an extension, reopening, correction, or withdrawal notice. The notice proposes to amend 26 CFR part 300 to reduce the IRS portion of the enrolled agent special enrollment examination (EA SEE) fee from $99 per part to $66 per part.

The prepared metadata shows one supporting related document and one ingested supporting material:

* `IRS-2026-0464-0002` — same-title interim final rule, ingested from PDF and HTML.

The supporting interim final rule contains the cost methodology, the biennial review discussion, the fee calculations, and the good-cause explanation that are only summarized or cross-referenced in the NPRM.

The available record therefore includes:

* the proposed regulatory action and comment invitation in the selected NPRM;
* the operative fee calculation and analytic explanation in the ingested interim final rule;
* special analyses covering Executive Order 12866, the Regulatory Flexibility Act, UMRA, and federalism.

**Classification**

```
Full analytical spine present
```

**Inference**

The selected document is the correct substantive NPRM for comment purposes. No docket override is needed. The supporting interim final rule was already ingested and is sufficient for a defensible summary, so there is no attachment-related stop condition.

## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim

The full cost of IRS oversight for the EA SEE is now $66 per part.

**Underlying assumption**

The updated timekeeping methodology and staffing projections capture the real direct and indirect cost of oversight with reasonable accuracy.

**Failure scenario**

If the revised timekeeping method understated actual staff time, omitted recurring oversight tasks, or shifted costs out of the program, the fee may no longer reflect full cost recovery under IOAA and OMB Circular A-25.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask Treasury and IRS to explain the methodology change in more detail and show why it still satisfies full-cost principles.

### Claim

The increase in examinee volume helps distribute fixed costs enough to reduce the user fee.

**Underlying assumption**

The three-year historical exam-part volume is a reasonable proxy for near-term demand.

**Failure scenario**

If volumes fall materially after the contractor price increase or because examinees respond to higher all-in testing costs, the per-part cost assumption may prove too low.

**Procedural implication**

The final rule could explain whether IRS expects demand stability despite the contractor fee jumping from $168 to $251 per part.

### Claim

The rule will not have a significant economic impact on a substantial number of small entities.

**Underlying assumption**

The relevant burden falls mainly on individual examinees rather than businesses, and any spillover to small firms employing enrolled agents is minor.

**Failure scenario**

If small tax practices or bookkeeping firms regularly sponsor employees through the exam process, the higher total out-of-pocket cost could affect hiring, credentialing, or reimbursement policies more than the RFA discussion suggests.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask for a clearer explanation of how IRS evaluated the total fee increase experienced by examinees once the contractor charge is included.

### Claim

The public-interest case for immediate interim-final implementation is adequate because charging the old higher IRS fee would be unnecessary.

**Underlying assumption**

Reducing the IRS fee before comments is less procedurally problematic because it lowers, rather than raises, the government portion of the charge.

**Failure scenario**

If the record does not adequately explain the same-day interim final rule structure or the practical consequences of the contractor increase, commenters may argue that the agency should have more fully justified the good-cause approach.

**Procedural implication**

The final rule should respond directly to any comments about the use of an interim final rule and the public's opportunity to comment on the all-in fee burden.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

The proposal would revise 26 CFR 300.4 to reduce the IRS user fee for each part of the enrolled agent special enrollment examination from $99 to $66.

The rule concerns only the IRS portion of the fee. The record separately states that the third-party contractor administering the exam will charge $251 per part for the May 2026 to February 2027 testing period.

The stated reasons for the lower IRS fee are:

* a 2025 biennial review under OMB Circular A-25;
* a change in timekeeping methodology that lowered estimated program expense;
* increased exam volume that spreads fixed oversight costs across more exam parts.

Evidence quotes:

> "As a result of the review, the IRS determined that its full cost for overseeing the EA SEE is now $66 per part."

> "The decrease in the user fee is primarily attributable to a change in timekeeping methodology, which resulted in a smaller estimated expense for administering the EA SEE program."

> "Additionally, there has been an increase in the number of exam takers, further distributing the fixed costs related to administering the exam."

### Inference

This is a relatively narrow cost-recovery NPRM. The central policy question is not whether the examination should exist, but whether the government has adequately shown that $66 is the correct IRS-side fee and whether the record gives enough practical context about the much larger contractor-side increase that examinees will experience in the same testing cycle.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

```
Regulatory objective
↓

↓

↓

↓

↓
Alternatives considered
Implicitly retain the existing fee, increase it, or use a different cost estimate; the record does not discuss many explicit alternatives because the agency treats full-cost recovery as the controlling principle
↓
Agency conclusion

```

**Missing links**

* The notice identifies a change in timekeeping methodology but does not describe the old and new methods in detail.
* The record quantifies the IRS portion of the fee reduction but gives limited public-facing analysis of the all-in candidate cost after the contractor increase.
* The rule explains that no lower contractor bid was available, but it does not analyze how the total exam price may affect access to the enrolled-agent credential.

**Observed Fact**

The record includes a concrete cost table, a stated overhead rate, projected total labor and benefits, other direct costs, historical exam-part counts, and the resulting per-part calculation. It also explains that the contractor charge is set separately by contract.

**Inference**

The strongest comment opportunities are about transparency and practical impact, not about an obvious absence of any cost reasoning.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

### Alternatives Evaluated

The record meaningfully addresses:

* keeping the EA SEE as a user-fee-supported service under IOAA and OMB Circular A-25;
* using current cost-accounting estimates rather than leaving the older $99 IRS fee in place;
* maintaining the separate contractor fee outside the IRS user-fee calculation.

### Plausible Alternatives Not Fully Analyzed

1. A fuller explanation of alternative volume assumptions if exam participation falls.
2. A discussion of whether smoothing or phasing the combined fee impact would better preserve access to the credentialing process.
3. A more detailed explanation of alternative timekeeping approaches and why the revised method better measures full cost.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Fact**

The cost calculation is internally coherent on its face:

* projected labor and benefits over FY 2026-2028: $3,505,101;
* projected travel, training, and supplies over the same period: $48,548;
* projected overhead: $2,205,409;
* projected total cost: $5,759,058;
* projected exam parts: 86,694;
* resulting cost per part: $66.43, rounded to $66.

**Inference**

The math presented in the record is straightforward and readable. The main technical question is not arithmetic, but whether the inputs, especially the revised timekeeping method and forward-looking volume assumptions, are sufficiently explained.

## Step 7 — Statutory Fit

**Observed Fact**

The NPRM relies on 31 U.S.C. 9701 and OMB Circular A-25. The record frames the fee as payment for a government service that confers a specific benefit on identifiable recipients: the opportunity to qualify for enrolled-agent status through the exam.

**Inference**

The statutory fit appears strong. This rule does not seem to stretch IRS authority beyond standard IOAA fee-setting principles. The more realistic commenter leverage is on whether the agency has shown the correct fee amount, not on whether it may charge a fee at all.

## Step 8 — Distributional and Equity Review

**Observed Fact**

The notice does not contain a substantial distributional analysis. It focuses on the fee as applying to individual exam takers and says only individuals, not businesses, can be enrolled agents. It does not discuss effects by income, geography, race, veteran status, disability, or access-to-profession considerations.

**Inference**

The absence of a deeper distributional analysis is understandable for a narrow fee rule, but commenters could still develop a useful record on whether higher all-in exam costs disproportionately affect lower-income entrants, career switchers, rural candidates, or small-firm employees seeking a credential that expands taxpayer representation capacity.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

1. **Cost-estimation mismatch.** The revised timekeeping methodology may undercount actual oversight effort.

2. **Demand overprediction.** Historic exam volume may not hold once candidates face a substantially higher contractor fee.

3. **Access effects obscured by fee segmentation.** The government fee drops, but examinees experience a much higher combined price because the contractor fee rises sharply.

4. **Thin response to comments on the interim-final structure.** If the final rule does not engage comments on the use of immediate effectiveness, the procedural record could remain underexplained.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Observed Fact**

The rule reduces the IRS component of the exam fee while the contractor raises its own component. The agency states that the contractor fee cannot be renegotiated under the current contract.

**Inference**

The proposal creates mixed incentives. IRS can say it is correcting its own fee to match lower oversight cost, but candidates deciding whether to pursue the credential will respond to the total exam price, not only to the federal portion. That makes the contractor increase highly relevant to the practical impact of the rule even if it is formally outside the IRS fee calculation.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Strong support**

* Legal authority for a user fee under IOAA and OMB Circular A-25.
* The basic arithmetic connecting projected costs to a lower per-part IRS fee.
* The explanation that the contractor fee is set through a separate procurement process.

**Moderate support**

* The use of a three-year historical exam volume to estimate future exam parts.
* The conclusion that the reduced IRS fee reflects full cost recovery rather than a policy subsidy.

**Weak or incomplete support**

* The explanation of what changed in the timekeeping methodology.
* The practical effect of the combined IRS plus contractor fee on exam access and candidate behavior.
* The assertion that small-entity effects are not significant once total candidate costs are considered.

## Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

1. **Methodology change detail.** The record says the main reason for the lower IRS fee is a different timekeeping methodology, but it does not describe the methodological change in a way a reader can independently evaluate.

2. **All-in cost context.** The notice gives the contractor fee numbers, but the discussion of impacts remains focused on the IRS portion of the charge instead of the total amount a candidate must pay.

3. **Demand sensitivity.** The rule does not discuss whether the large contractor fee increase could affect exam-part volume and thereby the reliability of the cost-per-part denominator.

4. **Professional access effects.** The record does not discuss whether higher total exam costs could affect entry into the enrolled-agent profession, especially for candidates with limited resources.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

**Low vulnerability: statutory authority.** The legal basis for charging a user fee appears conventional and well supported.

**Low-to-moderate vulnerability: reasoned explanation completeness.** The agency provides a calculation, but the final record would be stronger if it more clearly explains the timekeeping-method change and addresses comments about the all-in testing price.

**Low-to-moderate vulnerability: procedural compliance narrative.** Because the NPRM is paired with a same-day interim final rule, the final rule should respond carefully to comments about the good-cause rationale and the practical impact of immediate implementation.

# Phase 3 — Comment Generation

## Step 14 — Comment Themes

### Theme 1: Ask for more detail on the revised timekeeping methodology

**Observed Fact:** The record says the fee reduction is primarily attributable to a change in timekeeping methodology.

**Inference:** A commenter can reasonably ask what changed, why the new method is more accurate, and whether any recurring oversight functions were reclassified or omitted.

### Theme 2: Ask the agency to address the total price candidates face

**Observed Fact:** The IRS fee would fall to $66, but the contractor fee for the coming test period is $251 per part.

**Inference:** The practical candidate burden may increase even as the IRS portion decreases, so the record would benefit from a discussion of access and participation effects.

### Theme 3: Ask whether historic exam volume remains a sound denominator

**Observed Fact:** The per-part calculation uses historical exam-part volumes from FY 2022 through FY 2024.

**Inference:** Commenters can ask whether those volumes remain representative after the contractor price increase.

## Step 15 — Procedural Concern Score

**Score:** 2 out of 10

**Rationale**

This is a relatively narrow and data-backed fee rule. The record is not empty; it includes a cost table and cites the governing cost-recovery framework. The main concerns are modest and mostly about transparency and practical context, especially the limited explanation of the revised timekeeping methodology and the interaction between the lower IRS fee and the higher contractor fee.

## Step 16 — Suggested Evidence to Submit

Commenters could strengthen the record by submitting:

* evidence from EA candidates or preparer firms on how total testing costs affect whether candidates sit for all three parts;
* data or experience on reimbursement practices at small tax firms, payroll firms, bookkeeping firms, and solo practices;
* analysis of whether the contractor fee increase is likely to change exam volume enough to affect the IRS denominator;
* comments requesting additional disclosure about the revised timekeeping methodology and oversight tasks included in the $66 figure.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

1. Request a clearer explanation of the revised timekeeping methodology and how it changed the cost estimate.
2. Ask Treasury and IRS to discuss the all-in price candidates must pay, not only the IRS portion of that price.
3. Ask whether the projected exam-part volume should be updated or stress-tested given the contractor fee increase.
4. Ask whether the final rule should include a clearer explanation of how the agency considered access-to-credential concerns for lower-resource candidates.

## Step 18 — Comment Drafting Notes

A constructive public comment should recognize that the IRS appears to be correcting its own fee downward rather than imposing a new burden through the government-side charge. The most effective comments will likely focus on making the final record more transparent and more realistic about total candidate cost.

Potential requests:

* "Please explain in more detail what changed in the IRS timekeeping methodology and why the revised method better reflects full cost."
* "Please discuss the total amount candidates must pay for each exam part, including the contractor charge, and whether that total may affect exam participation."
* "Please explain whether the historical exam-volume denominator remains reliable after the contractor fee increase for the May 2026 to February 2027 testing period."
* "Please clarify whether the agency considered impacts on entry into the enrolled-agent profession for lower-resource candidates and small firms sponsoring employee credentialing."
