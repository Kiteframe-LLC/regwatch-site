### Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

**Claim**
The proposal will create clarity and uniformity across federal joint-employer analysis.

**Underlying assumption**
The proposed multi-factor approach captures a stable common denominator across courts and will be clearer in practice than the current mix of standards.

**Failure scenario**
If courts continue using materially different circuit-specific tests, the regulation may not deliver the promised uniformity even if it guides WHD investigators.

**Procedural implication**
Commenters can ask the Department to identify where circuit differences will remain and how regulated parties should handle conflict between the rule and contrary judicial formulations.

**Claim**
The proposed framework is closest to the statute as construed by courts.

**Underlying assumption**
The agency's synthesis of precedent is methodologically sound and representative rather than selective.

**Failure scenario**
If the cited cases support multiple plausible formulations, the agency may appear to be choosing among them without a reproducible method.

**Procedural implication**
That would weaken reasoned-explanation completeness and invite comments requesting a clearer mapping from precedent to each proposed factor.

**Claim**
Benefits and recurring cost savings will exceed familiarization costs.

**Underlying assumption**
Legal uncertainty materially increases compliance and litigation expense and the proposal will reduce that uncertainty.

**Failure scenario**
If affected entities still need counsel to navigate circuit law, or if worker disputes increase because the proposed standard changes coverage outcomes, cost savings may be overstated.

**Procedural implication**
The PRIA would benefit from sensitivity analysis and more explicit discussion of worker-side costs and transfer effects.

**Claim**
The same FLSA-centered analysis should govern FMLA and MSPA joint-employer determinations.

**Underlying assumption**
Shared statutory definitions outweigh contextual differences among those statutes.

**Failure scenario**
If FMLA leave-administration duties or MSPA agricultural labor contexts create distinctive risks, a single cross-referenced test may be under-tailored.

**Procedural implication**
A commenter can request statute-specific examples showing how the unified test works without erasing meaningful contextual differences.

### Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

**Observed Facts**
- The proposal would restore a general FLSA joint-employer analysis to part 791 after the Department has operated without joint-employer regulations there since 2021.
- It distinguishes vertical joint employment from horizontal joint employment.
- It would cross-reference the part 791 analysis into MSPA and FMLA regulations and make related agricultural cross-reference changes.
- It includes provisions addressing the relevance of certain business practices such as quality-control standards, legal-compliance requirements, and other common commercial arrangements.
- It includes a severability provision.

**Inference**
- This is primarily a legal-interpretive rule designed to steer enforcement, compliance advice, and court analysis rather than a rule imposing new reporting or engineering requirements.
- The policy stakes are still substantial because changing the governing joint-employer test can change who bears wage, leave, and agricultural-labor obligations.

**Evidence Quotes**
- "Additionally, the Department proposes to revise the regulations addressing joint employer status under MSPA and the FMLA to apply the analysis in part 791 when determining joint employer status under those statutes."
- "This NPRM does not contain a collection of information subject to OMB approval under the PRA."

### Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**  
Create a more uniform and judicially grounded joint-employer framework.

**Problem definition**  
Current conditions are described as fragmented and unclear because multiple institutions have articulated varying tests and standards.

**Data used**  
Statutory text, Supreme Court and appellate caselaw, the Department's rulemaking history, qualitative economic reasoning, entity-count and wage-rate inputs for familiarization costs, and high-level assumptions about compliance and litigation savings.

**Analytical method**  
Synthesize precedent into a multi-factor test, distinguish vertical and horizontal scenarios, then project costs and benefits qualitatively with one major quantified cost component.

**Alternatives considered**  
Readopt the 2020 rule, rely on the 2016 Administrator's Interpretation approach, and retain or adapt prior MSPA-related approaches.

**Agency conclusion**  
The proposed framework is said to be the best construction of the statutes, the most likely to promote clarity, and economically justified despite substantial one-time familiarization costs.

**Missing links / unsupported premises**
- The rule explains why it prefers this synthesis of precedent, but the method for translating a mixed body of cases into specific regulatory text is still not fully transparent.
- The PRIA quantifies familiarization costs but does not comparably quantify the worker-side or employer-side outcomes that drive the claimed net benefits.
- The cross-statute harmonization argument is plausible, but the notice would be stronger with more concrete FMLA and MSPA application examples.

### Step 4 — Analysis Obligations Review

**Adequate data:** Partially satisfied (`solicited_by_agency`)  
The NPRM uses legal history and some economic inputs, but it expressly admits it lacks data on how many businesses are or may be in joint-employment relationships.

**Transparent methodology:** Partially satisfied (`unacknowledged_gap`)  
The notice is transparent about legal sources and cost-estimation inputs, but less transparent about how it converts precedent and uncertainty into the bottom-line claim that benefits outweigh costs.

**Evaluation of reasonable alternatives:** Partially satisfied (`unacknowledged_gap`)  
Several alternatives are discussed, but narrower options between the current patchwork and the full proposed framework receive less development than headline alternatives.

**Explanation of analytical thresholds:** Partially satisfied (`unacknowledged_gap`)  
The proposal explains some relevance judgments for factors and business practices, but line-drawing around what is not probative versus what remains probative could still use more examples.

**Consideration of foreseeable impacts:** Partially satisfied (`solicited_by_agency`)  
The Department discusses costs, benefits, transfers, small-entity effects, and federalism, but it also invites further data because many effects remain difficult to quantify.

### Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

**Alternatives evaluated**
- Readopting the 2020 rule.
- Using the 2016 Administrator's Interpretation approach.
- Retaining existing MSPA-related concepts rather than full harmonization.
- RFA-style small-entity alternatives, which the Department rejects.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed in depth**
- A narrower FLSA-only final rule paired with additional examples before extending the framework to FMLA and MSPA.
- A more explicit safe-harbor or example-based clarification package for common commercial arrangements.
- A version that includes a commitment to retrospective review after a defined implementation period.

**Evidence suggesting feasibility**
- The Department already uses examples and distinguishes commercial practices in the proposed text.
- The notice repeatedly requests comments and data, suggesting the agency recognizes factual uncertainty that could support staged or narrower approaches.

### Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Fact**
This is not a technical modeling rule in the engineering sense. Its coherence question is whether the legal categories, factors, and exclusions fit together consistently.

**Inference**
The proposal is generally internally coherent: it distinguishes vertical from horizontal joint employment, explains why some business practices are not probative, and attempts to align related statutes. The weaker area is not logical contradiction, but how much practical clarity regulated parties will actually gain once different judicial approaches continue to exist.

### Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact**
The NPRM grounds itself in FLSA sections 3(d), 3(e), and 3(g), and discusses Rutherford Food, Falk, Bonnette, later appellate decisions, the 2020 rule, the 2021 rescission, and post-*Loper Bright* interpretive weight.

**Inference**
The legal grounding is substantial. The main litigation risk is not absence of authority, but whether the agency has adequately explained why this particular synthesis of precedent is preferable and sufficiently attentive to worker-protective purposes across all three statutes.

### Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

**Observed Fact**
This is a standard NPRM with a comment deadline, submission instructions, a severability discussion, PRA treatment, PRIA, IRFA, UMRA review, and federalism analysis.

**Inference**
Procedurally, the notice looks normal for a consequential labor-policy proposal. The main administrative-law pressure points are substantive adequacy and explanation, not unusual process.

### Step 9 — Failure Modes

- Businesses may assume the rule provides more certainty than courts will actually honor where circuit doctrine diverges.
- Workers may face a harder time identifying who is liable if the rule's emphasis on non-probative business practices is read too broadly.
- FMLA and MSPA contexts may reveal statute-specific complications that the harmonized framework does not fully anticipate.
- Litigation could still increase in the short term if parties test the boundaries of the new factor set and the new exclusions.

### Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Observed Fact**
The proposal says some common business practices, including quality-control standards and general legal-compliance requirements, do not make joint-employer status more or less likely.

**Inference**
That may reduce incentives for over-deterrence in ordinary commercial relationships, but it also creates an incentive for parties to characterize operational control as mere quality control or brand protection. Clearer examples distinguishing the two would strengthen the rule.

### Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Strongest-supported areas**
- Statutory text and legal history.
- Explanation of why the Department wants a vertical/horizontal distinction.
- Quantification of one-time familiarization costs using enumerated inputs.

**Moderately supported areas**
- Discussion of alternatives.
- Small-entity analysis.
- Transfer-effects discussion.

**Weakest-supported areas**
- Quantified or strongly demonstrated estimates of reduced litigation and compliance costs.
- Quantified prevalence of joint-employment relationships.
- Concrete evidence on how harmonization will work across FMLA and MSPA edge cases.

### Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

- No separate workbook or memorandum was included in the supplied materials showing how the Department modeled recurring cost savings.
- The notice admits limited data on the prevalence of joint-employment relationships; commenters could help by supplying industry-specific prevalence estimates.
- The record would be stronger with side-by-side illustrations showing how identical facts would be analyzed under FLSA, FMLA, and MSPA after the proposed revisions.
- The rule would also benefit from more examples showing when contractual oversight becomes employment control.

### Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

**Potential unacknowledged weakness**
The NPRM says benefits and cost savings will significantly outweigh familiarization costs while also conceding major data limitations and relying heavily on qualitative estimates.

**Potential unacknowledged weakness**
The proposal's claim to provide nationwide uniformity may overstate what a rule can achieve where judicial tests remain circuit-specific and *Skidmore*-style weight turns on persuasiveness.

**Deliberate solicitation**
The Department expressly requests comments, studies, and data on prevalence, impacts, and quantification. That solicitation is normal at the NPRM stage and should not itself be treated as a defect.

**Inference**
The best public comments are likely to focus on making the rule more defensible and more precise, rather than claiming the proposal is procedurally invalid on its face.

### Step 16 — Evidence Requests

- Request the economic workbook, assumptions table, and any sensitivity analysis underlying the claim that recurring savings exceed one-time costs.
- Request examples showing how identical fact patterns would be analyzed under FLSA, FMLA, and MSPA after harmonization.
- Request the agency's method for selecting and synthesizing judicial precedents into proposed factor language and non-probative business-practice categories.
- Request any enforcement data, case summaries, or internal analyses showing where the absence of a part 791 rule since 2021 produced inconsistent or inefficient outcomes.

### Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

**1. Cost-benefit transparency**
Ask the Department to show its sensitivity analysis and explain how its net-benefit conclusion changes under lower savings assumptions.

**2. Cross-statute fit**
Ask for statute-specific examples and discussion of whether harmonization may obscure distinct duties under FMLA and MSPA.

**3. Real-world boundary examples**
Ask for more examples distinguishing ordinary commercial oversight from employment control, especially for franchising, staffing, and subcontracting arrangements.

**4. Judicial divergence**
Ask the Department to discuss where circuit differences will remain and how regulated parties should handle those conflicts.

### Step 18 — Decision Brief

**Observed Fact**
This NPRM is a serious, fully formed proposal rather than a placeholder. The supplied record is sufficient to summarize without pausing for attachments.

**Inference**
The strongest constructive comments will not attack the rule for lacking any legal basis. They will press the Department to better document why its chosen synthesis of precedent is superior, to provide more concrete cross-statute examples, and to support its claimed cost savings with more transparent evidence.

**Bottom line**
The proposal is comment-worthy because its main vulnerabilities are fixable record-quality problems: qualitative benefit claims, limited prevalence data, and incomplete explanation of how a uniform standard will work across different statutes and judicial settings.
