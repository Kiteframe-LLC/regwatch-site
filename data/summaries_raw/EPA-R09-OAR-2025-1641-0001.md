## Phase 1 — Docket Integrity Check

**Classification:** Full analytical spine present

**Observed Fact**

The controller-selected document, `EPA-R09-OAR-2025-1641-0001`, is a substantive NPRM titled "Air Quality State Implementation Plans; Approvals and Promulgations: California; San Diego County Air Pollution Control District; Landfill Flares." The notice proposes conditional approval of SDCAPCD Rule 69.7 for inclusion in the California SIP.

The refreshed export packet for this document appended supporting text from eight related docket materials. The bundled record includes EPA's Technical Support Document, district background material describing the rule's origin and cost-effectiveness rationale, commitment-letter discussion, and supporting materials such as comparative and workshop analysis references.

The notice identifies one specific SIP deficiency: Rule 69.7 lets operators use specified ASTM methods or the "most current version" of those methods. EPA says that feature allows use of alternative test methods not reviewed and approved by EPA, which undermines enforceability under CAA section 110(a)(2)(A) and 40 CFR 51.212(c).

The same record also shows broader policy context that EPA itself is not fully resolving in this action. The TSD says San Diego is a Severe ozone nonattainment area and that Rule 69.7 was adopted to fulfill a prior commitment to adopt a landfill-flare NOx rule, but EPA says its present action "focuses on the approvability of Rule 69.7 for inclusion into the SIP under CAA section 110" and that any RACT determination will be made in a future rulemaking.

**Inference**

This is not a thin notice. The available record is strong enough to evaluate EPA's immediate conditional-approval theory because the preamble and appended support docs expose the key defect, the state and district commitments, the basic technical design of Rule 69.7, and the local agency's comparison-based rationale for choosing the rule's emission limit. The main public-comment opportunity is therefore less about finding a missing docket spine and more about testing whether EPA has explained the relationship between approvability, enforceability, and deferred RACT judgment clearly enough.

**Why that matters**

For commenters, the strongest leverage is not to dispute that EPA found a real deficiency. It is to ask whether EPA should say more about the practical consequences of approving a rule that the district touts as a RACT measure while EPA expressly declines to decide RACT adequacy here, and whether the rule's treatment of test methods, open flares, and future revisions leaves too much unresolved for a conditional approval.

## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Major claim: the commitment letters are enough to justify conditional approval

**Underlying assumption**

The district and CARB commitments are specific enough, credible enough, and close enough to the identified deficiency that EPA can reasonably conclude the defect will be cured within the required timeframe.

**Failure scenario**

The promised revision slips, narrows the fix, or resolves the cited ASTM-version issue while leaving adjacent enforceability concerns unaddressed.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask EPA to explain the precise minimum revision it expects and what would count as an adequate cure, so the conditional approval does not rest on an underspecified promise.

### Major claim: the ASTM "most current version" language is the only material defect preventing full approval

**Underlying assumption**

The rest of Rule 69.7's monitoring, testing, and compliance structure is sufficiently enforceable for SIP purposes once the method-version problem is fixed.

**Failure scenario**

Other parts of the rule, especially APCO-approved alternatives or open-flare treatment, also create implementation or enforceability ambiguity that EPA has not fully surfaced.

**Procedural implication**

The record would be stronger if EPA explained why it treats the ASTM-version issue as the decisive deficiency rather than one example of a broader enforceability concern.

### Major claim: it is reasonable to defer the RACT determination to a later action

**Underlying assumption**

CAA section 110 approvability can be evaluated cleanly now without materially prejudging the rule's future use in a Severe-area RACT SIP.

**Failure scenario**

Readers infer from this approval that EPA has effectively accepted the district's 0.06 lb/MMBtu limit as adequate RACT even though EPA says that judgment is still pending.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask EPA to draw a sharper line between present SIP approvability and future RACT adequacy so the final action does not blur the significance of later review.

### Major claim: the chosen NOx limit is justified even though it yields little immediate reduction

**Underlying assumption**

Backstop value, anti-backsliding logic, and future equipment replacement concerns are sufficient reasons to adopt and approve a rule whose own supporting record says existing flares already meet the limit.

**Failure scenario**

If existing units already comply, the practical weight of the rule may depend almost entirely on enforceability details and how future units or modifications are handled.

**Procedural implication**

That makes the clarity of testing, monitoring, and applicability boundaries more important than EPA's short notice text suggests.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

EPA proposes to conditionally approve SDCAPCD Rule 69.7 into the California SIP. The rule addresses NOx emissions from landfill gas flares at municipal solid waste landfills that meet the federal major-source threshold. The rule sets a NOx limit of 0.06 lb/MMBtu and a CO limit of 0.20 lb/MMBtu for enclosed flares, imposes operational and monitoring requirements, requires recordkeeping, and specifies source-test methods and schedules. EPA says the rule is mostly approvable but contains a SIP deficiency because it permits use of the "most current version" of certain ASTM methods instead of only EPA-reviewed method versions. EPA relies on commitment letters from SDCAPCD and CARB promising a revision by June 30, 2027, or within one year of final approval, whichever is sooner.

The appended supporting material adds several points that matter for public review. It says San Diego is a Severe ozone nonattainment area, that Rule 69.7 was adopted to fulfill a prior commitment to adopt a landfill-flare NOx rule, that the district compared the rule to other California flare rules, and that the district rejected a lower 0.025 lb/MMBtu limit as cost-prohibitive at an estimated $21 per pound of NOx reduced while describing the adopted 0.06 lb/MMBtu standard as costing about $0.31 per pound reduced. It also says existing flares already emit at or below the adopted NOx and CO limits.

### Inference

This NPRM is best understood as a limited enforceability approval action wrapped around a rule with larger future significance. EPA wants to secure SIP incorporation now while postponing the more politically and technically sensitive question of whether Rule 69.7 is adequate as RACT for a Severe ozone area. That means commenters have a concrete opening to ask EPA to explain what exactly this action does and does not resolve.

## Step 3 — Agency Argument Tree

**Regulatory objective**
Approve a district landfill-flare rule into the California SIP while preserving a path to cure one identified test-method deficiency.

**Problem definition**
San Diego adopted Rule 69.7 to control NOx from landfill gas flares, but the submitted version contains method-language that lets operators use unreviewed future ASTM versions, undermining enforceability.

**Data used**
Notice text, EPA's TSD, district rule summary, commitment letters, district comparative and cost-effectiveness discussion, and the rule's testing, monitoring, and compliance provisions.

**Analytical method**
EPA evaluates the rule for SIP approvability under CAA section 110 and 40 CFR 51.212(c), identifies a discrete test-method defect, and uses CAA section 110(k)(4) conditional approval authority because the state and district promise to revise the rule.

**Alternatives considered**
Full approval, disapproval, and conditional approval are the implicit federal options. The local supporting record also discusses a more stringent 0.025 lb/MMBtu alternative but rejects it on cost and comparability grounds.

**Agency conclusion**
Rule 69.7 largely satisfies relevant SIP requirements, but full approval is precluded by the ASTM-version problem; conditional approval is appropriate because state and district commitments would cure that deficiency.

### Missing links / unsupported premises / logical jumps

The main jumps are:

1. from one identified test-method flaw to confidence that the rest of the rule is enforceable enough for conditional approval,
2. from local comparative and cost-effectiveness reasoning to EPA's decision to postpone any RACT conclusion,
3. from "existing flares already comply" to the claim that the rule still meaningfully advances SIP integrity,
4. from general commitment letters to a sufficiently concrete description of what final revised language EPA expects.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact**

EPA grounds the action in CAA section 110(a)(2)(A), 40 CFR 51.212(c), and conditional approval authority under CAA section 110(k)(4). The notice says a final conditional approval would convert to a disapproval path if the promised revision is not submitted on time.

**Inference**

The legal vulnerability is not an obvious absence of statutory authority. It is record precision: whether EPA has explained clearly enough why the identified method-version flaw is both serious enough to preclude full approval and narrow enough to justify conditional approval rather than a broader critique of enforceability.

**Unknown**

## Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

**Classification:** Normal

**Observed Fact**

This is a standard EPA SIP NPRM. The notice states the proposal, identifies the comment deadline, cites the relevant legal authorities, describes the deficiency, and says EPA will consider public comments before taking final action.

**Inference**

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

The promised revision could fix the quoted ASTM-version issue narrowly while leaving adjacent enforceability ambiguities untouched.

Readers may overread this approval as an EPA endorsement of the rule's RACT adequacy even though EPA says that judgment is deferred.

If existing flares already comply and no immediate reductions are expected, weak monitoring or unclear applicability boundaries could matter more than the headline emission limit.

Open-flare provisions may create confusion because the rule applies broadly to landfill gas flares but some standards and source-testing provisions are described as inapplicable to existing open flares.

Reliance on APCO-added monitoring for open flares could create practical variability that the NPRM does not explain in much public-facing detail.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Observed Fact**

The district support materials say the adopted standard is already met by existing flares and that the rule's immediate value is preventing future backsliding and ensuring testing and monitoring requirements are in place.

**Inference**

That means the real compliance incentive turns less on forcing new controls today and more on preserving regulatory baselines, equipment choices, and test-method discipline over time. When that is the case, enforceability details matter disproportionately, which supports asking EPA to be more precise about the cure it expects and about how open-flare monitoring discretion will work.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

### 1. Ask EPA to define the cure precisely

A constructive comment can ask EPA to quote or summarize the exact revision it expects so the public can evaluate whether the promised cure is limited to deleting "most current version" language or includes related enforceability cleanup.

### 2. Ask EPA to separate SIP approval from RACT judgment more clearly

Commenters can press EPA to say explicitly what conclusions should not be inferred from this action, especially because the district record frames Rule 69.7 as part of a Severe-area RACT response.

### 3. Ask EPA to explain practical coverage

The record would be more useful if EPA clearly described the covered facilities, the number of affected flares, and how enclosed and existing open flares are treated.

### 4. Ask EPA to justify why one defect is treated as dispositive

EPA should explain why the ASTM-version issue is the only material enforceability deficiency it found, rather than leaving readers to infer that other ambiguities were either accepted or overlooked.

### 5. Ask EPA to make the alternative-limit analysis more accessible

The appended support text references a rejected 0.025 lb/MMBtu alternative and concrete cost figures. EPA could strengthen the final record by surfacing that comparison more directly.

## Step 18 — Decision Brief

### Strongest leverage point

"This action focuses on the approvability of Rule 69.7 for inclusion into the SIP under CAA section 110. If relied upon to meet RACT, we will evaluate whether Rule 69.7 meets the applicable CAA requirements for RACT when acting on the District's RACT SIP submittal."

### Routine description situated in context

EPA often uses conditional approval when a SIP submission is mostly approvable but a state commits to cure a deficiency by a date certain. That is a normal tool. The practical question here is whether EPA has described the scope of the cure and the limits of this approval clearly enough, given that the underlying local record presents Rule 69.7 as part of San Diego's ozone-control program.

### Regulatory effectiveness / statutory compliance / procedural adequacy

The proposal is legally and procedurally plausible on its face, but the administrative record would be stronger if EPA more clearly explained the exact cure, the reason it treats one method-incorporation problem as the decisive defect, and the relationship between this approval and later RACT review.

### Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage

If commenters press these points effectively, EPA may issue a more precise final explanation of the promised rule revision, clarify what this action does not decide about RACT, and improve the public-facing explanation of practical coverage and enforceability. That would make the final SIP action easier to evaluate and less vulnerable to later confusion.
