## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim

Restricting recognition to the United States and reciprocal-trade or `MRA` countries would improve trustworthiness, integrity, reciprocity, and national security in the equipment authorization process.

**Underlying assumption:** Country-based reciprocity status is a useful proxy for lab reliability, certification integrity, and vulnerability to national-security or `IP`-theft risks.

**Failure scenario:** If many non-reciprocal-jurisdiction labs produce reliable work and existing ownership or control restrictions already address the highest-risk cases, then the proposal may use a broad location proxy that overreaches the actual problem.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask the FCC to distinguish between foreign-adversary risk, non-reciprocity as a trade matter, and actual testing-integrity evidence rather than blending them into one justification.

### Claim

The FCC should clarify ambiguous test-lab recognition rules by withdrawing recognition from already recognized labs in non-reciprocal jurisdictions.

**Underlying assumption:** Existing ambiguity is itself harmful enough that a clear, stricter rule is better than a narrower clarification or grandfathering approach.

**Failure scenario:** If the ambiguity could be resolved with tighter ownership, auditing, or phased recognition standards instead of categorical exclusion, then immediate withdrawal may be broader than necessary.

**Procedural implication:** The record would be stronger if the FCC explains why clarification requires a prohibition rather than a narrower revision.

### Claim

An added fee, longer review, or other interim burdens could accelerate transition away from non-reciprocal labs in a cost-effective way.

**Underlying assumption:** A meaningful price or delay signal would change behavior without creating disproportionate costs or supply-chain bottlenecks.

**Failure scenario:** If the `FCC` has not quantified the current dependence on affected labs, a fee such as `$20,000` per authorization could operate as an arbitrary penalty rather than a calibrated transition tool.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters can press for evidence on market structure, expected pass-through costs, and whether the fee bears any relationship to administrative costs or risk.

### Claim

The proposal should consider `IP`-protection differences across reciprocal and non-reciprocal jurisdictions.

**Underlying assumption:** Reciprocity status correlates meaningfully with practical intellectual-property protection during testing and certification.

**Failure scenario:** If `IP` risk depends more on contract controls, lab-specific security practices, or sector-specific law than on `MRA` status, then the proposal may be using the wrong variable.

**Procedural implication:** The FCC may need more tailored evidence if it wants `IP` risk to carry real weight in the final rule.

### Claim

Modernizing the equipment authorization database would both improve enforcement and reduce administrative burden.

**Underlying assumption:** Better data architecture and sharing would materially help `TCBs` and the FCC detect prohibited-entity problems without imposing offsetting compliance burdens.

**Failure scenario:** If the needed information is hard to standardize or if new reporting fields are poorly designed, modernization could add cost without producing better enforcement outcomes.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters can ask for a narrower statement of the actual data problems, candidate data elements, and success metrics before the FCC expands information collection.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

* The FCC proposes to stop recognizing test labs, laboratory accreditation bodies, and `TCBs` that are located in, or that conduct relevant work from, countries lacking a reciprocal `MRA` or trade agreement with the United States.
* The proposed rule text would add reciprocity-based location restrictions to `47 CFR 2.949`, `2.951`, and `2.960`.
* The notice also proposes a new line item in the FCC fee schedule for “Device Testing in Non-MRA Country” at `$20,000`, but presents it as part of the questions on possible transition mechanisms rather than a fully defended final choice.
* The FCC seeks comment on delayed implementation, phased recognition loss at renewal, enhanced auditing or post-market surveillance, longer review periods, and other mechanisms short of an immediate outright prohibition.
* The notice separately asks how to protect intellectual property during testing and certification and whether reciprocal trade arrangements meaningfully reduce `IP`-theft risk.
* The FCC also seeks comment on making equipment authorization records more machine-readable and on modernizing the authorization database to support enforcement and reduce administrative burden.
* The notice says comments are due on or before `June 15, 2026` and reply comments are due on or before `July 14, 2026`; the controller metadata's `comment_end_date` of `2026-07-15T03:59:59Z` appears to reflect that same reply deadline in `UTC`.

### Inference

This SFNPRM is a structurally important tightening proposal for the FCC's equipment-authorization ecosystem. The main leverage point for commenters is not whether national security or certification integrity matter. It is whether the FCC has shown that country-based reciprocity restrictions, possible punitive transition fees, and database modernization are sufficiently tailored, evidenced, and administrable to justify a broad rewrite.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

**Alternatives evaluated:** The notice evaluates an outright prohibition, delayed implementation, phaseout at renewal, additional fees, more rigorous review or auditing, and other transition incentives. It also keeps several earlier `FNPRM` issues open without adopting them.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed:** The available text does not visibly provide a strong comparison of narrower options such as enhanced auditing of higher-risk labs regardless of country, ownership-and-control based screening without full location bans, disclosure-plus-surveillance approaches, narrower product-category limits, or a pilot period before a permanent categorical exclusion.

**Evidence suggesting feasibility:** The FCC itself notes that its existing rules already address foreign-adversary ownership and direction, that ambiguity rather than explicit permission may explain current non-`MRA` test-lab recognition, and that alternatives such as fees, longer review, or phased renewal treatment are plausible enough to ask about. That suggests the final record would benefit from a more explicit alternatives matrix before the Commission picks a categorical endpoint.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

The proposal is coherent at a high level. If the FCC concludes that equipment-authorization integrity depends not just on entity-specific restrictions but also on testing and certification taking place in reciprocal jurisdictions with stronger accountability or comparable access for U.S. entities, then reciprocity-based recognition limits are an understandable policy direction.

The weaker point is proxy fit. Reciprocity is partly a trade-policy concept, partly a procedural concept, and only indirectly a lab-integrity or `IP`-security concept. The notice does not do much visible work to separate those dimensions. The same problem appears in the fee concept. A `$20,000` charge might be intended as a deterrent, a rough cost proxy, or a surveillance funding mechanism, but the available text does not show which theory actually drives the number.

**Observed Fact:** The proposed rule text would categorically add reciprocity-based location restrictions to the recognition rules for accreditation bodies, measurement facilities, and `TCBs`.

**Inference:** The proposal's internal logic is strongest if the FCC is really making a trade-and-security line-drawing judgment. It is weakest if the final record needs to show a tight empirical connection between reciprocity status and actual testing unreliability, because that connection is not yet visible in the prepared materials.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact:** The FCC cites Communications Act provisions, the `CALEA`, the Secure and Trusted Communications Networks Act of `2019`, and the Secure Equipment Act of `2021`.

**Observed Fact:** The notice is framed as a further `NPRM` and repeatedly asks for comment on costs, implementation timing, and alternative mechanisms rather than claiming those questions are already resolved.

**Inference:** The basic legal footing to regulate the equipment-authorization program appears substantial. The more exposed legal issue is not whether the FCC has any authority to act. It is whether the final agency explanation will adequately connect the chosen reciprocity-based restrictions and fee or review mechanisms to the statutory objectives with enough evidence and reasoned line drawing.

**Unknown:** The available export does not include a fuller legal analysis of how the FCC would defend the use of reciprocity or trade-agreement status as a categorical criterion if commenters show more tailored entity-level safeguards would address the same harms.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

* The FCC may rely on reciprocity status as a blunt proxy for risks that vary more by entity, ownership structure, contract terms, or product type.
* A categorical exclusion could create testing bottlenecks or higher costs if affected manufacturers cannot shift smoothly to U.S. or reciprocal-jurisdiction labs.
* A `$20,000` fee or prolonged review mechanism could function as an arbitrary penalty if not tied to actual administrative or risk-based reasoning.
* `IP`-protection concerns may be invoked broadly without evidence showing that reciprocity status predicts meaningful differences in test-stage theft risk.
* Database modernization could expand information collection without clear data standards or demonstrable enforcement gains.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

The proposal may create incentives for:

* manufacturers to shift testing and certification work toward U.S. and reciprocal-jurisdiction providers
* recognized labs and `TCBs` to restructure operations or ownership to remain within the FCC-recognized network
* firms to push for longer implementation periods if current capacity in reciprocal jurisdictions is limited
* commenters to argue for audit- and surveillance-based alternatives instead of categorical location bans
* the FCC to use data-system modernization as a companion enforcement tool even if the location-based restrictions are narrowed

These incentives do not make the proposal unsound, but they show why the FCC needs a more visible explanation of capacity, transition effects, and how the different policy levers fit together.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

The evidentiary strength is moderate from the `SFNPRM` alone. The FCC has a plausible narrative that reciprocity matters, that the current rules are at least ambiguous for non-`MRA` test labs, and that equipment-authorization security should not depend solely on ownership-based restrictions.

But the visible record is much stronger on policy concern than on calibration. The available materials do not show much quantitative support for how many recognized entities are implicated, what harms are actually associated with non-reciprocal jurisdictions, how any proposed fee level was chosen, or why more tailored tools would be insufficient. That leaves meaningful room for constructive comments focused on evidence, transition design, and line drawing.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

The most plausible vulnerability is not lack of baseline statutory authority. It is underexplained fit between the categorical line and the stated harms. A final rule could draw challenge if the FCC uses reciprocity status as a broad stand-in for security, integrity, and `IP` concerns without more evidence that narrower controls would fail and without a clearer explanation of fee levels, transition burdens, and capacity consequences. The strongest comment strategy is therefore to demand evidence, structured alternatives analysis, and clearer implementation logic rather than to deny the legitimacy of the FCC's security objectives.
