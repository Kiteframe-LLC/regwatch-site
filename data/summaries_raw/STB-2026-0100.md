## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

Claim:
- Part 1144 now functions mainly as a barrier to relief rather than a workable standard.
Underlying assumption:
- The rarity of petitions and the absence of successful prescriptions are meaningful indicators of over-stringency rather than low demand, case-selection effects, or other market changes.
Failure scenario:
- If low invocation reflects limited commercial need or preference for private negotiation rather than a regulatory barrier, the main empirical basis for repeal weakens.
Procedural implication:
- Commenters can ask for a fuller case inventory and explanation of why low filing volume should be read as proof of regulatory dysfunction.

Claim:
- Restoring case-by-case adjudication under the statutory standards will better effectuate congressional intent.
Underlying assumption:
- The broader statutory standards are administrable without the screening rule and will improve access to competitive remedies.
Failure scenario:
- If repeal increases litigation costs and uncertainty without materially improving shipper access, the claimed improvement over the status quo becomes harder to support.
Procedural implication:
- The Board should explain expected evidentiary standards, burdens, and case-management principles after repeal.

Claim:
- Repeal will not negatively impact operations or investment decisions by carriers.
Underlying assumption:
- Carriers can absorb additional case-by-case proceedings and possible prescriptions without meaningful network or capital-planning disruption.
Failure scenario:
- If repeal changes bargaining leverage or creates new uncertainty around routing and switching obligations, carrier planning effects may be larger than the NPRM suggests.
Procedural implication:
- Commenters can request evidence or sensitivity analysis about operational and investment effects rather than accepting a conclusory assurance.

Claim:
- The rule is net deregulatory because competition benefits will outweigh increased administrative costs.
Underlying assumption:
- More open access to Board remedies will produce enough lower rates, better service, or negotiated settlements to offset litigation and adjudication costs.
Failure scenario:
- If adjudication volume rises but prescriptions remain rare or highly uncertain, the record may show more process cost than competition benefit.
Procedural implication:
- The NPRM would be stronger with a more explicit framework for how the Board expects benefits to materialize and how commenters should test that expectation.

Claim:
- Small entities are not significantly affected because the existing rules are not directed at small entities.
Underlying assumption:
- The relevant impact inquiry turns on the formal target of the regulation rather than the practical effect of changing access-to-competition remedies for small and medium-sized shippers or smaller carriers.
Failure scenario:
- If small shippers are among the principal beneficiaries or cost-bearers of repeal, the certification may look under-explained even if it is legally defensible.
Procedural implication:
- Commenters can ask for more detail on which market participants are expected to gain or bear costs after repeal.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

Observed Facts:
- The Board proposes to repeal 49 CFR part 1144 in full and reserve the part.
- Part 1144 currently governs prescriptions involving reciprocal switching, through routes, and through rates using an anticompetitive-conduct framework adopted in the mid-1980s.
- The NPRM argues that the relevant statutes, 49 U.S.C. 10705 and 11102(c), are discretionary and do not require the current narrowed standard.
- The Board explicitly asks for comment on whether repeal should instead be limited to the reciprocal-switching portions of part 1144.
- The notice states that OIRA deemed the rule significant under Executive Order 12866 and that the Board considers it an Executive Order 14192 deregulatory action.

Inference:
- The proposal is not just housekeeping. It would materially change the gatekeeping standard for parties seeking Board-ordered competitive access remedies.
- The practical effect would be to move from a settled but stringent screening framework toward more open-ended adjudication under broader statutory language.
- The strongest immediate comment opportunity is not whether the Board can repeal the rule at all, but whether the record adequately explains the scope of repeal and the real-world consequences of replacing a rule with case-by-case discretion.
