## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

- **Claim:** Aggregating standards from many 6-digit industries to 4- and 5-digit groups will reduce confusion without materially obscuring industry differences.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** Firms within the combined group face sufficiently similar market conditions and the higher-level standard will remain a workable proxy for non-dominance.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** A small specialized industry could be grouped with larger or structurally different industries, causing a threshold that is too high or too low for the specialized market.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Comments should identify industry pairs where the proposed aggregation changes eligibility in a way that the notice's general simplification rationale does not explain.
- **Claim:** Average market size based on national industry size, geographic markets, and net imports better reflects whether a firm is dominant.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** The three variables are measured consistently across industries and capture the relevant competitive conditions better than the prior factor set.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** The geographic-market or import adjustment may be sensitive to data definitions, and an omitted factor such as entry barriers or firm-size distribution may matter more in a particular industry.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** The record would benefit from industry-level examples, sensitivity tests, and disclosure of the underlying data and transformation rules.
- **Claim:** Adding productivity growth to inflation adjustments keeps receipt-based standards aligned with employee-based standards.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** A general productivity measure tracks the productivity changes relevant to each receipt-based industry.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** Industry-specific productivity may diverge from the general measure, producing thresholds that change eligibility without a sector-specific explanation.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Commenters can request the productivity series, time period, formula, and sensitivity range used for each receipt-based standard.
- **Claim:** Not reducing standards in 45 industries is justified by difficult economic conditions and SBA's mission.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** The broad no-reduction policy is a permissible and sufficiently tailored response to current conditions even where the revised analytics indicate a decrease.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** The policy may preserve thresholds that no longer distinguish small firms from dominant firms in some industries, or may shift benefits to firms that are already above the analytically indicated level.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** The agency should explain which industries are covered, how the no-reduction decision affects the statutory non-dominance inquiry, and when it will revisit the policy.
- **Claim:** The economic effects are de minimis even though eligibility changes affect contracts, loans, funding, and regulatory flexibility.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** The changes are primarily transfers among firms and do not materially change program access, competition, or administrative costs.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** A 1.8% increase in eligible firms may change competition for set-asides, lending eligibility, or compliance flexibility in concentrated industries, with effects that averages obscure.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Comments should request program-specific and industry-specific impact estimates rather than relying on the aggregate firm count.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

**Regulatory Action:** SBA proposes to revise 13 CFR part 121 and replace the existing size-standard table with 338 standards calculated through a revised methodology using a mix of 4- and 5-digit NAICS groupings, more employee-based thresholds, average market size, and a productivity adjustment for receipt-based standards.

**Affected Entities**
- Small businesses and firms near current or proposed thresholds
- Businesses seeking federal contracts and set-aside opportunities
- Applicants for SBA-backed loans and other federal small-business programs
- Businesses relying on small-entity regulatory flexibilities
- Federal contracting officers and program administrators
- Industries whose 6-digit standards are consolidated into 4- or 5-digit groups

**Statutory Authority:** The notice cites 15 U.S.C. 631 et seq., including 15 U.S.C. 632(a)(2)(A), and proposes to continue the authority citation for 13 CFR part 121 under 15 U.S.C. 632, 634(b)(6), 636(a)(36), 662, and 694a(9).

**Key Regulatory Change:** The proposal changes the unit of analysis, converts many receipt-based standards to employee-based standards, removes size-standard exceptions, changes the calculation factors and translation function, adds productivity growth to monetary standards, and retains standards from decreasing in specified cases.

**Observed Facts**
- The notice says the current system covers 978 NAICS industries and 18 subindustries through 102 size levels.
- The proposed system contains 338 standards and excludes NAICS 4911, 5211, 8141, and all of sector 92.
- The IRFA estimates eligible firms would increase from 6,344,967 to 6,459,508, or 1.8%.
- The proposal says 24 industry groups or industries would otherwise reduce the number of eligible firms, with the total decrease expected to be fewer than 200, and that SBA is retaining standards that use the same measure rather than reducing them.

**Inference:** The proposal is designed to broaden and simplify small-business eligibility overall, but it also redistributes eligibility within industries through aggregation, measure changes, and a no-reduction policy.
