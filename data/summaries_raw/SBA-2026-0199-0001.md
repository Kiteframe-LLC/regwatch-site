## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

**Regulatory Action:** Revise SBA's small-business size standards for 338 NAICS industry groups and industries, including receipt, employee, asset, and capacity-based thresholds.

**Affected Entities**
- small businesses near current or proposed thresholds
- firms seeking Federal contracts
- SBA loan applicants
- Federal contracting officers and agencies
- small-business program administrators
- industries receiving lower or unchanged effective thresholds

**Core Tradeoff:** Higher thresholds may improve growth runway and broaden eligibility, but may also increase competition from larger firms for programs intended for small businesses and may make industry comparisons less transparent.

**Inference:** A defensible final rule should explain the revised methodology, data sources, industry-level results, and distributional effects with enough detail for meaningful comment.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

- Phased implementation or delayed effective dates for large threshold changes.
- Industry-specific transition rules or caps where set-aside displacement is concentrated.
- A comparison using the 2024 methodology with quantified outcomes for each affected industry.
- Monitoring triggers or periodic review focused on contracting concentration and small-firm exits.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Facts**

**Inferences**

- The use of average market size may not capture variance within broad four-digit industry groups.

**Unknowns**

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Facts**

**Inferences**
- The authority discussion is facially connected to the proposed action.
- The legal and reasoned-explanation record would be stronger if it showed how the methodology implements non-dominance across individual industries and addressed relevant contrary evidence.

## Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

**Inferences**

**Unknowns**

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- Broad thresholds could shift set-aside competition toward firms with greater proposal, bonding, recruiting, and past-performance capacity.
- Data errors or stale SAM/FPDS records could misstate winners and losers.
- A uniform methodology could produce inequitable results across industries with different geographic and capital structures.
- The single effective policy could create a growth incentive while weakening the practical meaning of small-business set-asides.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Positive**
- Firms near current thresholds may have more room to grow without immediately losing access to programs.
- SBA may have a clearer repeatable framework for five-year reviews.

**Negative**
- Higher thresholds may encourage firms to remain eligible for set-asides rather than compete in unrestricted markets.
- Existing small firms may face stronger competition from newly eligible larger firms.

**Unknowns**
- Whether the proposal changes firm behavior differently by industry or program.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Strengths**
- The notice provides aggregate counts, contract estimates, cited statutes, alternatives, and explicit limitations for some datasets.

**Weaknesses**
- The core Revised Methodology is incorporated by reference and unavailable in the export.
- The notice provides limited industry-by-industry evidence for the claimed competitive effects.
- SBA acknowledges that SAM revenue and employee data may be outdated or inaccurate.

**Overall:** Moderate for the existence and direction of impacts; limited for replication and distributional evaluation.

## Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

- Revised Methodology white paper and underlying calculations.
- Industry-level baseline and sensitivity tables for all 338 changes.
- Analysis of set-aside concentration, firm size distributions, and displacement by industry.
- Validation of SAM/FPDS data quality and treatment of missing or self-reported values.
- Evidence supporting the conclusion that the sole effective reduction affects fewer than five firms.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

- Insufficient explanation if the final rule relies on a methodology or data not meaningfully available for comment.
- Potential arbitrary-and-capricious concerns if aggregate eligibility gains substitute for analysis of concentrated adverse effects.
- Potential challenge to unexplained deviations from analytical results or to the treatment of industries using different size measures.
- The record gap is remediable through disclosure, sensitivity analysis, and responses to industry-specific comments.
