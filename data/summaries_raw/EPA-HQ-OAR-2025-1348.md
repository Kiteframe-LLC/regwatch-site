**Agency:** Environmental Protection Agency
**Action:** Proposed rule; technology review and reconsideration for NESHAP subparts HH and HHH covering crude oil and natural gas production facilities and natural gas transmission and storage facilities

Evidence sources: notice text and supporting materials embedded in the export, including the April 2026 Background Technical Support Document and docket metadata describing the Economic Impact Analysis, ICR supporting statements, and related technical memoranda.

# Phase 1 — Docket Integrity Check

### Classification

**Full analytical spine present**

### Observed Fact

The available record shows the usual EPA analytic backbone for a section 112 rulemaking:

* the NPRM itself
* an April 2026 Background Technical Support Document
* an April 2026 Economic Impact Analysis memorandum
* ICR supporting statements for subparts HH and HHH
* Excel workbooks for process controllers and pumps
* historical MACT-floor, cost, and response-to-comments memoranda

The notice also states that EPA used a 2023 Phase 1 ICR and a 2024 Phase 2 ICR to support the review. The embedded TSD reports that “231 production facilities responded, and 57 transmission and storage facilities responded” in Phase 1, and that Phase 2 “included 220 production facilities and 86 transmission and storage facilities.”

### Inference

This is not a thin NPRM. The public-facing preamble compresses a larger technical record that exists in the docket. The main public-comment opportunity is therefore not to complain about total absence of analysis, but to test whether the analysis actually supports EPA’s strongest legal and economic conclusions.

### Implication

The document can be reviewed on the merits without pausing for an attachment handoff because the export already includes the notice text plus the most relevant technical support excerpt. Remaining uncertainty mainly concerns the quality of quantified support, not whether any support exists at all.

# Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

## Claim

No cost-effective developments justify revising current standards for currently regulated emission points.

### Underlying assumption

EPA’s comparison set for “developments” is complete and its cost-effectiveness threshold is appropriate for HAP control in this source category.

### Failure scenario

Relevant state requirements, newer NSPS-era practices, or lower-cost controls exist but were screened out too aggressively, or were judged against an unstated cost threshold.

### Procedural implication

A commenter could argue that the Agency’s no-revision conclusion is underexplained if the docket does not clearly show what alternatives were tested and rejected.

---

## Claim

EPA is not obligated under section 112(d)(6) to regulate previously unregulated emission points in this review.

### Underlying assumption

Section 112(d)(6) is best read as a review of already-promulgated standards, not as a trigger to add new source types whenever HAP-emitting units were previously omitted.

### Failure scenario

The statute, the LEAN-related litigation history, or EPA’s own prior practice is read to require a broader review once major unregulated HAP-emitting units are identified.

### Procedural implication

The legal interpretation could become the rule’s main vulnerability if the final record does not fully explain why EPA may defer regulation while simultaneously presenting an alternative MACT-floor package.

---

## Claim

Methanol standards for regulated subpart HH emission points will impose no additional control requirements.

### Underlying assumption

Existing controls that satisfy current requirements already capture methanol emissions to the same practical level, so the new pollutant-specific standard is mostly formal.

### Failure scenario

Methanol behavior differs from the previously regulated HAP mix, or some units that comply with current standards would need new testing, recalculation, or operating changes to show methanol compliance.

### Procedural implication

If “no additional control requirements” masks added monitoring or calculation burdens, commenters can press EPA to quantify those burdens rather than treating them as negligible.

---

## Claim

Alternative standards for previously unregulated emission points would have minimal costs and no emissions impacts.

### Underlying assumption

Baseline industry practice already meets the proposed standard, so the regulation mainly codifies business-as-usual plus paperwork.

### Failure scenario

Compliance varies more than EPA assumes across facilities, or the proposed standards materially change documentation, design, or operating practices at a meaningful subset of sources.

### Procedural implication

The no-emissions-impact framing becomes weak if commenters can show that some units would need real changes or that codifying current practice still has enforcement significance.

---

## Claim

At least 12 percent of existing transmission-and-storage controllers in 1999 were low-bleed devices.

### Underlying assumption

EPA can infer a historic best-performing share from incomplete information without distorting the MACT floor.

### Failure scenario

The 1999 baseline was materially different, making the proposed MACT floor for existing sources too stringent or too weak.

### Procedural implication

Because EPA expressly solicits comment on this assumption, it is better characterized as a deliberate solicitation than as an unacknowledged defect, but it remains a critical evidence point.

# Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

## Observed Facts

* EPA is conducting a section 112 technology review and reconsideration for oil and gas NESHAP subparts HH and HHH.
* EPA proposes **no revision** to currently regulated standards for currently regulated HAP emission points because it says no cost-effective developments were identified.
* EPA proposes a **legal first approach** under which it is not required to regulate previously unregulated emission points during a section 112(d)(6) review.
* EPA also puts forward an **alternative proposal** under sections 112(d)(2), 112(d)(3), and 112(d)(6) for several previously unregulated emission points, including AGRUs, certain storage vessels, transport loading, controllers, and pumps.
* EPA proposes methanol standards for certain already regulated subpart HH emission points.
* EPA proposes to revise the definition of “associated equipment” to stop aggregating dehydrators and storage vessels with wells for major-source determinations at production field facilities.

### Evidence Quotes

> “The EPA proposes no revisions to the current standards in NESHAP subparts HH and HHH are necessary pursuant to the CAA section 112(d)(6) technology review.”

> “The EPA proposes that we are not obligated to promulgate standards for additional emission points at this time as part of the CAA section 112(d)(6) technology review.”

> “The EPA also proposes in this document new standards for a previously unregulated HAP, methanol…”

## Inference

This NPRM is less about a broad new pollution-control push than about deciding how far EPA must go during a periodic technology review after litigation and reconsideration pressure. The rule’s practical significance lies in the legal fork: either EPA narrows the review to already regulated points, or it uses the same proceeding to add standards for several previously unregulated units while describing those additions as low-burden.

↓

Alternatives considered
↓

Agency conclusion
↓

### Missing links

* The public-facing notice does not clearly disclose the cost-effectiveness benchmark behind several “not cost effective” judgments.
* EPA’s assertion of “minimal costs” and “no emissions impacts” for the alternative package is stated more clearly than it is demonstrated in the visible record.
* The associated-equipment reinterpretation is acknowledged to have deregulatory cost savings, but EPA also says it lacks enough information to quantify those savings.

# Step 6 — Technical Coherence

### Strong points

* The rule has a coherent structure separating already regulated points, unregulated emission points, and unregulated HAP.
* EPA ties much of the proposal to concrete ICR efforts rather than relying only on legacy records.
* The alternative package is at least technically specified enough to make public comment meaningful.

### Weak points

* The Agency sometimes moves from “we think facilities already do this” to “therefore there are no emissions impacts,” which collapses a compliance-baseline question into an effect conclusion.
* Several MACT-floor judgments for omitted sources rely on assumptions about what facilities were already doing in 1999, even while EPA admits the record is incomplete and asks commenters to confirm or refute those assumptions.
* The associated-equipment reinterpretation is legally and practically important, but the technical consequence analysis remains thin.

### Bottom line

The proposal is technically organized but not fully technically transparent. The main coherence problem is not internal contradiction; it is the number of consequential assumptions that remain only partly quantified.

---

# Step 7 — Legal Grounding

### Observed Fact

The notice grounds different parts of the proposal in different legal authorities:

* section 112(d)(6) for the technology review of existing standards
* sections 112(d)(2) and 112(d)(3) for the alternative MACT-floor package for previously unregulated points
* section 112(n)(4) for the associated-equipment reinterpretation
* LEAN-related litigation history for regulation of previously unregulated HAP and omitted points

### Inference

EPA appears to be trying to preserve multiple legal pathways at once: a narrow reading of section 112(d)(6), plus a fallback broader approach if that narrow reading fails.

### Legal concern

That strategy is understandable, but it makes the final rule vulnerable if EPA does not cleanly explain why its preferred interpretation is lawful and why the fallback standards are analytically ready if the preferred interpretation is rejected.

# Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

### Deliberate solicitation

Several weak-looking areas are explicitly treated as comment requests rather than hidden assumptions:

* historic use of submerged loading
* historic prevalence and location of low-bleed controllers
* historic use of zero-emission pumps
* whether EPA should gather more information on metal HAP
* whether to reference software generically rather than by trademark

### Potential unacknowledged weakness

The following points are not framed mainly as open questions, yet they drive major conclusions:

* the cost threshold behind “not cost effective”
* the conclusion that alternative standards cause “no emissions impacts”
* the deregulatory associated-equipment change without quantified scope effects

### Assessment

Procedurally, the NPRM looks normal for a contested EPA section 112 action, but it still leaves several firm conclusions more compressed than they should be.

# Step 9 — Failure Modes

* EPA finalizes the narrow section 112(d)(6) interpretation without fully addressing why omitted emission points may be deferred despite the Agency having already developed a full alternative standard package.
* The final rule retains “minimal costs” and “no emissions impacts” language even if commenters show that a nontrivial subset of sources would need operational or documentation changes.
* The associated-equipment reinterpretation changes source classification outcomes without a solid estimate of how many units exit or avoid major-source treatment.
* Historic assumptions about 1999 controller, pump, or loading practices prove inaccurate, weakening MACT-floor determinations.
* Methanol regulation is finalized as nominally burden-free, but implementation reveals new testing, modeling, or documentation costs.

# Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

### Observed Fact

The proposal would:

* preserve much of the current status quo for existing regulated points
* create a possible path to regulate more omitted points while telling industry that many already meet the standards
* reduce burdens on some production-field facilities through the associated-equipment reinterpretation

### Inference

The incentive structure points in two directions at once:

* for industry, EPA signals that much of the proposal is either status-quo or deregulatory
* for commenters seeking stronger controls, the alternative package creates leverage to demand more quantified justification instead of blanket deferral

### Concern

If EPA overstates how widely facilities already comply, it may under-incentivize more careful comment on compliance and enforcement consequences.

# Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

### Strongly supported in the available record

* EPA conducted broad ICR collection across production and transmission/storage facilities.
* EPA considered multiple categories of units rather than limiting the review to one legacy source type.
* EPA is expressly requesting comment where key historical assumptions are not fully documented.

### Moderately supported

* Methanol appears to be present in production/processing responses and absent in reported transmission/storage responses.
* EPA has some technical basis for alternative standards on controllers, pumps, loading, and AGRUs.

### Weakly supported in the visible record

* the exact cost benchmark used for “not cost effective”
* the statement that alternative standards would create “no emissions impacts”
* the claim that the associated-equipment reinterpretation yields cost savings even though EPA cannot quantify them

### Overall assessment

The record looks real, not invented, but several of the rule’s most consequential conclusions are supported more by asserted synthesis than by public-facing quantified explanation.

---

# Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

* A clearer public explanation of the cost-effectiveness threshold used to reject stronger controls for already regulated points.
* Facility-count, burden, and classification consequences of the associated-equipment reinterpretation.
* More explicit support for the “already achieved” premise behind the alternative standards for omitted points.
* Better explanation of whether methanol regulation truly adds no new compliance burden for subpart HH units.
* Clearer documentation for 1999 baseline assumptions used in controller, pump, and loading MACT-floor analyses.
* More concrete discussion of how nearby communities and smaller operators experience the alternative proposals, even if aggregate cost is low.

---

# Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

### Most plausible themes

* **Reasoned explanation risk:** EPA may have to say more about why “not cost effective” means no revision is necessary for current standards.
* **Statutory authority risk:** the narrow section 112(d)(6) interpretation for previously unregulated points could be challenged if the final rule does not adequately distinguish review of existing standards from regulation of newly identified units.
* **Arbitrary-and-capricious risk:** “minimal costs,” “no emissions impacts,” and unquantified deregulatory savings are all conclusions that could look too categorical if the record remains mixed.
* **Implementation clarity risk:** the associated-equipment reinterpretation and nominally burden-free methanol standards could have practical effects that the rule understates.

### Less plausible themes

* direct retroactivity concerns
* federalism or preemption concerns

### Overall

This NPRM does not present an obvious fatal flaw on its face, but it does expose EPA to a meaningful challenge if the final rule keeps several high-confidence conclusions while leaving their quantitative support underdeveloped.

# Phase 3 — Comment Generation

## Step 14 — Commentable Sentences

1. EPA should explain the specific cost-effectiveness benchmarks and comparison assumptions that led it to conclude that no revision to currently regulated emission-point standards is “necessary.”

2. EPA should explain why section 112(d)(6) permits deferral of standards for previously unregulated emission points even though the Agency has already developed an alternative MACT-floor package for those same points.

3. EPA should quantify the basis for stating that the alternative standards would impose only minimal costs and “no emissions impacts,” including how many facilities would need procedural, operational, or recordkeeping changes to comply.

4. EPA should quantify the expected source-classification and compliance consequences of revising the “associated equipment” definition, rather than acknowledging likely cost savings without measuring them.

5. EPA should document the historical evidence supporting its MACT-floor assumptions for submerged loading, low-bleed controllers, and zero-emission pumps, especially where the preamble admits specific information is unavailable.

6. EPA should clarify whether methanol standards for already regulated subpart HH emission points truly create no new monitoring, modeling, or reporting obligations in practice.

# Step 15 — Procedural Concern Score

**Score: 4 / 5**

### Why

* The NPRM is substantial and supported by a real docket spine, so this is not a collapse-of-process case.
* But several of the most consequential conclusions are either not quantified, or are supported by admitted assumptions that EPA is still asking commenters to confirm.
* The strongest comment opportunities are therefore procedural-substantive hybrids: ask EPA to show more work before finalizing.

---

# Step 16 — Evidence Requests

Commenters could ask EPA to place or spotlight the following in the docket:

* the cost-effectiveness tables and threshold comparisons behind each no-revision conclusion
* facility-level or representative counts for sources that would newly face documentation or operational requirements under the alternative package
* the basis for concluding that alternative standards cause no emissions impacts
* quantitative estimates of how many production-field units are affected by the associated-equipment reinterpretation
* historical evidence supporting 1999 prevalence assumptions for submerged loading, low-bleed controllers, and zero-emission pumps
* any analysis comparing methanol compliance burdens with current HAP compliance burdens for the same units

---

# Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

* Ask EPA to either quantify or soften the categorical claim that the alternative package has “minimal costs” and “no emissions impacts.”
* Ask EPA to explain the legal boundary between a section 112(d)(6) review of existing standards and a duty to regulate previously omitted source types once they are squarely identified in the record.
* Ask EPA to quantify the practical effect of changing the associated-equipment definition before relying on it as a deregulatory measure.
* Ask EPA to provide the explicit cost-effectiveness screen it applied to OGI and other candidate developments for already regulated points.
* Ask EPA to distinguish which open questions are true requests for information and which conclusions it already considers sufficiently supported for final action.

---

# Step 18 — Decision Brief

### Bottom-line assessment

This is a significant NPRM with a real technical record and several genuine avenues for constructive comment. The strongest concerns are not that EPA ignored the subject matter, but that it presents several high-stakes conclusions more confidently than the visible record justifies.

### Best-supported comment themes

* demand more transparent cost-effectiveness reasoning for the no-revision decision
* challenge or probe the legal basis for deferring standards on previously unregulated emission points under section 112(d)(6)
* require EPA to quantify its “minimal cost,” “no emissions impact,” and deregulatory associated-equipment claims
* press EPA to document the historic assumptions underlying MACT-floor determinations for controllers, pumps, and loading operations

### Public-reader takeaway

The available record suggests EPA has done serious work, but it also suggests that the final rule should be improved by forcing the Agency to show more of that work in public-facing, quantified form before it locks in its preferred interpretation and burden estimates.
