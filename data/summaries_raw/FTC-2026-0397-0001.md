## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

**Observed Facts**
- This document is not an FTC-authored proposed rule. It is a notice that the Commission received a citizen petition and is requesting comments on that petition.
- The attached petition asks the FTC to require written disclosure, before signing, of the lender's buy rate, the consumer-facing sell rate, and the dealer reserve earned from the spread between those rates.
- The petition's factual theory is that discretionary dealer markups in indirect auto lending are often hidden from consumers and create incentives for dealerships to increase the interest rate above the lender-approved baseline.
- The petition cites a 2023 MIT/NBER working paper and references CFPB research as support for the claim that discretionary markups are common and costly.
- The FTC notice states that the Commission may grant, deny, or partially grant the petition after the comment period and that the notice itself does not begin a rulemaking.

**Evidence quotes**
- "This petition requests to promulgate a new rule or amend existing regulations to mandate disclosures regarding indirect auto lending."
- "The Commission will not consider the petition's merits until after the comment period closes."
- "It may grant or deny the petition in whole or in part, and it may deem the petition insufficient to warrant commencement of a rulemaking proceeding."
- From the petition attachment: "It is an unfair or deceptive act or practice for any Motor Vehicle Dealer ... to fail to clearly and conspicuously disclose" the buy rate, sell rate, and dealer reserve before the consumer signs.

**Inference**
- The main public question is not whether the FTC has already justified a disclosure rule. It is whether the petition identifies a real enough market problem, a clear enough disclosure concept, and a strong enough legal and empirical basis to justify moving into a fuller rulemaking stage.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- The Commission could overread the petition's cited studies and move toward a broad rule without establishing how indirect-lending practices vary across dealers, lenders, and borrower segments.
- A disclosure rule could become confusing if it requires estimates that consumers misinterpret as fixed or lender-mandated numbers.
- The record could conflate deceptive non-disclosure with routine financing negotiation practices without clearly defining the boundary between the two.
- If the FTC relies too heavily on this petition-stage record, a later rulemaking could lack the empirical depth and alternatives analysis needed for a stronger administrative record.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

- If a disclosure rule were adopted, dealerships could face reduced incentives to increase rates when doing so would require disclosing the markup and expected reserve.
- On the other hand, firms could respond by changing fee structures, compensation arrangements, or deal presentation formats in ways the petition does not analyze.
- A disclosure-only model may preserve dealer compensation while improving transparency, but it may also produce formal disclosures that consumers do not use effectively unless presentation rules are well designed.
