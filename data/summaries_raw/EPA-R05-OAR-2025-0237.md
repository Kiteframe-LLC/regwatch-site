## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim A

**Claim:** The approved source-specific package is sufficient to satisfy the Cleveland area's remaining moderate RACT obligations.

**Underlying assumption:** The three facilities discussed here are the remaining major non-CTG VOC and NOx sources that matter for moderate-area RACT completeness.

**Failure scenario:** If other relevant sources remain unresolved, or if one of these permit-based determinations is not approvable as SIP-strength RACT, the docket-wide “fully satisfied” conclusion becomes premature.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters could ask EPA to identify the full facility universe and explain why this package closes the moderate-area RACT record.

### Claim B

**Claim:** Existing controls at Lubrizol and Henkel are already strong enough, and stronger controls would be economically unreasonable.

**Underlying assumption:** The cost figures, destruction efficiencies, and operating constraints in the studies are representative and complete enough to show that more stringent options are not reasonably available.

**Failure scenario:** If the record omits lower-cost variants, understates feasible capture improvements, or relies on generalized cost claims without enough facility-specific support, the economic-unreasonableness conclusion weakens.

**Procedural implication:** EPA would benefit from a clearer explanation of how it screened alternatives and what benchmark it is implicitly using for cost reasonableness.

### Claim C

**Claim:** A 348.3-ton rolling 12-month NOx trigger is an acceptable RACT outcome for Cleveland-Cliffs reheat furnaces.

**Underlying assumption:** A trigger tied to maximum actual historical operation is a reasonable proxy for present feasibility, and reopening the analysis only after exceedance still satisfies moderate RACT.

**Failure scenario:** If RACT should require presently enforceable limits rather than a re-study trigger, or if the trigger is high enough to defer controls that are already cost-effective on other assumptions, the proposal may under-control current emissions.

**Procedural implication:** This is a likely focal point for comments because it blends methodology choice, enforceability, and prospective compliance design.

### Claim D

**Claim:** Manufacturer specifications, BAT limits, VOC-content caps, and good operating practices can constitute RACT for Cleveland-Cliffs VOC sources.

**Underlying assumption:** Those measures are specific enough, enforceable enough, and materially emission-limiting enough to serve as SIP-grade RACT rather than loose operating expectations.

**Failure scenario:** If the permit terms are too open-ended or the work-practice provisions are difficult to verify in practice, enforcement clarity may be weaker than EPA suggests.

**Procedural implication:** Commenters could ask EPA to quote or summarize the exact enforceable permit conditions it is incorporating by reference.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

* EPA proposes to approve Ohio source-specific VOC and NOx RACT determinations for Lubrizol, Henkel, and Cleveland-Cliffs Cleveland Works in the Cleveland ozone nonattainment area.
* The proposal addresses moderate-area obligations under the 2015 ozone NAAQS even though the area was later reclassified to Serious.
* For Lubrizol, EPA proposes to rely on existing high-efficiency thermal oxidizers, submerged filling, and loading-rack work practices and permit conditions.
* For Henkel, EPA proposes to treat existing condenser-based controls and low-VOC operating practices as RACT after rejecting stronger add-on controls as technically or economically unjustified.
* For Cleveland-Cliffs VOC sources, EPA proposes work-practice controls, BAT limits, and oil-content limits rather than new add-on controls.
* For Cleveland-Cliffs NOx sources, EPA proposes a mix of presumptive boiler limits, continued existing source-specific limits, and a 348.3 tpy trigger for each reheat furnace that would require a new RACT study if exceeded.

### Inference

This is a technical SIP approval whose real dispute is not whether EPA may use source-specific RACT studies, but whether the public-facing record adequately explains why permit-based work practices and trigger-based compliance mechanisms are stringent enough to count as RACT now.

### Evidence Quotes

* “If this action is finalized as proposed, the Cleveland nonattainment area will have fully satisfied its moderate RACT requirements...”
* “Given the already low emissions at Lubrizol (.01 to .88 tpy of VOC) ... additional controls would be economically unreasonable.”
* “The total annual cost was estimated at $1,442,656/yr. At a 98% control efficiency, this gives a cost effectiveness of over $29,482 per ton of VOC removed.”
* “For the Cleveland-Cliffs reheat furnaces, the EPA is proposing to approve a NOX RACT trigger... above which Cleveland-Cliffs must conduct a revised RACT study.”

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

**Alternatives evaluated:** Add-on VOC controls, stronger condenser systems, thermal and catalytic oxidation, carbon adsorption, burner replacement, SCR, SNCR, and source-specific content or work-practice requirements.

**Plausible alternatives not analyzed:** A lower reheat-furnace trigger, phased burner replacement when furnaces are already down for maintenance, more specific monitoring or recordkeeping for Cleveland-Cliffs VOC work practices, and a clearer side-by-side comparison of permit-based requirements against presumptive RACT outcomes.

**Evidence suggesting feasibility:** The Cleveland-Cliffs NOx study itself acknowledges that burner replacement appears cost-effective on a PTE basis, which suggests the real issue is the selected baseline and timing rather than total infeasibility.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

The technical story is mostly coherent. Lubrizol and Henkel each have an identifiable control narrative, and Cleveland-Cliffs distinguishes VOC and NOx pathways across multiple unit types.

**Observed Fact:** Lubrizol's reactor vents and filling operations already route emissions to high-efficiency thermal oxidizers.

**Observed Fact:** Henkel's condenser systems reportedly achieve 85 percent VOC removal, while colder operation risks ice formation and vent blockage.

**Observed Fact:** Cleveland-Cliffs' reheat-furnace burner retrofit is treated as potentially cost-effective on one baseline and not on another.

**Inference:** The biggest technical-coherence issue is not contradiction, but how much weight EPA gives to actual-emissions baselines and operating constraints when determining what is “reasonably available.”

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

**Observed Fact:** EPA grounds the proposal in Clean Air Act moderate-area RACT requirements and Ohio's SIP-submitted OAC rules for source-specific RACT studies.

**Observed Fact:** EPA notes the Cleveland area is now Serious, but this docket addresses only the earlier moderate-area obligations.

**Inference:** EPA's legal authority to approve source-specific RACT is not the main vulnerability. The more plausible legal risk is ordinary APA-style explanatory adequacy if the final rule does not better justify the trigger-based and permit-based choices.

**Unknown:** The notice does not fully unpack how EPA distinguishes what must be approved now for moderate RACT from what will be required later for Serious-area planning.

## Step 8 — Procedural Normalcy

**Classification:** **Normal**

**Observed Fact:** The record includes public-notice history for the Ohio determinations and supporting permit materials.

**Observed Fact:** Lubrizol and Henkel reportedly received no public comments; Cleveland-Cliffs materials include facility comments and Ohio responses.

**Inference:** This appears to be a routine SIP-approval format, albeit one involving unusually technical source-specific studies.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

* EPA could under-explain why a rolling re-study trigger is enough to qualify as current RACT for Cleveland-Cliffs reheat furnaces.
* Permit-based “good engineering practices” controls could prove harder to enforce than numerical emission limits if not described with enough specificity.
* The final action could look too conclusory about area-wide moderate RACT completion if it does not identify why these are the last relevant sources.
* Cost-effectiveness reasoning could be attacked as underexplained if EPA does not say why costs in the $14,000 to $44,000 per ton range are or are not acceptable in each facility context.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

* The trigger-based NOx approach gives Cleveland-Cliffs an incentive to stay below the historical-actual baseline rather than install more stringent burners now.
* Permit-based work-practice and material-content requirements encourage operational discipline but may also favor flexible, less capital-intensive compliance pathways.
* EPA's approach signals that strong existing controls plus cost-based rejection of further controls may be enough for source-specific RACT where agencies can document operating constraints.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

The evidentiary strength is moderate to strong. The notice is not operating from a bare assertion; it has underlying studies, vendor information, historical emissions data, and cost tables.

The evidentiary weakness is mostly one of translation. EPA summarizes the record more briefly than a skeptical reader might want, especially for the reheat-furnace trigger and the reliance on open-ended good-practice requirements for some VOC sources.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

The most plausible litigation vulnerability is inadequate explanation, not lack of authority. A commenter challenging this proposal would likely focus on whether EPA sufficiently justified the reheat-furnace trigger, the reliance on actual emissions instead of PTE for one major cost analysis, and the sufficiency of permit-based work practices as SIP-enforceable RACT.
