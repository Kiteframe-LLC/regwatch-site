## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

**Dewatering-structure exemption.** If dewatering structures can function like longer-term CCR management units, a categorical exemption could create a loophole rather than a clarification. Commenters can ask EPA for objective criteria distinguishing temporary dewatering from disposal or storage.

**Site-specific permitting pathway.** If permit writers can change monitoring points, cleanup levels, closure requirements, or closure timeframes without clear floor criteria, similar sites may receive inconsistent levels of protection. Commenters can ask EPA to identify mandatory findings and public-record requirements.

**Beneficial-use revisions.** If large unencapsulated non-roadway uses can create site-specific exposure pathways, eliminating the environmental demonstration requirement may shift risk screening from ex ante review to after-the-fact dispute. Commenters can ask EPA what evidence justifies the categorical change.

**Use of prior analytical record.** The legacy CCR RIA and risk materials support a substantial analytical spine, but the stress point is fit: commenters can ask EPA to show which prior analyses support each new amendment and what proposal-specific assumptions changed.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

**Failure Modes**
- Loophole-by-definition: exempt dewatering structures or storage piles could be used in ways that resemble regulated disposal.
- Permit drift: site-specific decisions could diverge across permitting authorities without minimum criteria.
- Demonstration removal: large unencapsulated beneficial uses could avoid ex ante environmental screening.
- Record-fit gap: older RIA/risk materials may not map cleanly onto every 2026 amendment.
- Community risk shift: flexible cleanup or closure decisions could shift residual risk to nearby communities without transparent tradeoff analysis.

**Not Observed**

- No apparent substitution of a different statutory program.
- No direct harm-before-review structure; implementation should generally occur through rule and permit processes.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

It may also incentivize requests for alternate monitoring points, cleanup levels, and closure timeframes. Those incentives are manageable if EPA defines objective criteria and public-record requirements; they become more problematic if the final rule leaves the standards mostly discretionary.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

Potential vulnerabilities include arbitrary-and-capricious challenges to under-explained line drawing, inadequate explanation for removing or replacing prior safeguards, insufficient treatment of environmental-justice and groundwater consequences under broader discretion, and inconsistent permit-level implementation if EPA does not specify limiting principles. EPA has stronger defenses on procedural posture, existence of a substantial CCR analytical record, and the general legitimacy of tailoring technical rules to site-specific conditions.

## Step 13A — Structural Integrity / Nonsense Detector

The proposal is not structurally incoherent. Its structural weakness is a discretion-control problem: EPA relies on site-specific variation to justify flexibility, but variation also increases the need for clear criteria, monitoring, documentation, and public review. The most constructive comment posture is to ask EPA to preserve flexibility only where protective floors and transparent records are explicit.
