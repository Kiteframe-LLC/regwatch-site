## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

- **Claim:** A scientifically supported link exists between stressed production or slaughter conditions and consumer risk.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** Evidence about animal welfare conditions, meat quality, environmental effects, or contamination risk can be translated into a truthful and material advertising disclosure for a broad product category.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** Evidence may support a narrower claim about a particular practice, facility, product, or marketing representation but not a universal warning for every covered product or advertisement.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Ask the FTC to identify the evidence and causal chain for each proposed disclosure category and to separate established food-safety findings from ethical or welfare concerns.
- **Claim:** The requested disclosures fall within FTC consumer-protection authority.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** Sections 5 and 12 authorize a generalized disclosure requirement about production conditions and health risks even when an advertisement makes no related representation.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** A disclosure could be viewed as compelled speech or as an attempt to regulate food safety, labeling, or production practices allocated to another agency without a demonstrated deception nexus.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Request a specific statutory and jurisdictional analysis, including how the rule would distinguish deceptive omissions from mandated factual warnings unrelated to a seller's representation.
- **Claim:** Warnings would protect consumers from deception and improve informed purchasing.
  
  **Underlying Assumption:** The warning's content would be comprehensible, substantiated, material to reasonable consumers, and effective without misleading consumers about product-specific risk.
  
  **Failure Scenario:** A broad warning may overwhelm or distort product information, imply uniform risks across production systems, or fail to tell consumers what a seller actually did.
  
  **Procedural Implication:** Request consumer-understanding evidence, substantiation standards, and a less-burdensome alternative such as disclosures tied to specific substantiated claims or production attributes.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

- **Failure Mode:** Overbreadth
  
  **Scenario:** A category-wide warning could apply to products, facilities, or advertisements that do not present the asserted condition or make the relevant representation.
  
  **Comment Question:** What product- and claim-specific criteria would limit coverage?
- **Failure Mode:** Misleading disclosure
  
  **Scenario:** A warning that combines health, welfare, and environmental claims may create a net impression that every covered product presents the same danger.
  
  **Comment Question:** What substantiation and consumer-testing standard would prevent the required message from misleading consumers?
- **Failure Mode:** Jurisdictional mismatch
  
  **Scenario:** An advertising mandate may be used to address food-safety or production conditions that require different expertise, inspection, or regulatory tools.
  
  **Comment Question:** How would the FTC coordinate with agencies that regulate production, safety, and labeling?
- **Failure Mode:** Uneven compliance burden
  
  **Scenario:** The same prominent-disclosure requirement may be feasible for national television advertisers but difficult for small restaurants, local businesses, or constrained digital formats.
  
  **Comment Question:** What small-entity, media-format, and accessibility impacts would be expected?

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Evasion:** Advertisers may avoid otherwise useful descriptive claims or shift marketing channels if liability turns on undefined terms such as humane, natural, wholesome, or prominent.

**Threshold Gaming:** If coverage depends on whether a product or representation falls within a broad category, firms may restructure wording without changing underlying practices.

**Strategic Compliance:** A standardized warning could become boilerplate that consumers ignore, while firms with stronger verification systems may face the same message as firms with weaker substantiation.

**Comment Leverage:** Ask for clear definitions, safe harbors for substantiated claims, and a mechanism to update disclosures as evidence and production practices change.
