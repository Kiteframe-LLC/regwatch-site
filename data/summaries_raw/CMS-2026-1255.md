## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim

Impacted payers should support electronic prior authorization for all drugs requiring prior authorization.

**Underlying assumption**

Drug prior authorization delays and inconsistent electronic workflows are sufficiently widespread to justify uniform federal technical and process requirements.

**Failure scenario**

If some drug classes, payer types, or provider settings cannot map cleanly onto either FHIR-based medical-benefit workflows or NCPDP pharmacy-benefit workflows, implementation may be uneven even if the rule is facially comprehensive.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask CMS to identify edge cases and explain how drugs that do not fit cleanly into either category should be treated.

### Claim

The medical-benefit/pharmacy-benefit distinction is the right dividing line for FHIR versus NCPDP standards.

**Underlying assumption**

Benefit categorization is stable and operationally visible enough for payers, providers, vendors, and pharmacies to route transactions reliably.

**Failure scenario**

Coverage treatment may vary by payer, site of care, drug formulation, plan design, or bundled service context. If routing is ambiguous, providers may not know whether to use the Prior Authorization API or NCPDP processes.

**Procedural implication**

The final rule may need more concrete decision rules, examples, and error-handling requirements.

### Claim

October 1, 2027 provides enough time for implementation.

**Underlying assumption**

Payers and vendors can update APIs, support NCPDP standards, adjust reporting systems, and coordinate with providers on that timeline.

**Failure scenario**

State Medicaid and CHIP FFS programs, smaller issuers, and vendors serving small provider organizations may face synchronized implementation demands from FHIR, NCPDP, endpoint reporting, usage metrics, and HIPAA transaction changes.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask for transition milestones, testing windows, or conditional extensions tied to standards readiness.

### Claim

API usage metrics will help assess policy impact.

**Underlying assumption**

The metrics will be comparable, reliable, and useful across payer types.

**Failure scenario**

If CMS does not specify enough detail about metric definitions, aggregation, publication timing, and treatment of failed or redirected transactions, the metrics may not measure real-world patient and provider experience.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask CMS to define metrics in a way that captures successful use, errors, denials, response times, and provider burden.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

* The notice proposes to require electronic prior authorization support for drugs across MA organizations, state Medicaid and CHIP FFS programs, Medicaid managed care plans, CHIP managed care entities, and QHP issuers on the FFEs, including small group market QHP issuers on the FF-SHOPs.
* For drugs covered under a medical benefit, the proposal would expand the Prior Authorization API based on HL7 FHIR and Da Vinci implementation guides.
* For drugs covered under a pharmacy benefit, the proposal would require use of NCPDP SCRIPT, NCPDP Formulary & Benefit, and NCPDP Real-Time Prescription Benefit standards by specified payers.
* The notice proposes October 1, 2027 for several drug prior authorization and standards compliance requirements.
* The notice proposes API endpoint reporting, API usage metrics, prior authorization denial-reason communication, shortened QHP decision timeframes, small group FF-SHOP issuer coverage, a new Open Payments "failure to report" definition, FHIR-based HIPAA transaction standards, and ONC standards adoption.
* The notice includes RFIs on event notifications, resiliency, payer API implementation, step therapy, laboratory tests, and DMEPOS items.

Evidence quotes:

> "These proposals are intended to improve the electronic exchange of health care data and streamline processes related to prior authorization by increasing the interoperability of systems used across the health care industry."

> "We are proposing to require impacted payers to expand their Prior Authorization API ... to incorporate drugs covered under a medical benefit."

> "We are also proposing to require impacted payers (other than MA organizations for whom requirements already exist) to support the proposed NCPDP standards for the electronic prior authorization of drugs covered under a pharmacy benefit."

### Inference

The rule is an interoperability expansion that attempts to close gaps left by the 2024 interoperability and prior authorization final rule, especially the exclusion of drugs and the limited coverage of small group FF-SHOP issuers. The strongest comment opportunities are not about whether the agency has a plausible problem statement, but about implementation details, standards routing, costs, metric quality, and transition timing.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

### Alternatives Evaluated

The notice states that CMS/HHS considered:

* voluntary updates for drugs under a medical benefit;
* an alternative NCPDP standard such as the NCPDP Telecommunication Standard;
* retaining the 15-day standard QHP prior authorization timeframe for non-drug items and services;
* taking no action on drug prior authorization timeframes;
* maintaining existing X12N HIPAA transaction standards rather than moving to FHIR-based standards;
* alternatives for small entities, including tailoring and exceptions.

### Plausible Alternatives Not Fully Analyzed

1. Phased compliance by payer type, vendor readiness, or transaction type.
2. A pilot or staged enforcement period for drugs that are difficult to classify as medical-benefit or pharmacy-benefit drugs.
3. More explicit safe-harbor or error-routing rules when a provider submits through the wrong standard set.
4. A more detailed public metric taxonomy before mandatory API usage reporting begins.
5. Separate accommodations for small or rural provider-facing vendors that may not be directly regulated payers but will experience implementation impacts.

### Evidence Suggesting Feasibility

The agency points to existing Medicare Part D standards, the 2024 Prior Authorization API framework, current FHIR and Da Vinci IG development, and prior public comments urging extension of prior authorization policies to drugs.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Fact**

The proposal relies on two technical ecosystems: HL7 FHIR/Da Vinci implementation guides for medical-benefit drug prior authorization and NCPDP standards for pharmacy-benefit drug prior authorization. The notice recognizes that the standards are mutually exclusive for these use cases and asks whether there are drugs that may not fit into either category.

**Inference**

The technical architecture is coherent at a high level because it tracks existing workflows. The main technical risk is boundary management: a provider or vendor must know which standard applies, how to detect coverage category, and what happens when the category is ambiguous or changes.

Potential coherence risks:

* inconsistent payer categorization of the same drug;
* site-of-care differences that change medical versus pharmacy treatment;
* transaction failures where API and NCPDP workflows do not hand off cleanly;
* versioning complexity from allowing any unexpired versions of incorporated standards;
* metrics that count API calls without measuring user-facing success.

## Step 7 — Legal Grounding

### Statutory Authority

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

1. **Benefit-category ambiguity:** Drugs that can be paid under either medical or pharmacy benefits may be routed inconsistently.
2. **Provider workflow fragmentation:** Providers may still need multiple portals or workflows if API adoption is incomplete or if payers implement standards differently.
3. **Metrics without actionability:** API usage reporting may not reveal whether prior authorization is faster, less burdensome, or clinically safer.
4. **Version drift:** Allowing unexpired versions of standards may ease transitions but create compatibility issues if trading partners adopt different versions.
5. **State program timing:** Medicaid and CHIP FFS programs may need more time or technical support than large private issuers.
6. **Small entity burden:** The IRFA spreads annualized cost across a large number of small businesses, but some small entities may experience concentrated implementation costs.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

The proposal may create incentives for:

* payers to classify edge-case drugs in ways that preserve existing workflows;
* vendors to implement minimum compliant functionality rather than user-centered transaction quality;
* affected entities to focus on metric reportability rather than measurable reductions in provider burden;
* payers to rely on exception or extension pathways if compliance timelines are too compressed;
* stakeholders to shift disputes from prior authorization substance to transaction routing and documentation sufficiency.

The proposed API endpoint reporting and usage metrics could counter some of these incentives if CMS defines the reporting fields well enough to reveal low adoption, failed transactions, delays, or denial-reason problems.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Strongest support**

* The notice builds on prior CMS interoperability rules and existing Part D electronic prior authorization standards.
* The problem statement is supported by prior comments and cited evidence about prior authorization burden and delays.
* The RIA includes implementation costs, maintenance costs, wage assumptions, affected-entity counts, information collection burdens, and small-entity analysis.

**Weaker support**

* Benefits are described more qualitatively than costs.
* Some estimates depend on assumptions the agency itself invites comment on, including the 15 percent implementation-cost estimate for adding drug information to existing APIs.
* The record does not fully resolve how to classify edge-case drugs or how to make usage metrics comparable across payer types.

**Overall assessment**

Moderate-to-strong evidentiary support for the general direction of the rule; moderate weakness on implementation assumptions and measurement design.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

The proposal does not show an obvious fatal APA problem on its face. The most plausible vulnerabilities would arise if the final rule:

* keeps the medical-benefit/pharmacy-benefit boundary ambiguous after asking whether some drugs do not fit either category;
* relies on cost or benefit assumptions without responding to contrary evidence submitted during the comment period;
* finalizes usage metric obligations without explaining why the chosen metrics meaningfully assess policy impact;
* fails to justify compliance dates for state programs, small group issuers, or entities facing multiple simultaneous standards changes.

These are comment-useful record-building issues rather than strong reasons to oppose the proposal wholesale.

## Step 16 — Evidence Requests

Commenters could request:

* examples of drugs or plan designs that CMS believes fit the medical-benefit and pharmacy-benefit categories;
* a decision tree for routing drug prior authorization requests between FHIR and NCPDP standards;
* data supporting the 15 percent estimate for adding drug prior authorization functionality to existing APIs;
* sensitivity analysis for implementation costs by payer size, program type, and vendor dependency;
* definitions for API usage metrics, including failed calls, abandoned workflows, transaction response times, denial-reason completeness, and provider-facing success rates;
* testing and certification plans for API endpoint publication and interoperability;
* an explanation of how CMS will use reported API usage metrics after collection;
* small-entity and state-program implementation examples.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

1. **Clarify standards routing for ambiguous drugs.** Ask CMS to include examples, a decision tree, and error-handling rules for products that may be treated differently across medical and pharmacy benefits.
2. **Strengthen API usage metrics.** Recommend metrics that measure successful end-to-end prior authorization completion, not just API availability or call volume.
3. **Test implementation cost assumptions.** Provide or request payer- and vendor-specific evidence on implementation, maintenance, and transition costs.
4. **Request staged compliance safeguards.** Ask CMS to define testing milestones, extension criteria, and enforcement discretion for entities with documented standards-readiness or procurement constraints.
5. **Improve small-entity and provider-facing analysis.** Ask CMS to explain how costs and workflow changes fall on small plans, small vendors, rural providers, and safety-net settings.

The proposal uses existing health IT standards ecosystems to implement CMS program requirements and HIPAA transaction modernization. **Regulatory effectiveness, statutory compliance, procedural adequacy**

A clearer routing framework would improve compliance and make the final rule easier to defend as reasoned decision-making.

**Real-world consequence of invoking the leverage**
