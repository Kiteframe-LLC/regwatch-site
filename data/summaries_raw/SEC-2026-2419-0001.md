## Step 1 — Claim Stress Test

### Claim

CAT costs have grown enough to justify a comprehensive redesign review.

**Underlying assumption**

The budget figures are not only accurate but also sufficient to evaluate whether CAT's design, governance, retention, and funding choices remain justified.

**Failure scenario**

If the cost figures are presented without parallel evidence about regulatory benefits, incident avoidance, investigative utility, or relative performance against legacy systems, commenters may be nudged toward cost-cutting without a comparable view of what would be lost.

**Procedural implication**

High-value comments should ask the Commission to pair cost figures with use-case evidence, avoided duplication evidence, and enforcement-value evidence rather than treating budget size alone as the decision metric.

### Claim

The Commission can sensibly evaluate CAT, EBS, and related systems in one combined inquiry.

**Underlying assumption**

The systems are similar enough in function, risk, and stakeholder burden that a single omnibus inquiry will yield usable comparisons.

**Failure scenario**

If CAT lifecycle data, EBS executed-trade data, and manual request systems solve different regulatory problems, broad combined questions may produce muddled comments rather than comparable evidence.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can press for separate decision criteria by function: surveillance, investigations, large-trader identification, privacy-sensitive customer tracing, and cybersecurity exposure.

### Claim

The post-PII CAT architecture still enables necessary customer-tracing functions through CCID plus separate manual requests.

**Underlying assumption**

The manual-request workflow is timely and workable enough that the loss of direct PII collection does not materially impair oversight.

**Failure scenario**

If manual requests introduce delay, inconsistency, or operational burden during fast-moving investigations, privacy-protective redesign may have created unmeasured enforcement frictions.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters can ask for concrete evidence on investigation timelines, workload, and whether current architecture meaningfully affects enforcement quality.

### Claim

The concept release is open to broad participation.

**Underlying assumption**

Retail investors, civil-liberties groups, and smaller firms can answer the questions in a meaningful way despite the notice's heavy dependence on technical system design, budget history, and confidential operational context.

**Failure scenario**

If only SROs, large broker-dealers, vendors, and repeat CAT participants can answer the questions with specificity, the resulting record may be skewed toward incumbents and operators.

**Procedural implication**

Commenters should ask the Commission to publish simplified baseline metrics, plain-language use-case summaries, and nonconfidential system-performance data so less-resourced participants can comment effectively.

## Step 2 — Essence of the Rule

### Observed Facts

The notice does not propose concrete regulatory text changes. Instead, it requests comment on whether and how the SEC should change CAT, EBS, large-trader reporting, data retention, governance, funding, privacy controls, cybersecurity expectations, and transparency around the comprehensive review.

The notice states that CAT costs rose from a 2016 estimate of about $55.8 million to more than $248 million in the 2025 budget, and that the 2026 budget is about $156 million. It also states that CAT no longer stores customer and account-level information directly, requiring regulators to obtain such information separately through manual requests.

The inquiry includes questions about eliminating CAT features, replacing CAT entirely, retiring duplicative systems, changing EBS, using alternative funding methods, and adopting different security or privacy approaches.

### Inference

This is not a routine request for incremental improvement. It is a broad reconsideration of what the SEC's market-audit-trail architecture should do, how much it should cost, how much personal data it should hold, and who should bear the expense and operational burden.

**Missing links**

The notice offers budget figures and system descriptions but little public-facing comparative evidence about which regulatory uses actually require which data features.

The inquiry repeatedly asks commenters to weigh cost, privacy, and operational utility, but it does not provide a neutral scorecard or common metrics for doing so.

The notice references many prior orders, letters, and exemptions but does not synthesize them into a public baseline that a nonexpert commenter could easily use.

## Step 5 — Missing Alternatives Detector

### Alternatives Evaluated in the Notice

The notice expressly invites comment on:

* keeping the CAT with modifications;
* eliminating some CAT features;
* replacing CAT with a different audit trail or data source;
* retiring partially duplicative systems;
* modifying or replacing EBS;
* using different funding approaches, including alternatives to the current allocation structure;
* changing retention, privacy, and cybersecurity design.

### Plausible Alternatives Not Fully Developed

1. A functional split in which CAT retains order-lifecycle surveillance data while a separate privacy-minimizing request-response system handles identity resolution.
2. A phased decision path that would evaluate use-case-specific retention periods instead of treating all CAT data retention questions together.
3. A public-interest baseline comparing how often specific regulatory tasks rely on CAT alone, EBS alone, manual requests, or combined systems.
4. A participation-support alternative in which the SEC publishes simplified datasets, use-case summaries, and plain-language technical primers for commenters without institutional access.

## Step 6 — Technical Coherence

**Observed Fact**

The notice is internally coherent about the basic distinctions among CAT, EBS, manual requests, clearing-agency reports, and market-data feeds. It explains that CAT is strong on order lifecycle linkage, while EBS remains important for customer and account-level information and large-trader workflows.

**Inference**

The technical coherence problem is not contradiction but compression. The notice assumes significant background knowledge and leaves important operational tradeoffs implicit, especially where privacy-protective design choices may shift burden onto manual requests or separate systems.

## Step 7 — Statutory Fit

**Observed Fact**

The notice grounds the inquiry in Exchange Act oversight obligations and repeatedly asks what regulatory use cases must be enabled for the Commission and SROs to fulfill statutory duties.

**Inference**

That framing is appropriate and likely strengthens the eventual record. The key comment opportunity is to push the Commission to tie each requested data category or system feature to a concrete statutory or supervisory function rather than relying on generalized oversight language.

## Step 8 — Distributional and Equity Review

**Observed Fact**

The notice recognizes civil-liberty, privacy, and confidentiality concerns and invites comments from civil-liberties groups. It also implicates cost allocation across SROs, broker-dealers, and ultimately market participants.

**Inference**

The distributional stakes are wider than the notice makes explicit. Large broker-dealers, exchanges, and vendors are best positioned to discuss system design and cost allocation, while retail investors and smaller firms may feel downstream effects without access to equivalent evidence. Privacy burdens may also fall asymmetrically on ordinary investors whose trading identities can be reconstructed through linked systems even when PII is not stored directly in CAT.

## Step 9 — Failure Modes

1. **Cost-only distortion.** High budgets could dominate the record even if commenters lack equivalent evidence on enforcement value.
2. **Incumbent capture.** The most technically detailed responses may come from entities already embedded in CAT governance or compliance operations.
3. **False comparability.** CAT, EBS, and manual-request systems may be compared as substitutes when some functions are complementary.
4. **Privacy oversimplification.** Removing direct PII from CAT may be treated as solving privacy concerns without fully addressing linkage, access, retention, and request-response risks.
5. **Underdeveloped public baseline.** Broad questions without shared metrics may produce a fragmented record that is hard for the Commission to evaluate transparently.

## Step 10 — Regulatory Incentives

**Observed Fact**

The notice emphasizes CAT cost growth, duplicative reporting concerns, and operational burden, while also preserving the possibility of maintaining or replacing systems for regulatory need.

**Inference**

The structure of the inquiry creates strong incentives for commenters to argue from institutional self-interest. Exchanges and broker-dealers may emphasize cost and duplication; regulators may emphasize surveillance utility; privacy advocates may emphasize minimization and access controls. A useful public-interest comment should force the Commission to show where those interests align or diverge using common factual benchmarks.

## Step 11 — Evidentiary Strength

**Strong support**

* The notice clearly describes the broad functions of CAT, EBS, and other data sources.
* The notice provides concrete CAT budget figures and explains that CAT no longer stores customer and account-level information directly.
* The notice explicitly places major alternatives on the table rather than pretending only one path is available.

**Moderate support**

* The notice's claim that changing market conditions, privacy concerns, and technology shifts justify a full review.
* The notice's implication that cost, privacy, and cybersecurity can be balanced through redesign choices.

**Weak or incomplete support**

* Comparative evidence showing which regulatory use cases truly require which data fields or retention periods.
* Public-facing explanation of how privacy and civil-liberties concerns should be measured against enforcement value.
* Accessibility of the inquiry to commenters without access to proprietary operational experience.

## Step 12 — Administrative Record Gaps

1. **Missing common metrics.** The notice asks broad comparative questions but does not supply a common scorecard for cost, latency, enforcement value, privacy risk, or cybersecurity exposure.
2. **Missing public baseline on use cases.** The record does not quantify how often CAT, EBS, or manual requests are used for distinct investigative and supervisory tasks.
3. **Missing accessibility aids for commenters.** The notice invites broad participation without giving less-resourced commenters enough simplified system-performance context.
4. **Missing clearer privacy-risk framing.** The notice recognizes privacy concerns but does not present a plain-language framework for evaluating linkage risk, access control risk, retention risk, and downstream misuse risk separately.

## Step 13 — Litigation Vulnerabilities

**Low vulnerability at this stage:** Because this is a concept release rather than a final rule, the main exposure is not immediate APA vulnerability but the quality of the eventual administrative record.

**Moderate future vulnerability if unaddressed:** If the SEC later chooses a major redesign or funding shift without first building a clearer comparative record on regulatory necessity, privacy tradeoffs, and system-performance metrics, the present notice may look too open-ended and too dependent on comments from insiders.

# Phase 3 — Comment Generation

## Step 14 — Comment Themes

### Theme 1: Ask for use-case-specific baselines

The highest-value contribution is to insist that the SEC map each major audit-trail function to concrete statutory and supervisory tasks, along with the minimum data features actually needed for each task.

### Theme 2: Ask for comparative metrics, not just open questions

The notice asks many good questions, but the record would improve if the SEC publishes comparable metrics on cost, latency, retention, access, and cybersecurity across CAT, EBS, and any replacement workflows.

### Theme 3: Ask for privacy framing that goes beyond direct PII storage

The notice should not treat removal of direct PII from CAT as the only privacy issue. Commenters can push for analysis of linkage risk, manual-request processes, access permissions, and deletion rules.

### Theme 4: Ask for broader public participation support

The SEC should make it easier for noninstitutional participants to comment by publishing plain-language summaries, nonconfidential baseline data, and examples of the types of evidence that would be most useful.

## Step 15 — Procedural Concern Score

**Score:** 5 out of 10

**Rationale**

The notice is not thin in scope, and it openly invites alternatives. The concern is not that the SEC is hiding the ball, but that it has framed a very technical and consequential inquiry without enough common baseline information for a broad public to engage on equal terms.

## Step 16 — Suggested Evidence to Submit

Commenters could strengthen the record by submitting:

* examples of concrete regulatory tasks that do or do not require CAT's current breadth of data;
* empirical evidence on investigation timing when identity resolution requires manual requests;
* proposals for privacy-preserving architectures, retention limits, and access controls;
* evidence about who ultimately bears CAT and EBS costs and how those costs affect market participation or competition;
* suggestions for public-facing metrics and transparency reports that would let future commenters assess redesign options.

## Step 17 — High-Leverage Comment Angles

1. Require the SEC to publish a use-case matrix linking each data feature to a concrete regulatory need.
2. Ask for side-by-side metrics comparing CAT, EBS, and manual-request workflows on timeliness, scope, privacy exposure, and cost.
3. Ask the SEC to separate questions about surveillance data, identity resolution, and large-trader reporting rather than assuming one architecture should solve everything.
4. Press for privacy analysis that addresses linkage, retention, access control, and request-response systems, not only whether PII sits inside CAT.
5. Ask for participation aids so comments are not dominated by entities already embedded in CAT governance or operations.

## Step 18 — Decision Brief

**Worth commenting?** yes

**Why**

The notice puts foundational questions about U.S. securities-market audit trails back on the table: what data regulators actually need, what privacy protections are adequate, whether CAT's cost and governance are sustainable, and whether legacy or replacement systems should take over some functions. Those choices will shape surveillance capacity, confidentiality protections, and cost allocation across the market.

**Minimum useful comment package**

If time is limited, the most useful comment would:

* ask the SEC to publish a function-by-function use-case matrix for CAT, EBS, and manual requests;
* ask for comparative public metrics on cost, latency, and privacy/security risk;
* identify at least one privacy-protective or cost-reducing alternative architecture and the evidence needed to evaluate it;
* request clearer participation aids so less-resourced commenters can engage meaningfully.
