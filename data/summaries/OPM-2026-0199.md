# Public Summary: OPM-2026-0199-0002

## What This Document Does
- Proposes new hazardous-duty pay rules for federal employees who work on prescribed wildland fires.
- Would add a **25 percent differential** for:
  - General Schedule employees through Hazardous Duty Pay.
  - Federal Wage System employees through Environmental Differential Pay.
- Coverage is not for all prescribed-fire work. The proposal limits it to employees who are:
  - participating as a member of a firefighting crew, and
  - on the fireline, and
  - doing work that directly involves implementation and control of a prescribed fire.
- OPM also proposes a conforming change for GS-0456 wildland firefighters.
- The notice says the employing agencies would decide whether employees meet the criteria.

## Why It Matters
- The proposal would increase pay for a significant number of federal wildland-fire personnel. OPM says about **10,000 GS employees** and **2,500 FWS employees** could be affected.
- OPM’s core argument is that prescribed-fire implementation and control expose workers to many of the same risks as wildfire fireline work: smoke, heat, unstable terrain, falling material, chainsaw work, and fire-weakened trees.
- The record is not empty. The docket includes a supporting attachment from USDA and DOI, plus specific references to:
  - 2,142 reported smoke or inhalation exposure incidents during prescribed-fire operations from 2018-2023,
  - 15 prescribed-fire fatalities from 2003-2023,
  - research on lung-function decline and other health effects,
  - a consensus recommendation from the Federal Prevailing Rate Advisory Committee for the FWS portion.
- The main public-comment opportunity is not whether hazards exist. The questions are:
  - why the right rate is **25 percent**,
  - how the line between covered and noncovered duties will work,
  - whether the cost estimates are transparent enough,
  - and whether agencies will apply the rule consistently.

## Who Is Affected
- Federal wildland-fire personnel at USDA and DOI performing prescribed-fire fireline duties.
- GS employees who may qualify for Hazardous Duty Pay.
- FWS employees who may qualify for Environmental Differential Pay.
- Supervisors, payroll staff, and agency administrators who will need to decide when duties count as covered.
- Crews with mixed assignments may be especially affected if a work period includes both:
  - pre-ignition preparation, and
  - active-fire implementation, patrol, control, or mop-up.
- Agency budgets are also affected. OPM reports FY2026 costs of roughly **$20 million for USDA** and **$12.5 million for DOI**.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: Why 25 percent is the right rate
   - Why it matters: The record supports hazard pay in general, but the public explanation for choosing the same 25 percent rate used for wildfire fireline work is still compressed.
   - Practical question to ask: What comparative analysis shows that prescribed-fire implementation/control duties warrant the same rate as wildfire fireline duties, rather than a lower rate or a narrower trigger?

2. Issue: Boundary between “preparation” and covered active-fire work
   - Why it matters: OPM deliberately excluded “preparation” activities, but crews often perform mixed duties over the course of an operation. If the boundary is unclear, agencies may apply the rule unevenly.
   - Practical question to ask: How will OPM define and document the point at which a duty changes from noncovered preparation to covered fireline implementation/control, especially during mixed assignments?

3. Issue: Administrative consistency across agencies
   - Why it matters: The notice says employing agencies will decide whether employees qualify. Without clear standards, similar crews could be treated differently across units.
   - Practical question to ask: What payroll coding, supervisory certification, or audit controls will OPM require so agencies apply the eligibility standard consistently?

4. Issue: Cost assumptions and future growth
   - Why it matters: OPM provides cost estimates, but the rule also says prescribed-fire activity is likely to continue and grow. That makes assumptions about hours, participation rates, and future burn targets important.
   - Practical question to ask: Please provide the main assumptions and sensitivity ranges behind the USDA and DOI cost estimates, including how projected growth in prescribed-fire operations was handled.

5. Issue: Limited discussion of alternatives
   - Why it matters: The notice explains one important narrowing change, but it does not say much about whether OPM considered other options such as lower-rate, phase-in, or more task-specific eligibility standards.
   - Practical question to ask: What alternative rate structures or narrower eligibility approaches did OPM consider, and why were they rejected?

## Evidence Quotes
- “The proposed new differentials would apply to GS and FWS employees participating as a member of a firefighting crew engaged in activities on the fireline directly involving the implementation and control of prescribed wildland fires.”
- “Based on updated analyses, physical, chemical, and biological hazards associated with a prescribed fire may affect an employee's short- and long-term safety and health in unavoidable ways, similar to the hazards found in a wildfire...”
- “Safety practices and Personal Protection Equipment (PPE) ... reduce—but cannot eliminate—these risks.”
- “The Committee recommended this change by consensus.”
- “The language approved by OPM in this proposed regulation replaces ‘related to’ with ‘directly involves’ and does not include activities of ‘preparation’ for a prescribed fire...”
- “OPM expects this rulemaking to impact approximately 10,000 GS employees and 2,500 FWS employees at USDA and DOI.”
- “Utilizing current GS and FWS pay rates, USDA has estimated that providing HDP and EDP for prescribed fires would cost the Government $20 million, while the DOI has estimated it to be $12.5 million in FY2026.”
