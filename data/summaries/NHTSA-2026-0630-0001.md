## What This Document Does
- NHTSA proposes a narrow update to FMVSS No. 110, the tire-and-loading placard rule for lighter vehicles.
- Today the standard assumes the placard goes on the “driver's side” in specified locations. NHTSA says that assumption does not fit some automated driving system (ADS) vehicles that have no manually operated driving controls and therefore no obvious driver side.
- The proposal would let those vehicles use the left side of the vehicle instead.
- If none of the listed locations works, manufacturers could still submit drawings or photos to NHTSA and ask for approval of another location.
- NHTSA presents the change as a small modernization step within its broader effort to adapt older safety rules to ADS vehicle designs.

## Why It Matters
- This is a small rule, but it still makes a real policy choice. NHTSA is not just clarifying wording; it is deciding that “left side” is the right substitute for “driver's side” across a category of unconventional vehicles.
- The proposal is probably directionally sensible. A vehicle with no steering wheel or human driver position may need a different placard reference point. Keeping the placard requirement while changing the default location is a modest way to reduce compliance friction.
- The weak point is not that the idea is obviously wrong. The weak point is that the notice gives very little explanation for why the left side is the best universal substitute. Some ADS shuttle or pod-style designs may have unusual access patterns, symmetrical layouts, or service practices that make another placement rule more intuitive.
- NHTSA also says the change will not affect safety, will not create new regulatory costs, and will reduce the need for exemptions. Those claims may all be plausible, but the available record does not provide examples, counts, or analysis to support them.
- Because the proposal is narrow, commenters should avoid overstating the problem. The better approach is to ask for a more complete explanation and a few concrete examples so the final rule has a stronger record.

## Who Is Affected
- Manufacturers developing ADS-equipped vehicles without manually operated driving controls.
- Compliance teams that need a predictable placard-placement rule for nontraditional vehicle interiors and door layouts.
- Fleet operators, maintenance personnel, and inspectors who may still depend on consistent placard placement for tire and loading information.
- NHTSA itself, because the agency says the rule should reduce the need for exemption requests or ad hoc approval work.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: The notice does not explain why “left side” is the best substitute for “driver's side.”
   - Why it matters: The rule may work well for many vehicles, but unconventional ADS layouts could make another placement standard clearer or more durable.
   - Practical question: What vehicle-design examples led NHTSA to choose left-side substitution over other options, such as a visibility-and-accessibility standard or a manufacturer-designated placard zone?

2. Issue: The no-safety-disbenefit conclusion is asserted more than demonstrated.
   - Why it matters: Even a narrow placard rule should explain why the new default location will remain legible, discoverable, and useful in real service and inspection settings.
   - Practical question: What analysis, testing, or operational experience supports NHTSA's statement that the amendment would not affect safety?

3. Issue: The exemption-reduction benefit is plausible but not quantified.
   - Why it matters: If only a small number of vehicles are affected, the benefit may still be real, but the agency should say roughly how large it expects the problem to be.
   - Practical question: How many current or anticipated ADS vehicle platforms does NHTSA expect this amendment to help, and how often has the existing wording created a need for exemptions or special approvals?

4. Issue: The cost discussion is very light.
   - Why it matters: NHTSA says the rule adds no new requirements, but manufacturers may still face design-review, documentation, or alternate-location approval work.
   - Practical question: Can NHTSA provide at least a qualitative estimate of the compliance work saved and any residual approval burden for atypical vehicle designs?

## Evidence Quotes

- “The proposed addition to S4.3 would provide that, if the vehicle does not have a driver's side to affix the placard, the left side of the vehicle may be substituted...”
- “NHTSA believes this addition provides clarity and flexibility for different types of vehicles without affecting safety.”
- “At this stage, the agency has not quantified any potential benefits or costs.”
- “For this rule, NHTSA does not anticipate any new regulatory costs...”
- “NHTSA does not anticipate any safety disbenefits for the proposed changes.”
- “The benefits to this rule would be reduced need for vehicle exemptions from this standard.”
