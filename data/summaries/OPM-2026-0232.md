## What This Document Does
- Proposes to revise OPM's `critical position pay` rules for a narrow hiring and retention tool used for mission-critical federal positions.
- Would make `EX-I` the default maximum pay level.
- Would allow pay above `EX-I` with written approval by the Director of OPM, instead of the current regulation's extra case-specific presidential approval step.
- Would remove the current `exceptional circumstances` and `rare circumstances` standards, which OPM says are not required by statute and are not clearly defined.
- Would allow service agreements and would clarify that some decisions to reduce, not increase, or terminate critical pay are not ordinary grievance, appeal, or chapter 75 adverse-action matters if employees were told the pay was time-limited and reviewable.

## Why It Matters
- This is a legal-procedural NPRM, but it has real effects. It could make it easier for agencies to offer very high salaries for a small number of positions where they say they need exceptionally qualified people.
- OPM's core argument is that the current rules are harder to use than the statute requires. The agency says undefined criteria and extra approval layers may have discouraged agencies from using the authority, especially for pay above `EX-I`.
- The record in this run looks thin. OPM gives statutory history, proposed text, and a short impact discussion, but no separate utilization study, legal memo, or economic analysis is included in the supplied export.
- The strongest issues are not whether critical pay should exist at all. Congress already authorized it. The real questions are whether OPM has shown enough support for:
- deleting the current cautionary criteria,
- treating `E.O. 13415` as the operative written-approval framework above `EX-I`,
- and limiting employee review rights when critical pay is later reduced or terminated.
- OPM also gives only an illustrative cost estimate. It says that if roughly `400` positions eventually received pay above `EX-I`, with average annual increments of `$50,000-$100,000`, the added government cost could be about `$20 million-$40 million` per year. That is useful context, but it is not a modeled forecast in the visible record.

## Who Is Affected
- Federal agencies seeking to recruit or retain people for especially critical positions.
- Employees who may receive critical position pay, especially at or above `EX-I`.
- Agency HR, compensation, and legal staff who would prepare requests, manage service agreements, and handle annual review or termination decisions.
- Employees whose pay could later be reduced or ended under time-limited arrangements without ordinary grievance or MSPB-style adverse-action treatment.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: OPM says current rules deter use, but the visible record offers little concrete evidence.
   - Why it matters: The practical case for this NPRM depends on showing that today's criteria and approval structure are a real barrier, not just an administrative annoyance.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What historical data or agency examples show that the current regulatory standards materially delayed or discouraged legitimate requests for critical pay?

2. Issue: The notice gives a compressed explanation of why `E.O. 13415` satisfies the written-approval requirement above `EX-I`.
   - Why it matters: This is the key legal move in the proposal.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Please explain in more detail why the delegation in `E.O. 13415` supports the specific regulatory change proposed here.

3. Issue: OPM removes vague criteria without saying much about the replacement framework.
   - Why it matters: Deleting unclear standards may improve flexibility, but it can also reduce transparency unless OPM explains what evidence and factors will now matter most.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Once `rare circumstances` and `exceptional circumstances` are removed, what concrete factors will OPM and OMB use to evaluate requests above `EX-I`?

4. Issue: The grievance/appeal exclusion could have meaningful employee-side consequences.
   - Why it matters: Critical pay may be temporary, but employees can still rely on it in practice. Notice quality and documentation standards matter if later reductions will not receive ordinary review channels.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What minimum notice, service-agreement, and documentation requirements should apply before an agency can reduce or terminate critical pay without grievance or appeal rights?

5. Issue: The cost discussion is only illustrative.
   - Why it matters: OPM predicts utilization will stay limited even while making the authority easier to use.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What assumptions support the `$20 million-$40 million` example, and did OPM analyze other utilization scenarios?

## Evidence Quotes
- "the delegation in E.O. 13415 constitutes that written approval"
- "OPM's experience suggests that burdensome regulatory requirements, including additional approval layers and undefined criteria, has contributed to agencies' reluctance to request use of this flexibility."
- "Removing this language promotes consistent and transparent administration of the critical position pay authority"
- "the rate payable for EX-I would serve as the default maximum rate for critical position pay"
- "An employee has no right to grieve or appeal a decision to reduce, not increase, or terminate a critical position pay rate."
- "the resulting incremental cost to the government could range from approximately $20 million to $40 million per year."
