## What This Document Does

- VA proposes to amend its State Veterans Home regulations so it can formally reimburse medical model adult day health care, or MMADHC, through State Home Care Agreements.
- The proposal adds a definition of MMADHC and a definition of State Home Care Agreement to 38 CFR part 51.
- It also revises the payment rule for MMADHC. The proposed rate would be the lesser of 65 percent of the nursing-home prevailing rate or the provider's documented daily cost of MMADHC.
- VA presents this as an implementation rule for 38 U.S.C. 1745(d), the 2018 statute authorizing payment for this kind of care.
- The proposal also updates related agreement language, allows retroactive payment requests in some MMADHC cases, and makes a terminology correction elsewhere in the rule.

## Why It Matters

- This rule matters because payment design can determine whether State homes actually offer MMADHC and whether the program expands beyond the few homes already providing it.
- VA describes MMADHC as a medically supervised form of adult day health care for veterans who would otherwise need nursing-home care. If the program works as intended, veterans may remain at home and in their communities rather than entering an institution.
- The proposal also matters because it converts a payment practice that VA says it is already using into a formal regulation. Once that happens, the chosen formula becomes harder to change without another rulemaking.
- The available record gives a plausible policy story: MMADHC is meant to be more intensive than ordinary adult day health care but cheaper than nursing-home care, so a special reimbursement rule is justified.
- The thinner part of the record is the specific choice of the 65 percent benchmark. VA says State homes preferred that number during consultation, and the impact analysis projects savings if payment is capped at the lesser of 65 percent or actual daily cost. But the public-facing materials do not show much comparison against other options or much detail on how daily cost will be measured.

## Who Is Affected

- Veterans who would otherwise need nursing-home care but may be able to remain in the community with MMADHC support.
- State veterans homes that already provide MMADHC or may consider starting a program.
- State governments that own and operate those homes.
- Family caregivers who may benefit if veterans can stay at home while still receiving medically supervised day services.
- Taxpayers, because VA's impact analysis projects lower expenditures under the proposed lesser-of formula than under continued payment at 65 percent without the daily-cost cap.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

- Issue: The record does not explain the 65 percent benchmark in much depth.
Why it matters: That percentage is the core design choice in the rule, yet the available support mainly says State homes preferred it during consultation and that VA already uses it.
One practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence compared 65 percent against other reimbursement options, and why was that number chosen instead of another benchmark or a direct-cost method?

- Issue: The alternatives analysis is unusually narrow.
Why it matters: The supporting impact analysis discusses only a no-rule baseline rather than comparing several payment designs that might also satisfy the statute.
One practical question a commenter can ask: Did VA evaluate direct-cost reimbursement, different percentage formulas, regional adjustments, or a phased review mechanism, and if not, why not?

- Issue: The daily-cost cap needs clearer accounting rules.
Why it matters: If "daily cost of care" is not defined consistently, similar homes could be reimbursed differently because of accounting practices rather than actual service needs.
One practical question a commenter can ask: What cost categories, transportation rules, and invoice standards will VA use to make sure MMADHC costs are calculated consistently across State homes?

- Issue: The supporting evidence appears to come from a very small set of current providers.
Why it matters: The notice says only three State homes currently provide MMADHC. A formula that works for those homes may not work as well for future providers in other labor markets or transportation environments.
One practical question a commenter can ask: How representative are the current MMADHC homes, and what evidence suggests the same formula will work for new entrants in other States?

- Issue: The projected savings are not stress-tested in the public record.
Why it matters: VA projects transfer savings over five years, but the impact analysis gives limited sensitivity analysis and little explanation of how results change if utilization, transportation costs, or provider participation shift.
One practical question a commenter can ask: Can VA provide sensitivity analysis showing which assumptions drive the projected savings and how robust those estimates are?

## Evidence Quotes

- "There are currently three State homes that provide MMADHC; these three facilities are in New York, Minnesota, and Hawaii."
- "During this consultation, members of NASVH stated their preference for calculating per diem for MMADHC at the rate of 65 percent of the prevailing rate..."
- "VA proposes to continue paying at the 65 percent rate described above but add a caveat that if the daily cost of MMADHC is less, then we will pay the lesser amount."
- "Estimated Impact: VA has determined this rulemaking results in transfer savings of $324,829 in FY 2026 and $2.1 million from FY2026 - FY2030."
