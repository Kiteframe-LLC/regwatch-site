## What This Document Does

- EPA proposes to approve Colorado's request for `Class VI` underground injection control (`UIC`) primacy for geologic carbon sequestration wells under the Safe Drinking Water Act.
- If finalized, Colorado's Energy and Carbon Management Commission (`ECMC`) would become the main permitting and enforcement authority for most `Class VI` wells in the State.
- EPA would keep primacy on Indian lands. The proposal therefore creates a split system: Colorado would run most in-state `Class VI` permitting, while EPA would continue to handle wells on tribal lands.
- The notice says EPA reviewed Colorado's primacy package, including the state program description, statutes and regulations, public-participation materials, an Attorney General statement, and a memorandum of agreement with EPA Region 8.
- The record also says Colorado adopted a new `1400 Series` of rules for `Class VI` wells and intends to use both in-house staff and outside contractor expertise for technically complex review work.

## Why It Matters

- This is a meaningful governance decision, not a minor administrative update. It would move first-line `Class VI` permitting from EPA to Colorado for most wells in the state.
- That matters because `Class VI` wells are the federal permitting category used for long-term geologic sequestration of carbon dioxide. These wells raise technically demanding issues involving site characterization, plume modeling, monitoring, financial responsibility, and long-term protection of underground sources of drinking water.
- The main public-comment opportunity is not to say that primacy is inherently unlawful. EPA likely has authority to approve state primacy if the state satisfies `SDWA section 1422` and the federal `UIC` regulations.
- The stronger question is whether EPA has explained the approval basis clearly enough. The proposal says EPA performed a “technical evaluation” and determined that Colorado's program meets federal requirements, but the surfaced discussion is still fairly compressed.
- The record does contain some important support. The export already includes the primacy application, the program description, and the applied statutes and regulations. Those materials make this a real docket, not a thin one.
- Even so, the public-facing explanation is stronger on listing what Colorado submitted than on showing exactly why EPA concluded that Colorado is operationally ready now for a technically complex program.

## Who Is Affected

- Colorado agencies, especially `ECMC`, which would take on front-line review, permitting, and enforcement responsibilities for most `Class VI` wells.
- Carbon capture and sequestration project developers seeking `Class VI` permits in Colorado.
- Communities and water users affected by how well the program protects underground sources of drinking water.
- Tribal governments and communities on or near Indian lands, because EPA would retain primacy there and the state-federal boundary will matter in practice.
- Commenters focused on groundwater protection, carbon-management permitting, tribal consultation, and state administrative capacity.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. EPA should show more clearly why Colorado is ready now, not just eligible in theory.
Why it matters: The proposal states EPA completed a technical review and found Colorado's program sufficient, but the publicly surfaced explanation remains fairly high level for such an important transfer of authority.
Practical question a commenter can ask: Will EPA publish a clearer crosswalk showing how each major federal `Class VI` primacy requirement is satisfied by a specific Colorado authority, procedure, or staffing commitment?

2. EPA should explain staffing and vacancy assumptions more concretely.
Why it matters: The record says there are five staff devoted to energy-transition programs and four additional positions in recruitment. That is useful, but primacy approval is stronger when readers can see expected workload and minimum staffing assumptions.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What permit volume, review workload, and minimum filled positions did EPA assume when concluding that Colorado has adequate capacity to run the program?

3. EPA should clarify how contractor support fits into state implementation.
Why it matters: The program description says Colorado anticipates using third-party contractor expertise for modeling and risk analysis in complex projects. That may be practical, but contractor use should not substitute for core public accountability.
Practical question a commenter can ask: In what circumstances would Colorado rely on contractors, and how will EPA ensure that contractor-supported review still leaves the state with clear responsibility for technical judgments?

4. EPA should make the oversight backstop more operationally specific.
Why it matters: EPA says it will retain oversight after primacy transfer, but the proposal is thinner on how oversight will work in practice.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What are the expected permit-review touchpoints, audit cadence, reporting requirements, and intervention triggers that EPA will use after Colorado receives primacy?

5. The Indian-lands carveout deserves more edge-case explanation.
Why it matters: EPA would retain primacy on Indian lands while Colorado would administer most other wells. That split may be legally necessary, but the record would be stronger if it discussed how near-boundary or cross-jurisdiction questions will be handled.
Practical question a commenter can ask: How will EPA and Colorado coordinate on projects, plume questions, or enforcement situations that implicate Indian lands or tribal consultation concerns?

6. EPA should explain why full approval now is preferable to transitional alternatives.
Why it matters: The visible record mainly presents an approve-or-not-approve choice, even though narrower options could exist, such as phased transition or enhanced early-years reporting.
Practical question a commenter can ask: Did EPA consider conditional or staged primacy tools, and if so, why were they rejected in favor of full approval at this stage?

## Evidence Quotes

- “The EPA has determined that Colorado's UIC Class VI program meets federal requirements for primacy under SDWA section 1422 and the applicable implementing regulations in 40 CFR parts 144, 145, and 146.”
- The proposal covers Colorado wells “except those on Indian lands for which the EPA retains primacy.”
- EPA says it reviewed “the State's complete program description, copies of all applicable State statutes and regulations, forms, an Attorney General's statement of legal authority, a summary of Colorado's public participation activities, and a Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) between Colorado and the EPA's Region 8 office.”
- “The EPA reviewed the application for completeness and performed a technical evaluation of the application materials.”
- “There are currently five staff devoted entirely to energy transition programs, with four more positions that have been created and are in various stages of recruitment.”
- “ECMC has access to and anticipates utilizing third-party contractor expertise on an as-needed basis, particularly in the areas of modeling and risk analysis for complex projects.”
