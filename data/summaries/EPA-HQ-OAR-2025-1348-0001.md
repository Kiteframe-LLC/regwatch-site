## What This Document Does

- Proposes a technology review and reconsideration update for EPA’s hazardous-air-pollutant standards covering crude oil and natural gas production facilities and natural gas transmission and storage facilities.
- Keeps the current standards for already regulated emission points largely unchanged because EPA says it “has not identified cost-effective developments” that make revisions necessary.
- Proposes a new methanol standard for certain already regulated subpart HH emission points, while saying this would result in no additional control requirements.
- Presents two paths for previously unregulated emission points:
  - EPA’s preferred legal approach says section 112(d)(6) does not require the agency to add standards for those omitted points during this review.
  - EPA’s alternative approach would add standards for several previously unregulated units, including acid gas removal units, some storage vessels, loading operations, natural-gas-driven process controllers, and pumps.
- Proposes to revise the definition of “associated equipment” so glycol dehydrators and certain storage vessels at production field facilities are no longer excluded from the anti-aggregation rule in Clean Air Act section 112(n)(4).
- Includes technical corrections and targeted requests for comment.

## Why It Matters

- The key dispute is how far EPA must go during a periodic technology review. EPA says it can defer standards for previously unregulated emission points under section 112(d)(6), but it also presents a fallback package that would regulate many of those same units now.
- EPA’s no-revision decision for already regulated points depends on repeated statements that stronger controls were not “cost effective,” yet the public-facing text does not clearly show the benchmark behind that conclusion.
- EPA also says the fallback standards would impose only minimal costs and no emissions impacts because many facilities already meet them.
- The “associated equipment” revision could narrow major-source coverage at production-field facilities. EPA says it likely has deregulatory cost savings but cannot quantify them in the notice.
- The record appears substantial, not thin. The export points to a Background Technical Support Document, Economic Impact Analysis materials, ICR supporting statements, and workbooks. The main comment opportunity is to force EPA to explain key conclusions more clearly.

## Who Is Affected

- Owners and operators of crude oil and natural gas production facilities, including well pads, gathering and boosting stations, centralized production facilities, natural gas processing plants, and related equipment.
- Owners and operators of natural gas transmission and storage facilities.
- Facilities with glycol dehydrators, storage vessels, transport loading operations, acid gas removal units, pneumatic controllers, and pumps.
- Companies that may gain from a narrower major-source determination if the associated-equipment definition is revised as proposed.
- Nearby communities concerned about hazardous air pollutant emissions from oil and gas operations.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. EPA’s legal theory for deferring standards on previously unregulated emission points
- Why it matters: EPA says section 112(d)(6) does not obligate it to regulate omitted emission points during this review, yet it also presents a developed alternative package for those same points.
- One practical question a commenter can ask: Why does section 112(d)(6) allow deferral here, and how does that reading fit with the litigation and reconsideration history described in the rule?

2. The unexplained cost-effectiveness screen behind the no-revision decision
- Why it matters: EPA’s decision not to tighten existing standards rests on the statement that no “cost-effective developments” were found, but the public-facing text does not clearly show the benchmark.
- One practical question a commenter can ask: What cost-effectiveness thresholds and assumptions did EPA apply when it concluded that stronger controls were not necessary?

3. The claim that alternative standards would have minimal costs and no emissions impacts
- Why it matters: EPA appears to be saying many facilities already meet the alternative standards, so regulation would mostly formalize current practice.
- One practical question a commenter can ask: How many facilities would need new procedures, monitoring, documentation, or equipment changes to comply?

4. The deregulatory “associated equipment” change without quantified consequences
- Why it matters: EPA says the reinterpretation likely reduces burdens and costs, but also says it cannot quantify those effects.
- One practical question a commenter can ask: How many production-field units would stop being treated as major sources, or avoid future major-source treatment, under the revised associated-equipment definition?

5. Historic assumptions used for controllers, pumps, and loading operations
- Why it matters: EPA explicitly asks commenters to support or refute assumptions about what facilities were already doing in 1999, and those assumptions help determine MACT floors for existing sources.
- One practical question a commenter can ask: What historic evidence supports EPA’s assumptions about submerged loading, low-bleed controllers, and zero-emission pumps, and how sensitive are the MACT-floor outcomes to different baseline assumptions?
