## What This Document Does
- EPA proposes to approve part of Wisconsin's Moderate ozone SIP package for three Wisconsin areas under the 2015 ozone standard: the Milwaukee area, Kenosha County, and Sheboygan County.
- The proposed approvals cover five main pieces: the `2017` base-year emissions inventory, the `15%` reasonable-further-progress demonstration, the `2023` motor-vehicle emissions budgets, the motor-vehicle inspection and maintenance program certification, and the nonattainment new source review certification.
- EPA is also starting the transportation-conformity adequacy process for the `2023` budgets.
- EPA is not deciding the full Wisconsin Moderate-area package here. The notice says the attainment demonstration, RACM demonstration, and contingency measures will be addressed in a separate action.

## Why It Matters
- This is a partial approval with real practical consequences. Even though EPA is postponing some of the most outcome-oriented issues, the current notice would still approve the planning foundation and conformity budgets that can shape transportation and permitting decisions.
- The Milwaukee supporting plan makes clear that this is not a simple local-emissions story. It describes a Lake Michigan ozone problem driven by both regional transport and lake-breeze meteorology. That matters because commenters can reasonably ask EPA to explain more clearly what the approved local planning elements can do, and what their limits are, in a transport-heavy setting.
- The strongest issue is record clarity, not absence of analysis. The supplied materials show substantial technical work for Milwaukee, including emissions inventories and a state claim that NOx falls by about `22%` and VOC by about `16%` between `2017` and `2023`. But EPA is proposing the same approval posture for three areas at once, while the surfaced support in the review package is much fuller for Milwaukee than for the other two areas.
- The split structure also matters because commenters are being asked to assess inventories, RFP, budgets, I/M, and NNSR now while attainment, RACM, and contingency measures are deferred.

## Who Is Affected
- Residents of the three Wisconsin ozone nonattainment areas, especially shoreline and near-shore communities affected by ozone planning decisions.
- Metropolitan planning organizations, state transportation agencies, and federal transportation agencies, because the `2023` budgets may become the conformity ceilings they must use.
- Facilities that may need ozone-related permits, because EPA is relying on Wisconsin's existing SIP-approved NNSR structure rather than presenting new permitting text.
- State and local air regulators, because this action would approve key planning elements now while leaving omitted attainment-related pieces for later.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. EPA should explain the split action more clearly.
   - Why it matters: The notice approves several important planning elements now but postpones attainment, RACM, and contingency measures, which makes it harder to understand how the full package fits together.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Why is it appropriate to approve these pieces now, and could the later action on attainment, RACM, or contingency measures change the practical meaning of the approvals proposed here?

2. EPA should surface equal area-specific support for all three areas.
   - Why it matters: The supplied package gives much clearer technical visibility into Milwaukee than into Kenosha or Sheboygan.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Will EPA provide a short area-by-area explanation or table showing the main technical basis for each approved element in Milwaukee, Kenosha, and Sheboygan?

3. EPA should justify the transportation-budget assumptions in more plain language.
   - Why it matters: The budgets have direct conformity consequences, and the NPRM says Wisconsin added a `7.5%` VMT increase for Milwaukee and Kenosha and `15%` for Sheboygan.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What is the practical basis for those VMT cushions, and did EPA or Wisconsin test alternative assumptions or sensitivity cases?

4. EPA should explain how older I/M and NNSR approvals map to current Moderate-area obligations.
   - Why it matters: EPA is relying on legacy approved programs to satisfy present requirements after reclassification and revised nonattainment boundaries.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Can EPA provide a clear crosswalk showing how the earlier approved I/M and NNSR provisions satisfy the current Moderate-area requirements for each of the three areas?

5. EPA should connect the transport discussion to the approved local planning tools more directly.
   - Why it matters: The Milwaukee plan emphasizes that Lake Michigan transport and mesoscale meteorology are major drivers of ozone problems, which may limit what local planning tools can accomplish by themselves.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: How does EPA account for the transport-heavy nature of these ozone problems when concluding that the approved inventories, RFP showing, budgets, and legacy program certifications are sufficient for this stage?
