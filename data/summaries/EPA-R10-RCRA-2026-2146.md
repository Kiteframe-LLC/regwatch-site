## What This Document Does
- Proposes to give Alaska final authorization to run most of its hazardous-waste program in place of EPA's direct federal Subtitle C program.
- Says Alaska has adopted the core hazardous-waste rules closely enough to federal law for approval under RCRA section 3006 and 40 CFR part 271.
- Explains that some functions would still stay with EPA, including certain HSWA requirements, import/export rules, land-disposal restrictions, e-manifest functions, delisting, Indian country, and areas of exclusive federal jurisdiction.
- Identifies several Alaska provisions as more stringent than federal law, such as annual reporting or notification requirements and incorporation of groundwater-monitoring guidance.
- Identifies some Alaska provisions as broader in scope rather than federally authorized, especially Alaska-specific siting rules for new hazardous-waste facilities.
- Tentatively accepts Alaska's decision to add electronic waste as a state-only universal-waste stream.

## Why It Matters
- This is not just a paperwork action. If finalized, it shifts a large share of day-to-day hazardous-waste permitting and administration in Alaska from EPA to the State.
- The proposal rests on a real administrative record, not a thin one. The docket includes a program description, legal materials, a memorandum of agreement, an attorney general statement, and a completeness determination.
- The stronger comment opportunities are about clarity and explanation. EPA often states its conclusions in a compressed way rather than showing the reasoning path in the NPRM itself.
- The main practical question is how clearly facilities, transporters, and generators will understand the boundary between state-administered duties and federal-only duties after authorization.
- A second important question is whether EPA has explained unusual Alaska-specific choices well enough, especially the state-only treatment of electronic waste as universal waste without using the federal petition subpart.
- A third issue is whether EPA has shown enough about how oversight will work during the transition from federal administration to state administration.

## Who Is Affected
- Hazardous-waste generators, transporters, and treatment, storage, and disposal facilities in Alaska.
- Regulated entities that need to know whether a specific requirement will be enforced by Alaska, EPA, or both.
- Tribal governments, Alaska Native stakeholders, and entities operating in or near Indian country or areas of exclusive federal jurisdiction.
- EPA Region 10 and Alaska regulators.
- Commenters concerned about state-specific universal-waste, siting, and enforcement choices.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: The state/federal authority split is legally described but not operationally mapped clearly enough
   - Why it matters: The NPRM lists several functions that remain federal, but regulated parties may still struggle to understand what changes on day one of authorization and what does not.
   - Practical question to ask: Can EPA publish a simple crosswalk showing which Subtitle C functions transfer to Alaska and which remain federal?

2. Issue: EPA's electronic-waste reasoning is too compressed
   - Why it matters: EPA says Alaska may include electronic waste in its universal-waste program if it satisfies the criteria of 40 CFR 273.81, but the NPRM does not explain in plain terms how EPA reached that judgment.
   - Practical question to ask: What criteria did EPA use to conclude that Alaska's state-only electronic-waste category is appropriate without adopting 40 CFR part 273 subpart G?

3. Issue: The equivalence finding is asserted more than shown
   - Why it matters: EPA says Alaska adopted the federal program almost verbatim, with some exceptions and modifications, but the NPRM does not summarize the most important comparison points for readers who are not going through the full application packet.
   - Practical question to ask: Can EPA summarize the key state/federal comparison issues and explain why the Alaska-specific differences do not undermine equivalence or consistency?

4. Issue: Enforcement and oversight mechanics need a more practical explanation
   - Why it matters: The docket appears to contain a real memorandum of agreement and legal support, but the NPRM only briefly explains how oversight and intervention will work after approval.
   - Practical question to ask: How will EPA monitor early implementation and respond if problems arise during the first phase of authorization?

5. Issue: The NPRM does not discuss transition-focused alternatives
   - Why it matters: EPA may be right that Alaska is ready for authorization, but the record would be stronger if it explained why a fuller implementation guide, phased clarification, or other transition tools were unnecessary.
   - Practical question to ask: Did EPA consider any added transition measures to reduce confusion, and if so, why were they rejected?

## Evidence Quotes

- "Therefore, the EPA proposes to grant Alaska final authorization to operate its hazardous waste program in the State in lieu of the Federal program, subject to the authority retained by the EPA in accordance with RCRA, including HSWA."

- "The EPA has tentatively determined that Alaska can adequately enforce compliance with its hazardous waste management regulations."
