## What This Document Does
- EPA proposes to roll back part of its April 26, 2024 PFAS drinking-water rule.
- The rollback would remove the federal drinking-water standards tied to PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA (GenX), and the hazard-index mixture built from those substances plus PFBS.
- EPA would keep the 2024 PFOA and PFOS standards in place.
- EPA says the rollback is needed because, for the rescinded PFAS, the agency used the wrong legal sequence under the Safe Drinking Water Act when it proposed and finalized the 2024 rule.
- In plain terms, EPA now says it should have finished the "determination to regulate" step before proposing the drinking-water standard, instead of doing those steps together.
- EPA also says the proposal would cut compliance burdens for public water systems and related state or tribal regulators.

## Why It Matters
- This is not a small technical cleanup. It would remove recently finalized federal standards for four PFAS-related drinking-water actions while leaving the PFOA/PFOS standards behind.
- EPA frames the case as a legal correction, not as a new scientific conclusion that these PFAS no longer matter.
- That distinction matters for commenters. The central question is not whether PFAS are harmful in the abstract. The central questions are whether EPA is right about the legal defect, whether full rescission is the right remedy, and whether the agency has been transparent enough about the cost of losing those protections.
- The preamble gives high-level numbers. It says removing the PFHxS NPDWR would save about `$11.6 million` per year in annualized quantified costs, and that the proposal would reduce previously expected benefits by about `$6.7 million`, with additional nonquantifiable effects on both sides.
- Those figures make the record look balanced at first glance, but they are still summary-level. The notice does not fully unpack, in the public-facing text, how those values were built, how many systems drive them, or which communities would lose the most protection.
- The proposal also raises a second legal issue. EPA cites authority saying revised drinking-water regulations generally must "maintain, or provide for greater, protection of the health of persons." EPA may believe that principle does not block rescission here because it views the 2024 action as unlawfully issued. But that bridge needs to be explained clearly for the record.

## Who Is Affected
- Community water systems and non-transient non-community water systems that would no longer have to meet the rescinded PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and hazard-index requirements.
- State, tribal, and territorial drinking-water regulators that would no longer have to administer those parts of the 2024 PFAS framework.
- Communities served by systems with these PFAS concerns, especially where treatment or planning had already begun because of the 2024 rule.
- Public-health groups, water-system operators, and local governments that may have different views on whether legal correction should outweigh the loss of near-term protective standards.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. EPA explains the legal defect more clearly than it explains the remedy.
- Why it matters: even if EPA is right that the 2024 sequence was unlawful, commenters can still ask why full rescission is the right next step instead of a narrower correction.
- Practical question: Why can EPA not preserve the standards temporarily while it runs a new sequential determination-and-rule process?

2. The proposal needs a clearer answer on SDWA's health-protection floor.
- Why it matters: the notice itself cites authority suggesting revised NPDWRs cannot reduce health protection, yet this proposal would plainly remove some protections.
- Practical question: How does EPA reconcile this rollback with the statutory and case-law principle that drinking-water revisions must maintain or increase protection?

3. The cost and benefit discussion is still too summary-level.
- Why it matters: EPA cites savings and foregone benefits, but the public-facing record does not fully show the assumptions, affected-system counts, and uncertainty behind those numbers.
- Practical question: Will EPA place in the docket the full modeling and assumptions behind the cited savings, foregone benefits, and nonquantifiable effects?

4. The notice says alternatives were considered, but it does not show much of that comparison.
- Why it matters: commenters need to know whether EPA tested options short of full rescission, such as stays, phased correction, or temporary preservation.
- Practical question: What specific alternatives did EPA evaluate, and why were they rejected?

5. Transitional impacts on water systems are underexplained.
- Why it matters: some systems may already have adjusted treatment planning, monitoring plans, or procurement decisions because of the 2024 integrated PFAS rule.
- Practical question: How many systems have already planned or invested around the rescinded requirements, and how would EPA manage that transition?

6. Distributional impacts need more visibility.
- Why it matters: a national savings figure can hide where the lost protections fall, including on smaller systems or communities with higher contamination burdens.
- Practical question: Which types of systems and communities account for the largest share of the foregone benefits if EPA finalizes this rollback?

## Evidence Quotes
- "The EPA is proposing to rescind its determinations to regulate three PFAS individually-PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA-and any mixture containing two or more of PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and PFBS through a hazard index (HI)."
- "The EPA proposes to rescind the relevant regulatory determinations, MCLGs, and MCLs because they were promulgated using an unlawful procedure."
- "The Act thus requires ... a preliminary regulatory determination, a public comment period, and a final regulatory determination; and ... a proposed substantive regulation no earlier than the final regulatory determination."
- "Removing the PFHxS NPDWR is estimated to save $11.6 million (in 2022 dollars) in annualized quantified costs per year."
- "This proposed deregulatory action is expected to yield a reduction in previously expected benefits of approximately $6.7 million."
- "This action does not impact the MCLGs, MCLs or the regulatory provisions ... related to" PFOA and PFOS.
