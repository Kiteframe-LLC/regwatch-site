## What This Document Does

- OMB proposes a broad rewrite of the government-wide rules for Federal financial assistance in 2 CFR.
- The proposal goes well beyond technical cleanup. It would change how Federal grants and cooperative agreements are designed, monitored, paid, terminated, and conditioned across many agencies.
- Major changes described in the record include:
  - ending fixed amount awards and fixed amount subawards;
  - requiring more payment justification and stronger pre-payment screening;
  - requiring recipients and subrecipients to use E-Verify for covered workers under Federal awards;
  - strengthening subaward reporting and oversight;
  - expanding or clarifying termination and suspension authority;
  - revising domestic preference requirements;
  - restricting some foreign collaborations and publication-cost charges; and
  - revising nondiscrimination and other national-policy provisions.
- OMB says these changes are meant to improve transparency, accountability, and oversight, clarify OMB's legal role in setting these rules, and reduce burden in some areas.

## Why It Matters

- These rules sit near the center of the Federal grant system. Changes here can affect agencies, States, local governments, universities, nonprofits, Tribal entities, and private organizations that receive or pass through Federal funds.
- The proposal is important not just because of any single section, but because it bundles many cross-cutting controls into one package.
- The available record shows a real analytical spine: the NPRM itself, a separate Regulatory Impact Analysis, and a separate small-entity analysis referenced by the preamble.
- The main weakness is not a missing record. It is that several big conclusions appear more qualitative than quantified.
- OMB relies heavily on examples of waste, fraud, policy misuse, and inconsistent agency practice, then uses those examples to justify wide government-wide changes. That may be enough for some provisions, but it leaves room to ask whether each specific amendment is matched to a clearly documented problem.
- The Regulatory Impact Analysis also appears to rely mostly on qualitative benefits while describing several new compliance costs as minimal to moderate and asking for comment on some of them.
- That creates a practical comment opportunity: ask OMB to show more clearly what problems are widespread, what burdens are removed, what new burdens are added, and why narrower alternatives were rejected.

## Who Is Affected

- Federal grant-making agencies that implement or conform to OMB's subtitle A rules.
- Prime recipients of Federal assistance, including nonprofits, universities, States, local governments, Tribal governments, hospitals, and other organizations.
- Pass-through entities and subrecipients, especially where subaward design, payment practices, and reporting would change.
- Research performers and program operators that may be affected by publication-cost limits, foreign-collaboration restrictions, or domestic preference terms.
- Small organizations may be especially interested in the rule's claim that the proposal will not have a significant direct economic effect on a substantial number of small entities.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

- **Issue: OMB's overall problem statement may be broader than the record clearly demonstrates.**
  Why it matters: the rule cites examples of waste and policy abuse, but commenters can ask whether those examples support a government-wide rewrite of many grant rules rather than more targeted reforms.
  Practical question: What program-level evidence did OMB use to match each major amendment to a documented oversight failure or gap?

- **Issue: The proposal's alternatives analysis looks real but still thin in important places.**
  Why it matters: the RIA says OMB considered alternatives, yet the reviewed material often explains why the status quo is not enough without fully comparing narrower middle-ground options.
  Practical question: Why were risk-based thresholds, phased rollouts, pilot programs, or narrower exceptions not adopted for the most burdensome changes?

- **Issue: The burden-reduction narrative needs a clearer accounting.**
  Why it matters: the rule says it reduces burden, but it also adds payment justifications, E-Verify obligations, pre-payment checks, subaward oversight demands, and tighter cost allowability rules.
  Practical question: Can OMB provide a table showing which requirements are removed, which are added, and which recipient categories bear the net burden?

- **Issue: E-Verify and pre-payment screening deserve sharper implementation detail.**
  Why it matters: these are cross-cutting operational mandates that may work very differently across program types, staffing models, and urgency levels.
  Practical question: What exceptions, timing rules, safe harbors, or program-type distinctions would OMB recognize to avoid unnecessary disruption?

- **Issue: The small-entity conclusion appears contestable from the available record.**
  Why it matters: OMB says most small-entity effects are indirect, but some recipients and subrecipients may face direct compliance changes in payroll, procurement, reporting, and payment systems.
  Practical question: What concrete analysis supports OMB's conclusion that a substantial number of small entities will not face significant direct economic effects?
