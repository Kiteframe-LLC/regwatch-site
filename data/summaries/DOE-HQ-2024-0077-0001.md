## What This Document Does
- Updates DOE's general pricing rule for materials, goods, and services sold outside the Federal Government.
- Rewrites 10 CFR part 1009 to modernize definitions, restate the default "full cost" rule, expand the list of exclusions, and add a new "special pricing activities" section.
- Sets a new public-facing Federal Administrative Charge for non-federal reimbursable work at 1 percent of direct plus indirect costs, starting October 1, 2025.
- Clarifies that DOE contractors, foreign entities, governments, and international organizations can fall within the rule's scope.
- Adds explicit carveouts for many activities already governed by separate statutes or regulations, including isotope sales, uranium transfers, certain information requests, loan program fees, and real property.

## Why It Matters
- The proposal is framed as a cleanup of a pricing rule that has not been updated since 1980, but it changes the public rule that tells outside parties when DOE will charge full cost, when it will not, and when it may negotiate or waive charges.
- DOE's clearest policy move is lowering the Federal Administrative Charge from 3 percent to 1 percent for covered non-federal reimbursable work. DOE says that reduction would cut aggregate costs for non-federal entities by about $4 million per year based on 2022 work volumes.
- That matters because it is one of the few quantified points in the NPRM. The notice gives the result, but not the underlying calculation in the available record.
- DOE also says the proposal improves transparency by giving a more comprehensive list of exclusions and by aligning the regulation with current statutes and DOE practice. That likely helps as a legal map of categories.
- The practical transparency gain is less clear. The proposal still says pricing information will usually come from the DOE facility or office providing the good or service, and DOE often can provide only an estimate rather than a fixed price.
- The proposal also adds several discretionary or negotiated pricing areas, including nuclear-material-removal arrangements, user-facility incremental charges, and negotiated royalty rates. The public question is whether DOE has given enough standards for consistent use of that discretion.

## Who Is Affected
- Universities, companies, utilities, researchers, nonprofits, foreign entities, and other non-federal users that buy DOE materials or services or enter reimbursable arrangements with DOE facilities.
- DOE contractors and DOE laboratories, because the proposal expressly applies the rule to contractors and distinguishes between NNSA and non-NNSA user facilities.
- Technology-transfer users, reactor operators, museum visitors, and parties involved in specialized DOE programs that may fall under exclusions or special-pricing provisions.
- Program offices and facility managers inside DOE, because they will continue to play a large role in how prices are communicated and how some discretionary pricing decisions are applied.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. DOE does not fully show why 1 percent is the right Federal Administrative Charge.
- Why it matters: the NPRM gives the new rate and a savings estimate, but not the public calculation showing why 1 percent is appropriate rather than another rate up to the 3 percent statutory cap.
- Practical question: What cost methodology, work-volume assumptions, and alternative rates did DOE evaluate before deciding that 1 percent was the correct public-facing charge?

2. The proposal promises transparency, but most practical pricing information still appears decentralized.
- Why it matters: outside parties may still need to contact individual facilities or offices and may receive only estimates, which limits the practical value of the rule's legal clarifications.
- Practical question: Will DOE publish recurring charges, common pricing methodologies, or annual summaries of exclusions and special-pricing decisions in a centralized public location?

3. The exclusions list is broader, but the framework behind it is thin.
- Why it matters: a longer list may help, but commenters still need to know how DOE decides whether a transaction belongs in the default full-cost rule, an exclusion, or a special-pricing category.
- Practical question: What criteria or decision rules does DOE use to classify activities into Sec. 1009.4 exclusions versus Sec. 1009.5 special pricing activities?

4. Several important pricing decisions remain discretionary.
- Why it matters: the rule leaves room for negotiated acceptance fees, laboratory-management approval, incremental charges, and CFO clarification of when the administrative charge does not apply.
- Practical question: What written standards, documentation practices, or review mechanisms will DOE use to make sure discretionary pricing decisions are applied consistently?
