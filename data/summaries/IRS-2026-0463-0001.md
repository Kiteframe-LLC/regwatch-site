## What This Document Does
- This proposal updates IRS regulations after a 2025 statute changed two things: the deduction rule for wagering losses and the reporting threshold for many business payments.
- For wagering losses, the proposal changes the regulations to match the statute's new rule that only `90 percent` of wagering losses are deductible, and only up to wagering gains.
- For information reporting, the proposal updates the regulations so the general threshold moves from `$600` to `$2,000` for calendar year 2026, with inflation adjustments after 2026.
- The proposal also updates backup-withholding rules and gambling-reporting examples so they match the new threshold, including bingo, keno, and slot machine winnings.

## Why It Matters
- The reporting-threshold change is large. The notice says that for tax year 2024 there were millions of 1099, W-2G, and some W-2 filings in the range between the old and new thresholds, and it projects that about `3.6 million` payors could be affected by the statutory change and the clarity this rule provides.
- The IRS and Treasury also estimate that the higher threshold will reduce filing burden by about `$982 million` in 2024 dollars for calendar year 2027 filings. That is the biggest quantitative claim in the record.
- At the same time, the agency says the statutory language is “likely to have no ambiguity” and that informal guidance may already be enough for many taxpayers. That means the strongest comment opportunity is not whether the rule is lawful. It is whether the notice clearly explains the rule's added value beyond statute, instructions, and software updates.
- The wagering-loss part also matters because it affects taxpayers who still itemize gambling losses. But the notice itself says the discretionary effect of the regulatory update is likely small because preparers and tax software will likely update anyway.

## Who Is Affected
- Businesses and other payors that issue Forms `1099-MISC`, `1099-NEC`, some `W-2` forms in narrow circumstances, and `W-2G` forms for gambling winnings.
- Payors that may need to apply backup withholding when reporting thresholds are met.
- Gambling establishments that use reporting rules for bingo, keno, and slot machine winnings.
- Taxpayers who itemize deductions for wagering losses.
- Small entities are a major part of the affected group. The notice says roughly `98 percent` of affected entities are small entities under the agency's measure.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: The notice does not fully explain the incremental need for formal regulations.
   - Why it matters: The agency says the statute is likely unambiguous and that subregulatory guidance may already be sufficient. If that is true, the final rule should explain more concretely what confusion the rule actually prevents.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What specific compliance problems would remain if taxpayers relied on the amended statute, IRS instructions, and software updates without these regulatory amendments?

2. Issue: The large paperwork-burden reduction estimate could use more sensitivity analysis.
   - Why it matters: The notice relies heavily on a projected `$982 million` reduction in filing burden, but the summary discussion does not show much range-testing or uncertainty analysis.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Please provide the main assumptions and sensitivity ranges behind the filing-reduction estimates, including how payer behavior and baseline filing practices were modeled.

3. Issue: The wagering-loss discussion is brief compared with the threshold discussion.
   - Why it matters: The rule changes how the wagering-loss limit is described in regulations, but the notice gives only a compressed explanation of compliance effects and practical implementation.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Please add examples showing how the `90 percent` wagering-loss rule applies in common filing situations, including joint returns and taxpayers who itemize.

4. Issue: Small-entity transition costs are discussed lightly.
   - Why it matters: The RFA section focuses on issuing fewer forms and says the impact is not significant, but short-run systems changes, vendor updates, and implementation costs are not discussed in much detail.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What one-time software, payroll, or vendor transition costs does the agency expect for small businesses and gaming payors when moving to the new threshold?

5. Issue: The rule assumes updated software and preparer practices will smooth implementation.
   - Why it matters: That may be true for many taxpayers, but the record does not explain whether there are residual confusion risks for self-preparers, smaller operators, or niche reporting contexts.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence supports the assumption that software, preparers, and IRS publications will adequately resolve implementation questions without further examples in the final rule?

## Evidence Quotes
- “The proposed amendments ensure that the regulations reflect current law, thereby preventing confusion by individuals and entities who are impacted by the updates to the relevant statutory provisions.”
- “Because the interpretation and application of the newly enacted statutory language is likely to have no ambiguity, the sub-regulatory and informal guidance will likely provide sufficient guidance for taxpayers, and the benefits of the proposed regulations beyond such guidance may be limited.”
- “After adjusting for an expected growth rate to tax year 2026, the Treasury Department and the IRS estimate that 3.6 million payors are potentially affected by the statutory change and the clarity provided by these proposed regulations.”
- “For calendar year 2027, reflecting tax year 2026 returns, the Treasury Department and the IRS estimate that the higher reporting threshold will result in an overall reduction in filing burden of $982 million in 2024 dollars...”
- “The Treasury Department and the IRS project a total of 673,000 taxpayers will take an itemized deduction for wagering losses for tax year 2026.”
- “However, it is expected that tax software companies and tax professionals will update their products and services to reflect the law change even in the absence of updated regulations.”
