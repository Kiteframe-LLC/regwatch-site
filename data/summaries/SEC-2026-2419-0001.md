## What This Document Does
- This document is a SEC concept release, not a finished rule text. It asks whether the Commission should keep, narrow, redesign, or even replace the Consolidated Audit Trail (`CAT`) and related systems such as the Electronic Blue Sheets (`EBS`) system.
- The notice explains what CAT, EBS, and other data sources currently do and why the SEC believes a broad review is warranted now.
- The SEC highlights several pressure points at once: CAT cost, duplication, privacy and civil-liberties concerns, cybersecurity, governance, retention of trading data, and who should pay.
- The notice asks what regulatory use cases truly require CAT, what features could be removed, whether CAT should be replaced, whether EBS should be modified or retired, and whether alternative funding models should be used.

## Why It Matters
- This is a high-impact inquiry because CAT sits near the center of modern securities-market oversight. It was built to let regulators reconstruct order lifecycles across markets, but the SEC says experience since 2016 has changed the picture.
- The clearest hard fact is cost growth. The SEC says CAT costs rose far above the roughly `$55.8 million` estimate used in 2016, reaching more than `$248 million` in the 2025 budget and about `$156 million` in the 2026 budget.
- Privacy and data-governance stakes are also central. The notice says CAT no longer stores customer and account-level information directly and that regulators must obtain that information separately through manual requests.
- The comment opportunity matters because the notice asks broad balancing questions without giving the public enough shared metrics for comparing surveillance benefit, privacy risk, cybersecurity exposure, cost, and operational burden.

## Who Is Affected
- Self-regulatory organizations, broker-dealers, exchanges, market-data and compliance vendors, and firms that bear CAT and EBS reporting costs.
- SEC staff and SRO regulatory staff who rely on order-lifecycle data, large-trader information, and manual requests during investigations, examinations, and surveillance.
- Investors and the public, because audit-trail design affects privacy, confidentiality, cybersecurity risk, and the cost structure of market oversight.
- Civil-liberties groups, academics, and smaller market participants that may care about data minimization and governance but may lack the operational information that large institutions have.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: The notice asks broad design questions without providing a clear use-case baseline.
   - Why it matters: Cost, privacy, and cybersecurity cannot be weighed well unless the SEC shows which regulatory tasks require which data features.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Please publish a function-by-function matrix showing what CAT, EBS, manual requests, and other systems each contribute to investigations, surveillance, examinations, and policymaking.

2. Issue: Cost figures are concrete, but benefit comparisons are not.
   - Why it matters: The notice gives strong budget numbers for CAT, but much less evidence about what regulatory value those costs buy or what would be lost if CAT were narrowed.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Please provide comparative evidence on the enforcement and surveillance tasks that depend on CAT's current scope and how often those tasks could be handled by narrower alternatives.

3. Issue: The inquiry may favor commenters with insider knowledge.
   - Why it matters: Large firms, SROs, and vendors are better positioned than retail investors or smaller entities to answer technical questions about system design and operational burden.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Please publish plain-language background materials and nonconfidential baseline metrics so less-resourced commenters can engage meaningfully.

4. Issue: Privacy framing may be too narrow if it focuses only on direct PII storage.
   - Why it matters: Even without direct PII storage, linked trading records plus manual identity requests can still create meaningful privacy and civil-liberties risks.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: How does the SEC evaluate privacy risk from linkage, retention, access permissions, and request-response workflows, not just from whether direct PII sits in CAT?

5. Issue: The notice treats several systems as potentially substitutable without fully separating their functions.
   - Why it matters: CAT, EBS, manual requests, and other data sources may overlap in some areas but not in others. Treating them as interchangeable could oversimplify the record.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Which regulatory use cases require full order-lifecycle linkage, which require customer identity resolution, and which can be handled by narrower or separate systems?

## Evidence Quotes
- "Importantly, the annual costs of maintaining and operating the CAT have grown well beyond the Commission's 2016 estimate of approximately $55.8 million, increasing to over $248 million per year in the 2025 budget initially approved by the SROs."
- "Although steps have been taken by the Commission and the SROs to manage and contain costs, the 2026 budget approved by the SROs of approximately $156 million remains significantly in excess of the operational costs estimated by the Commission when it approved the CAT NMS Plan in 2016."
- "The CAT is intended to furnish both the Commission and the SROs with timely access to a comprehensive, uniform, accurate, and linked set of trading data..."
- "This transactional data may only be associated with customer and account-level information if such information is obtained separately from Industry Members through a manual request."
- "What are the regulatory use cases that must be enabled for the Commission and the SROs to fulfill their statutory obligations?"
