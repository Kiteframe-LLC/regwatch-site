# Public Summary: ATF-2026-0337-0001

## What This Document Does
- Revises ATF's definitions of `adjudicated as a mental defective` and `committed to a mental institution` in `27 CFR 478.11`.
- Narrows the first definition so it focuses more on severe global cognitive impairment, guardianship-based findings, certain permanent incompetency-to-stand-trial findings, and similar high-severity cases.
- Moves or clarifies that many dangerousness, insanity, and commitment-based cases belong under the separate `committed to a mental institution` category instead of the `mental defective` category.
- Adds procedural conditions for a qualifying civil adjudication, including notice, a hearing, ability to present evidence, representation by counsel, and at least `clear and convincing evidence`.
- Asks the public to comment on the historical meaning of the term, possible public-safety consequences, and how the rule would affect NICS record submission and cleanup.

## Why It Matters
- This is a significant narrowing proposal inside federal firearms law. ATF argues that its existing definition became too broad, especially because it has been used to reach people with isolated functional limits, such as needing help managing benefits or finances, rather than the kind of serious mental disability Congress meant in `1968`.
- The proposal also tries to separate two concepts that ATF says have been blurred for years: being `adjudicated as a mental defective` and being `committed to a mental institution`. That matters because the rule could change which existing records still support a firearms disability and how future records should be classified.
- The strongest comment opportunities are not broad arguments about whether ATF may regulate here at all. They are narrower questions about:
  - whether ATF has shown enough historical support for its new reading of the statute,
  - whether the proposed hearing and counsel requirements fit how state systems actually work,
  - whether shifting cases between the two categories could create gaps or inconsistency,
  - whether ATF is understating the practical cost of reviewing old NICS records and changing submission practices.
- The NPRM openly admits several uncertainties. ATF says many costs are likely sunk, but also says it does not know the details of every state's systems and is asking for more information. That means implementation-focused comments could materially change the final record.

## Who Is Affected
- People currently or formerly subject to firearms-disability records tied to mental-health adjudications, fiduciary findings, guardianship proceedings, or commitment-related rulings.
- Veterans and others whose records may have been influenced by benefit-management or fiduciary determinations rather than direct dangerousness findings.
- State courts, agencies, boards, and other authorities that submit records to the National Instant Criminal Background Check System.
- Small governmental jurisdictions that may need to review old records, adjust submission standards, or update internal procedures.
- Public-safety groups, disability-rights advocates, and legal-services organizations likely to comment on whether the narrowing goes too far or not far enough.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. ATF's historical-language case needs more visible support.
- Why it matters: The rule's core move depends on the claim that `mental defective` in `1968` did not include people with isolated functional deficits. The NPRM states that conclusion clearly, but the visible record is lighter on direct historical evidence than on the conclusion itself.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: What primary historical sources, legal materials, or contemporaneous usage evidence most strongly support ATF's narrowing interpretation of `mental defective`?

2. The proposed procedural thresholds may not map cleanly onto state systems.
- Why it matters: The rule would require things like notice, a hearing, counsel access, and at least `clear and convincing evidence` for a civil adjudication to count. Some state processes may be reliable yet not match that package exactly.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: Which common state adjudication models would no longer qualify under the proposed safeguards, and how many existing NICS records could be affected?

3. The split between `adjudicated` and `committed` may leave edge cases.
- Why it matters: ATF wants a cleaner separation between the two statutory prongs, but it also asks whether some jurisdictions have dangerousness or insanity findings without a commitment that fits the proposed definition. That signals a real implementation risk.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: How will ATF handle jurisdictions where a dangerousness, insanity, or incompetency finding does not produce a formal commitment record that fits the revised definition?

4. The cost analysis for states and other submitters is thin.
- Why it matters: ATF says most review and system-adjustment costs are probably sunk, but also says it lacks detailed information about many state systems. If record review is labor-intensive, the NPRM may be understating transition burden.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: Please estimate the likely staff, legal, and system-review costs for states and other record submitters that must reassess existing `922(g)(4)` records.

5. The small-jurisdiction and federalism analysis remains incomplete.
- Why it matters: The notice says it may impose substantial direct compliance costs on state and local governments, yet also says current information does not justify a federalism summary impact statement unless comments supply more evidence.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: What kinds of data from states or local jurisdictions would cause ATF to revise its federalism and small-entity assessments before finalizing the rule?

## Evidence Quotes
- “A review of both the VA competency process under 38 CFR 3.353 and the contemporaneous public meaning of the term ‘mental defective’ indicates that the current regulatory definitions are not a correct interpretation of the statute in all respects.”
- “Those with isolated functional deficits are not the kind of individuals who were understood to be mentally defective as that term was used in the GCA.”
- “Second, ATF believes its current regulations also fail to properly distinguish between ‘adjudicated as a mental defective’ and ‘committed to a mental institution.’”
- “In a civil proceeding, a determination based on at least clear and convincing evidence.”
- “ATF believes that the costs that states and other sources incur to submit records to NICS or review already-submitted records would not go up for most sources, as the costs to review and maintain systems are sunk costs.”
- “However, ATF does not know the specific aspects of every single state or other source's records-submission and review systems and processes or how each one might change.”
