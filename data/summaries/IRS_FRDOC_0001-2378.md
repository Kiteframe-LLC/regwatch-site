## What This Document Does
- Proposes IRS and Treasury amendments to `26 CFR parts 1 and 31` to match recent statutory changes made by the One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act.
- Updates the wagering-loss regulation so taxpayers may deduct only `90 percent` of wagering losses, and only up to wagering gains, for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025.
- Replaces older `$600` references in information-reporting and backup-withholding regulations with the new section `6041(a)` threshold.
- Sets that threshold at `$2,000` for calendar year `2026`, with inflation indexing after `2026`.
- Updates the gaming-reporting regulation so bingo, keno, and slot-machine winnings use the new `$2,000` threshold while keeping the existing wager-based limitations.
- Applies the reporting and backup-withholding amendments to payments made on or after January 1, 2026.

## Why It Matters
- This is mostly a conforming rule. Congress already changed the underlying law, and the IRS is updating the regulations so the Code, regulations, and tax-administration rules say the same thing.
- The practical effect is significant for businesses that file information returns. Moving from `$600` to `$2,000` means many lower-dollar payments will no longer trigger filing under the affected rules.
- The proposal also matters for people who claim wagering-loss deductions. The rule makes clear that, starting with the relevant tax years, the deduction is no longer `100 percent` of losses up to gains. It is now limited to `90 percent` of losses, still capped by wagering gains.
- The IRS describes the rule as economically significant for review purposes, but it also says the discretionary effects of the regulations themselves are modest because most of the real change comes from the statute. In other words, the proposal is less about creating a new policy than about reducing ambiguity after the law changed.
- The preamble says the higher threshold should sharply reduce paperwork. Its Paperwork Reduction Act discussion estimates a large reduction in filed forms across `1099-MISC`, `1099-NEC`, `W-2`, `W-2G`, and `945`, with roughly `48.9 million` fewer forms and about `16.8 million` fewer burden hours in calendar year `2027`.
- The small-entity discussion points in the same direction. The IRS estimates about `3.6 million` taxpayers are affected, about `98 percent` of them are small entities under the agency's metric, and the average small entity would issue `10` fewer information returns under the higher threshold.
- The main weakness in the record is not the legal basis for the rule. The legal basis looks straightforward. The weaker point is that the public-facing explanation of transition and implementation effects is fairly compact for a rule that will still require businesses, software vendors, payroll systems, and gaming operators to update their compliance logic.

## Who Is Affected
- Businesses and payors that file information returns under sections `6041` and `6041A`.
- Entities that administer backup withholding tied to section `3406`.
- Gaming operators handling bingo, keno, and slot-machine reporting.
- Tax software vendors, payroll and compliance service providers, and tax professionals updating forms and threshold logic.
- Individual taxpayers who itemize wagering losses.
- Small businesses that formerly had to file information returns for payments above `$600` but below `$2,000`.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Transition guidance for gaming-reporting entities
   - Why it matters: The proposal says bingo, keno, and slot-machine reporting thresholds should move to `$2,000`, but the public-facing discussion is brief on how that change should be implemented in existing reporting workflows.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Can the IRS provide clearer examples or a transition crosswalk showing how the revised threshold interacts with existing game-specific reporting practices and form instructions?

2. Small-entity implementation costs may deserve more detail
   - Why it matters: The NPRM says small entities will generally benefit because they will file fewer forms, and that overall logic is plausible. But the notice gives only a limited public explanation of one-time software, vendor, and system-update costs that may occur before the burden reductions are realized.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Can the IRS provide more detail on temporary implementation costs for small businesses and explain how those costs were weighed against the estimated paperwork savings?

3. Backup-withholding coordination could be explained more concretely
   - Why it matters: Section `3406` now cross-references the updated reporting threshold, so businesses may need to update withholding logic and information-return logic at the same time.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Can the IRS clarify how payors should coordinate threshold updates across reporting and backup-withholding systems so implementation errors are minimized?
