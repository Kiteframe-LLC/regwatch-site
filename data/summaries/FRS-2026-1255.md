# Public Summary: FRS-2026-1255-0001

_Source file: `summaries/FRS-2026-1255-0001.md`_
_Detected type: `NPRM`_

## What This Document Does
- Proposes a substantial recalibration of how the Federal Reserve measures and assigns capital surcharges to the largest U.S. global systemically important bank holding companies, or GSIBs.
- Would lower some current method 2 surcharge calculations by updating old coefficients, indexing them over time, changing how short-term wholesale funding is measured, narrowing score bands, and revising several systemic-risk indicators.
- Would require some indicators to be measured using daily or monthly averages instead of a single year-end snapshot, which is meant to reduce incentives for temporary balance-sheet adjustments around reporting dates.
- Would also revise the FR Y-15 systemic risk report, the form banks use to supply data for these calculations.
- Includes its own economic analysis, three alternatives, and administrative-law sections rather than relying only on a short policy preamble.

## Why It Matters
- This is not a minor reporting cleanup. The Board estimates that the proposal would reduce method 2 scores by about 29 percent and reduce GSIB surcharges by about 40 basis points on average. That means the proposal could materially lower capital requirements for the biggest banking groups.
- The Board argues that this is justified because the current method 2 framework has drifted over time. In the Board's view, inflation, economic growth, and changes in the financial system mean the existing fixed coefficients no longer measure systemic risk as accurately as intended.
- The rule therefore presents a classic tradeoff. On one side, the Board says the proposal would improve measurement, reduce year-end “window dressing,” smooth cliff effects between surcharge buckets, and let some firms deploy capital more efficiently. On the other side, the Board openly acknowledges that lower surcharges could reduce firm resilience and increase systemic-stability risk.
- The notice is more analytically developed than many NPRMs. It has a real economic analysis and it evaluates three alternatives. But some of the most important estimates rely partly on confidential supervisory data and special data collections that outside commenters cannot fully inspect from the available record. That makes transparency a central comment issue.
- The strongest comment opportunities are not simple “for or against” positions. They are questions about whether the Board has shown that this exact recalibration is the best one, whether the magnitude is justified, and whether the public has enough information to test the Board's conclusions.

## Who Is Affected
- U.S. GSIBs directly, because their capital surcharge calculations could change.
- Other very large banking organizations and foreign banking organizations that file FR Y-15 data or are affected by related prudential-category measures.
- Market participants who rely on large banks for funding, repo activity, derivatives intermediation, custody, and other financial services.
- The broader public, indirectly, because lower capital buffers can affect financial-system resilience while higher buffers can affect credit supply and market liquidity.
- Commenters with technical or operational knowledge about systemic-risk reporting, short-term wholesale funding, and year-end balance-sheet management.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: The public record may not let outsiders reproduce the most important quantitative estimates
   - Why it matters: The Board says some impact estimates rely on confidential supervisory information and a special data collection. If outside commenters cannot test those assumptions, the agency may be asking for comments on conclusions that cannot be independently verified.
   - Question to ask: Can the Board publish anonymized tables, summary statistics, or worked examples that would let commenters evaluate the estimated surcharge reductions and indicator changes without disclosing confidential firm information?

2. Issue: The proposal's exact calibration choices may still be underexplained
   - Why it matters: The one-time 1.2 coefficient adjustment, the 20 percent short-term wholesale funding weight, and the narrower score bands are important line-drawing choices. Even if the direction of change is reasonable, commenters can still ask why these particular values are best.
   - Question to ask: What sensitivity analysis did the Board run around the chosen adjustment factor, weighting assumptions, and score-band design, and how would nearby alternatives affect capital outcomes and systemic-risk measurement?

3. Issue: The alternatives analysis is meaningful but still incomplete
   - Why it matters: The Board discusses three alternatives, which is better than many notices, but it also admits that some future-adjustment details for alternatives 2 and 3 are unspecified. That leaves room to ask for more complete side-by-side analysis.
   - Question to ask: How would phased implementation, narrower partial recalibrations, or alternative future-indexing rules compare with the proposal on resilience, transparency, and operational burden?

4. Issue: Daily or monthly averaging may impose real compliance costs
   - Why it matters: The Board says the added burden should be modest, but it also asks questions about operational challenges and acknowledges one-time and recurring costs. Some firms may have better data systems than others, and different indicators may justify different frequencies.
   - Question to ask: For each affected indicator, what evidence shows that the chosen averaging frequency is necessary, and what are the estimated one-time and recurring reporting costs by firm type?

5. Issue: The resilience downside deserves more quantification
   - Why it matters: The Board acknowledges that lower surcharges could reduce resilience and increase the probability or severity of crises. That is a major public-interest concern, not a side note.
   - Question to ask: What additional modeling did the Board perform on the systemic-stability cost of lowering average GSIB surcharges, and how sensitive are those results to assumptions about firms' capital and balance-sheet responses?

6. Issue: The analysis focuses heavily on GSIB effects and less on broader distributional effects
   - Why it matters: The rule may affect non-GSIB competitors, market users, and the public through changes in bank behavior, liquidity provision, and crisis risk. Those consequences are discussed less fully than the direct effects on covered firms.
   - Question to ask: How does the Board expect the proposal to affect competition, market structure, and the distribution of risk between very large banks, smaller institutions, and the public if the lower surcharges lead GSIBs to expand activity?
