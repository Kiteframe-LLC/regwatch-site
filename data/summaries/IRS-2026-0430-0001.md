## What This Document Does

- Proposes rules to implement the new 1 percent excise tax on certain remittance transfers under Internal Revenue Code section 4475.
- Applies the tax only when a sender funds a covered remittance transfer with cash, a money order, a cashier's check, or a similar physical instrument. The proposal would add traveler's checks to that list.
- Uses existing excise-tax procedures, including Form 720 reporting and deposit rules, for remittance transfer providers acting as collectors.
- Borrows several definitions from the Electronic Fund Transfer Act and Regulation E, including concepts such as sender, remittance transfer, designated recipient, and remittance transfer provider.
- Rejects Regulation E's 500-transfer safe harbor for low-volume providers because Treasury and IRS say identical taxable transactions should not turn on the provider's annual volume.
- Defines the tax base as the amount transferred to the recipient. Fees, State taxes, and other amounts not sent to the recipient are excluded, while promotional amounts sent to the recipient are included.
- Treats a remittance funded through a provider or agent cashing a check payable to the sender as a cash-funded remittance.
- Includes an anti-avoidance rule for transactions structured with a principal purpose of avoiding the remittance transfer tax.

## Why It Matters

- The rule affects remittance transfer providers, agents, and households that send money abroad using cash or cash-like instruments.
- Treasury and IRS estimate that about 3.6 million households per year have used nonbank money transfer services, with about 1.1 million to 1.3 million likely using cash or cash-like instruments.
- The notice recognizes that unbanked households are especially likely to rely on cash. That makes the practical burden of switching payment methods important.
- The proposal is analytically stronger than a bare implementation notice, but several important conclusions rely on proxy estimates rather than direct data about how remittance customers actually pay.
- The final rule will need clear operational guidance because taxable status may depend on details at the retail counter: cash, checks, check-cashing services, prepaid cards, mixed payment methods, cancellations, and refund claims.

## Who Is Affected

- Senders who make remittance transfers from the United States to recipients abroad and fund them with taxable instruments.
- Remittance transfer providers, including money services businesses, that collect and remit the tax.
- Retail agents such as grocery stores, convenience stores, pharmacies, or other locations that accept remittance payments on behalf of providers.
- Unbanked, underbanked, immigrant, rural, military, and other cash-dependent households may be especially affected if they cannot easily switch to non-taxable digital or account-based payment methods.
- Banks and credit unions are expected to be less affected because Treasury and IRS say their remittance transfers are primarily funded by non-cash instruments.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. Evidence for cash-funded remittance estimates
   - Why it matters: The 30 percent to 36 percent cash/cash-like estimate drives the agency's understanding of affected households, but the notice says public information on actual payment methods is limited.
   - Practical question to ask: Can Treasury and IRS supplement the final record with direct provider, agent, survey, or corridor-specific data on payment instruments used for remittance transfers?

2. Distributional effects on cash-dependent senders
   - Why it matters: The rule recognizes that unbanked households are more likely to use cash, but it does not fully analyze effects by income, corridor, immigration status, rural access, language access, or military overseas context.
   - Practical question to ask: How will the final rule account for communities that cannot practically switch from cash to lower-cost non-taxable payment methods?

3. Small retail-agent burden
   - Why it matters: The RFA certification treats agent burden as attributable to providers or small relative to existing contractual duties, but agents may still face training, point-of-sale, customer-service, signage, and error-resolution costs.
   - Practical question to ask: What evidence supports the conclusion that small retail agents will not experience significant economic burden from implementation?

4. Refund process for canceled or expired transfers
   - Why it matters: The proposal says the sender may file a refund claim when a transfer is canceled or expires, but the sender may not have the records or tax-form relationship needed to make that process usable.
   - Practical question to ask: Should providers be required or encouraged to give senders standardized documentation and instructions for refund claims?

5. Anti-avoidance and payment-method boundaries
   - Why it matters: A facts-and-circumstances anti-avoidance rule can prevent abuse, but unclear boundaries may create uncertainty for legitimate prepaid-card, gift-card, store-credit, or mixed-payment transactions.
   - Practical question to ask: Can Treasury and IRS add examples showing when ordinary use of non-listed payment instruments is not tax avoidance?

6. Implementation timing and good-faith compliance
   - Why it matters: Providers and agents must update systems and customer-facing procedures while the tax is already effective for transfers after December 31, 2025.
   - Practical question to ask: Should the final rule include transition relief, good-faith compliance treatment, or detailed implementation guidance for providers and agents?

## Evidence Quotes

- "Section 4475 imposes an excise tax equal to one percent of the amount of any taxable remittance transfer."
- "The proposed regulations are needed to clarify the scope and application of the tax."
- "The Treasury Department and the IRS do not have readily available parameters and models to quantify the impact the proposed regulations may have on the type of funding instruments used to pay for remittance transfers or on the level of remittance transfers."
- "In addition, the Treasury Department and the IRS estimate that approximately 3.6 million households per year have sent remittance transfers through nonbank money transfer services in recent years, among which 30 percent to 36 percent, or 1.1 million to 1.3 million households, likely used cash or cash-like instruments."
- "Based on the available models and data, the Treasury Department and the IRS estimate that the economic costs and benefits of the proposed regulations would be small."
