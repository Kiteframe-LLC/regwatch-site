## What This Document Does

This FCC notice proposes a broad anti-robocall update to the agency's numbering rules. The main idea is that the FCC should not focus only on carriers that get telephone numbers directly from the North American Numbering Plan Administrator (`NANPA`). It also wants to reach the resale layers where numbers can pass through multiple providers before they reach end users.

The proposal has three main parts.

* It would extend existing robocall-related numbering certifications beyond interconnected `VoIP` direct-access applicants to all providers that receive numbers directly from the `NANPA` and to “resellers of telephone numbers.”
* It would revise `NRUF` reporting so the FCC, the `NANPA`, and state regulators get more detailed information about how numbers are being used and passed along in the secondary market.
* It asks whether the FCC should go further on related issues such as limiting numbering resale to a single level, addressing number cycling, giving state commissions more access to provider-specific numbering data, and giving states more authority to trigger withholding of numbering resources.

The certification piece matters because it would require covered entities to certify that they will not use numbers to facilitate illegal robocalls or spoofing, that they comply with `STIR/SHAKEN` and robocall-mitigation requirements, and that they comply with specified foreign-ownership reporting rules. The reporting piece matters because the FCC wants more visibility into “intermediate” numbers and reseller relationships.

The proposal is therefore not a narrow cleanup rule. It is an attempt to treat numbering policy itself as an anti-robocall enforcement tool.

## Why It Matters

This matters because illegal robocalls often depend on access to large pools of numbers and on weak visibility into who actually controls those numbers. The FCC is arguing that the current rules leave a transparency gap once numbers move beyond the first provider in the chain. If that premise is right, then broader certifications and better reporting could help identify bad actors earlier and make it harder for them to hide behind wholesale or reseller arrangements.

The strongest part of the notice is that overall logic. It is reasonable for the FCC to ask whether numbering policy should do more to prevent abuse, especially where numbers are resold through multiple layers and existing reporting may not show where those numbers ultimately go.

The weaker part of the record is how much evidence the FCC shows for the size of the problem and for the burdens of the proposed fix. The notice relies heavily on state-commission observations, prior FCC experience, and industry reporting concerns. But the available text does not give much quantified support for how widespread reseller-linked abuse is, how often `NRUF` reporting fails in practice, or how much the new reporting system changes would really cost providers. The FCC gives a headline estimate that provider costs would likely be less than `$560,000` in total, but the public-facing basis for that estimate is thin.

That creates real comment leverage. A useful commenter does not need to deny that robocalls are a serious problem. The better move is to ask the FCC to show where the abuse is concentrated, how the new data fields will improve enforcement, and whether narrower or phased approaches would solve most of the problem with less burden.

## Who Is Affected

* Providers that receive numbering resources directly from the `NANPA`, not just interconnected `VoIP` direct-access applicants.
* Resellers of telephone numbers, including a broad set of `LECs`, `CMRS` providers, and interconnected `VoIP` providers that resell service including geographic numbers.
* Wholesale providers that may need to gather more information about downstream resellers and could be pressured to verify whether those resellers have filed certifications.
* State commissions, and possibly state attorneys general or other state entities, if the FCC expands access to provider-specific `NRUF` data or delegates more withholding authority.
* Smaller providers and resellers that may face the same basic certification and reporting duties but have less internal compliance capacity.

Unknown from available text:

* how many providers would actually fall within the proposed reseller definition
* how many existing `NRUF` problems come from deliberate evasion versus system limitations or confusion
* how costly it would be for providers to redesign internal systems to support more granular intermediate-number reporting
* whether the FCC would ultimately adopt stronger ideas like single-level resale limits or broader state withholding authority

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. The FCC has not yet shown enough public-facing evidence that the entire proposed reseller category is the right target.
Why it matters: The final rule will be stronger if the FCC can show whether abuse is concentrated in certain types of reseller chains rather than assuming the whole market needs the same treatment.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What data or enforcement experience shows that the proposed reseller definition captures the main sources of numbering-related robocall abuse?

2. The revised `NRUF` reporting structure needs a clearer implementation case.
Why it matters: Better data is only useful if providers can classify and report the new categories accurately and consistently.
Practical question a commenter can ask: How does the FCC expect providers to distinguish intermediate assigned, intermediate other, and intermediate available numbers in real operational systems?

3. The burden estimate is too compressed to test well.
Why it matters: The FCC says provider costs would likely stay below `$560,000` in total, but the available record does not show much of the calculation behind that figure.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What assumptions, provider counts, system-change estimates, and labor estimates support the FCC's cumulative cost estimate?

4. Stronger structural ideas deserve a clearer alternatives analysis.
Why it matters: A single-level resale limit or broader wholesale-provider policing duties could change competition and business models in ways the notice only briefly acknowledges.
Practical question a commenter can ask: Why should the FCC adopt stronger structural restrictions before testing whether broader certifications and better reporting solve most of the problem?

5. Expanded state-facing enforcement tools need clearer guardrails.
Why it matters: More state access and authority could improve enforcement, but it could also create confidentiality, consistency, and appeal-right concerns.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What proof thresholds, confidentiality protections, and appeal procedures would apply if a state commission can direct withholding of numbering resources?

## Evidence Quotes

* “We now propose to further extend the robocall certification requirements to all service providers that receive numbering resources directly from the ... NANPA ... and also to resellers of telephone numbers.”
* “We explore implementing enhanced reporting and tracking of the use of numbering resources by resellers of telephone numbers as a way to prevent them from enabling robocalls and from obstructing robocall enforcement.”
* “The intermediate number use category ... should be updated to reflect the current secondary market and to make it more useful for identification and analysis of trends in number use.”
* “Staff estimates indicate the costs would likely be less than $560,000 cumulatively to providers.”
* “Limiting resale to a single level has the potential to reduce resale-based competition to a certain degree.”
