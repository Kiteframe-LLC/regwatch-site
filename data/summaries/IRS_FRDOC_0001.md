## What This Document Does
- Proposes Treasury and IRS regulations implementing section `4475`, the new `1 percent` excise tax on certain remittance transfers after December 31, 2025.
- Revises `26 CFR part 40` on excise-tax procedure and adds a new remittance-transfer rule in `26 CFR part 49`.
- Uses Electronic Fund Transfer Act and Regulation E concepts to define key terms such as `remittance transfer`, `remittance transfer provider`, `sender`, and `designated recipient`.
- Treats cash, money orders, cashier's checks, and traveler's checks as the payment instruments that trigger the tax.
- Treats a provider-cashed personal or business check as cash funding when the resulting cash is used for the remittance.
- Adds a broad anti-avoidance rule allowing Treasury to disregard or recharacterize transactions undertaken with a principal purpose of avoiding the tax.

## Why It Matters
- The statute creates the tax, but Treasury still has major discretion over implementation details. This proposal decides who counts as a provider, which payment methods trigger tax, when liability attaches, and how mixed or avoidance-oriented transactions are treated.
- The rule tries to borrow familiar consumer-finance definitions to reduce compliance burden. That is sensible in general, but the proposal also departs from Regulation E where Treasury thinks tax results would otherwise become inconsistent.
- The most important departure is Treasury's rejection of Regulation E's `500 or fewer remittance transfers` normal-course-of-business safe harbor. The notice says keeping that safe harbor could produce inconsistent tax treatment for otherwise similar transactions, but it does not compare many narrower alternatives.
- The economic discussion matters because the tax only reaches a subset of remittance transfers. Treasury estimates that about `3.6 million` households will send remittances through money services businesses each year and that roughly `30 to 36 percent` of remittance transfers are funded with cash. Those estimates appear to rest on layered proxies from Census, FDIC, and other public sources rather than on a clean provider-level dataset.
- The rule also relies on a broad principal-purpose anti-avoidance standard. That can help stop easy workarounds, but it also raises ordinary predictability questions for providers using prepaid products, check-cashing services, and retailer-agent arrangements.

## Who Is Affected
- Money services businesses and other remittance transfer providers.
- Retail agents and other businesses that help accept payments for remittances.
- Consumers who fund remittances with cash or cash-like instruments rather than U.S.-issued debit or credit cards.
- Smaller or occasional providers that may have relied on Regulation E's low-volume safe harbor in the consumer-finance context.
- Tax administrators and commenters focused on burden, market structure, immigrant households, and remittance access.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Rejection of the low-volume Regulation E safe harbor
   - Why it matters: Treasury says full rejection is needed to avoid inconsistent tax treatment, but the notice does not show why narrower tax-specific options would fail.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Why is full rejection of the `500 or fewer` safe harbor better than a modified safe harbor, transition rule, or simplified low-volume compliance option?

2. Breadth of the anti-avoidance rule
   - Why it matters: A principal-purpose, facts-and-circumstances standard can catch obvious workarounds but may also leave ordinary providers unsure which product designs or transaction sequences will be recharacterized.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What additional examples, safe harbors, or objective indicators will Treasury provide so ordinary non-abusive transactions are not swept into the anti-avoidance rule?

3. Thin explanation for the economic assumptions
   - Why it matters: The burden and impact discussion depends heavily on assumptions about how many households use money services businesses, how many remittances are cash-funded, and how sensitive senders are to higher transaction costs.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Please publish sensitivity analysis showing how the burden and distributional conclusions change if the cash-funded share, provider count, or elasticity assumptions differ from the chosen proxies.

4. Need for clearer rules on mixed transactions
   - Why it matters: The proposal covers provider-cashed checks and prepaid-card avoidance examples, but many real-world transactions involve agents, split services, stored value, or delayed steps that may be harder to classify.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Can Treasury add more examples showing when check cashing, prepaid products, retailer-agent conduct, or sequential transactions do and do not become taxable cash-funded remittances?

5. Inclusion of traveler's checks
   - Why it matters: Treasury says traveler's checks are "virtually indistinguishable" from money orders and cashier's checks, but the public record gives little indication of how often this issue matters in the current remittance market.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence shows that traveler's checks remain sufficiently relevant in remittance practice to justify separate inclusion, and what operational problems would arise if they were not included?
