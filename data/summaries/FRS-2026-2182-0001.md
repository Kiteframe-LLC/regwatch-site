# Public Summary: FRS-2026-2182-0001

_Source file: `summaries/FRS-2026-2182-0001.md`_
_Detected type: `NPRM`_

## What This Document Does
- The Federal Reserve Board proposes to amend Regulation D so that a new type of Federal Reserve account, called a `Payment Account`, would earn no interest.
- The proposal also would bar a Payment Account holder from participating in an `excess balance account` or `EBA`, which is a limited-purpose account that can pay interest on certain Reserve Bank balances.
- The Board is pairing this rule with a separate same-day proposal that would impose a `Closing Balance Limit` on Payment Accounts through the Federal Reserve's Payment System Risk policy.
- In practical terms, the Board is trying to design Payment Accounts as transaction-focused accounts for clearing and settling payments, not as places to park value overnight.
- The rule does not change reserve requirement ratios, which remain at zero.

## Why It Matters
- This proposal is about the economic design of direct Federal Reserve access. If Payment Accounts are created, the Board wants them structured so they do not become attractive substitutes for interest-bearing balances.
- The Board's main rationale is balance-sheet control. It says paying zero interest and imposing a Closing Balance Limit should keep Payment Account balances relatively small, which in turn should limit effects on Federal Reserve liabilities and monetary-policy implementation.
- The Board also argues that allowing Payment Account holders into interest-bearing `EBA` structures could undermine that design by letting firms sidestep the no-interest rule.
- The record is meaningful but not especially quantified. The Board openly admits that some indirect effects are difficult to assess and depend on factors such as who uses the accounts, where funds come from, and how much sweeping activity occurs between banks and Payment Accounts.
- That makes this a solid comment target. The agency has stated a clear policy direction, but much of the case for this exact design is qualitative rather than measured.

## Who Is Affected
- Institutions that may seek a future Federal Reserve `Payment Account`, especially payments-focused or nontraditional firms looking for direct account access.
- Banks and other eligible institutions that may compare a Payment Account to existing access paths such as master accounts or correspondent arrangements.
- Firms that might otherwise want to hold overnight balances in a way that earns interest.
- Reserve Banks, which would administer the account category and the related balance-management framework.
- Commenters with expertise in payments operations, liquidity management, banking regulation, stablecoins, settlement systems, or Federal Reserve balance-sheet mechanics.
- Smaller eligible institutions are also relevant because the Board estimates roughly 200 small entities might be affected if they chose to request Payment Accounts.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: The Board's balance-sheet rationale is plausible but lightly quantified.
Why it matters: The NPRM says zero interest and the Closing Balance Limit should keep balances small, but it does not show expected balance ranges under different adoption or rate scenarios.
Question a commenter can ask: What scenario analysis supports the conclusion that zero interest will materially limit balances in normal and stress periods?

2. Issue: Alternatives to a blanket zero-interest rule are acknowledged but not deeply analyzed.
Why it matters: The notice says the Board considered paying interest up to the Closing Balance Limit, yet the explanation for rejecting that approach remains fairly short.
Question a commenter can ask: Why is a blanket zero-interest rule better than threshold-based interest, phased-in interest, or another more targeted design?

3. Issue: The `EBA` ban rests on an anti-circumvention theory that is only briefly explained.
Why it matters: If narrower safeguards could prevent circumvention, a categorical ban may be broader than needed.
Question a commenter can ask: What concrete balance-management behavior would undermine the proposal if Payment Account holders could participate in EBAs?

4. Issue: The notice flags operational complexity but does not explore it much.
Why it matters: One commenter said zero interest could increase end-of-day balance minimization activity. That could create real operational costs even if no new paperwork is required.
Question a commenter can ask: What operational burdens, payment-timing effects, or liquidity-management costs could result from aggressive end-of-day balance minimization?

5. Issue: The Board's low-burden framing may be too formalistic.
Why it matters: Saying the proposal creates no reporting or recordkeeping requirement does not answer whether it changes real incentives, account usefulness, or economic feasibility for potential users.
Question a commenter can ask: How does the Board distinguish formal compliance burden from practical operational and opportunity costs?

## Evidence Quotes
- "The Board believes that paying zero interest and establishing a Closing Balance Limit would support the goal of limiting balances in Payment Accounts."
- "However, the indirect effects of Payment Accounts on other Federal Reserve liabilities is difficult to assess ..."
- "The Board has also considered the alternative of paying interest on balances in a Payment Account up to the Closing Balance Limit ..."
- "Permitting Payment Account holders to participate in EBAs could allow them to circumvent the zero interest rate ..."
- "Another commenter noted that paying zero interest could increase end-of-day balance minimization activity, adding operational complexity."

The most useful comments here will probably not challenge the idea of Payment Accounts in the abstract. A stronger strategy is to press the Board on comparative design: whether the available record really shows that zero interest plus an EBA ban is the best way to keep the account narrow, or whether a more tailored approach could meet the same policy goals with fewer side effects.
