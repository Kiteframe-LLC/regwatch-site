## What This Document Does
- Proposes a new Department of Labor joint-employer framework under the Fair Labor Standards Act by restoring regulations to 29 CFR part 791.
- Distinguishes between "vertical" joint employment, where multiple entities benefit from the same work, and "horizontal" joint employment, where an employee works separate hours for associated employers in the same workweek.
- Would also revise FMLA and MSPA regulations so that joint-employer status under those laws follows the same part 791 analysis.
- Adds rules about certain business practices the Department says should not, by themselves, make joint-employer status more or less likely, including some quality-control and legal-compliance requirements.
- Includes a severability provision and a preliminary regulatory impact analysis.

## Why It Matters
- Joint-employer rules affect who can be held responsible for wages, overtime, leave obligations, and agricultural-worker protections. In practice, that changes risk for workers, staffing firms, subcontractors, franchise systems, and business clients.
- The Department says the current landscape is too fragmented and that this proposal would create more clarity and uniformity. That matters because uncertainty about joint-employer status can shape contracting, compliance advice, investigations, and litigation.
- The notice is strongest when it discusses statutory text and caselaw. It is weaker when it moves from that legal discussion to larger practical claims, especially that the proposal will reduce litigation and compliance costs enough to outweigh large one-time familiarization costs.
- The rule also matters because it does more than restate FLSA law. It tries to harmonize FLSA, FMLA, and MSPA joint-employer analysis, which may be attractive for consistency but also raises questions about whether different statutory contexts are getting enough separate attention.

## Who Is Affected
- Workers whose labor is arranged through staffing agencies, labor contractors, subcontractors, franchise systems, or other layered business relationships.
- Employers and business clients that may benefit from labor supplied by another entity.
- Agricultural employers and farm labor contractors subject to MSPA.
- Employers dealing with FMLA joint-employer questions, especially where one entity is the "primary" employer and another is secondary.
- Compliance staff, employment counsel, and payroll teams that need to decide who bears legal obligations under shared-work arrangements.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: The Department says the proposal will create more clarity and nationwide uniformity.
   - Why it matters: That is the central practical promise of the rule, but courts may still apply different circuit-specific approaches.
   - Question to ask: Which parts of the current circuit split would remain even after this rule, and how should regulated parties handle conflict between the regulation and contrary case law?

2. Issue: The Department says recurring savings and benefits will outweigh familiarization costs, but many of those savings are not quantified.
   - Why it matters: The PRIA quantifies one-time familiarization costs but relies heavily on qualitative reasoning for reduced litigation and compliance costs.
   - Question to ask: Please publish the assumptions, sensitivity analysis, and any underlying workbook for the claim that recurring savings exceed one-time costs.

3. Issue: The notice admits the Department lacks data on how many businesses are or may be in joint-employment relationships.
   - Why it matters: That uncertainty makes it harder to test whether the rule's projected benefits, transfers, or burdens are realistically sized.
   - Question to ask: What additional data sources, industry estimates, or enforcement records did the Department consider before concluding the rule is net beneficial?

4. Issue: The proposal extends one FLSA-centered framework to FMLA and MSPA.
   - Why it matters: Shared definitions support harmonization, but the statutes still operate in different practical settings with different duties and risks.
   - Question to ask: Please provide worked examples showing how the same fact pattern would be analyzed under FLSA, FMLA, and MSPA after harmonization.

5. Issue: The rule says some common business practices should not count much in the joint-employer inquiry.
   - Why it matters: That can reduce over-deterrence in ordinary contracting, but businesses may try to label real control as mere quality control or compliance oversight.
   - Question to ask: Can the final rule add more examples showing when oversight crosses the line into supervision of employees' day-to-day terms and conditions?

6. Issue: The alternatives discussion is meaningful but still leaves narrower middle-ground options underexplored.
   - Why it matters: The agency discusses major alternatives, yet gives less attention to staged or example-heavy approaches that might improve clarity without fully locking in one broad harmonized framework.
   - Question to ask: Why did the Department not analyze a narrower FLSA-only approach or a version with more interpretive examples before extending the framework across all three statutes?
