## What This Document Does

- EPA proposes to delay the near-term criteria-pollutant part of its 2024 Tier 4 vehicle rule for two model years.
- Instead of starting Tier 4 criteria-pollutant standards in model year 2027, the proposal would keep the older Tier 3 standards in place for model years 2027 and 2028 and start the Tier 4 phase-in in model year 2029.
- The proposal covers light-duty vehicles, light-duty trucks, medium-duty passenger vehicles, and medium-duty vehicles.
- EPA also proposes related timing changes for certification test procedures and on-board-diagnostics requirements.
- This is only “Part 1” of EPA’s reconsideration. EPA says broader possible changes to the Tier 4 program will come later in a separate Part 2 rulemaking.

## Why It Matters

- This proposal matters because it would delay pollution-control improvements that were finalized only in April 2024.
- EPA’s basic argument is that the 2024 Tier 4 schedule assumed much higher battery-electric-vehicle sales than EPA now expects for model years 2027 and 2028.
- Under the 2024 rule, higher BEV sales would have made it easier for manufacturers to meet fleet-average non-methane organic gases plus NOx limits and some particulate-matter phase-in obligations, because BEVs count as zero-emission vehicles for those calculations.
- EPA now says lower BEV sales, changed federal and state policy, and automaker product-plan shifts mean manufacturers would need more near-term hardware changes on gasoline vehicles than they originally expected, including more catalyst work, more gasoline particulate filters, more supplier coordination, more calibration work, and more time.
- EPA estimates the delay would save manufacturers about $1.77 billion in present value at a 3 percent discount rate, or about $1.66 billion at a 7 percent discount rate.
- But EPA also acknowledges that the proposal would increase emissions compared with keeping the current Tier 4 schedule. The notice describes those increases as small at the national level, but they are still increases.
- EPA did not do new air-quality modeling for this proposal. Instead, it says the expected ambient impacts are likely small because the emissions changes are relatively small in national onroad inventory terms.

## Who Is Affected

- Vehicle manufacturers and importers are the direct regulated parties.
- Automotive suppliers are indirectly affected because the proposal changes the timing pressure for catalyst upgrades, gasoline particulate filters, and related emissions-control systems.
- Buyers of new vehicles may be affected if manufacturers avoid product-plan disruptions or keep certain models on the market that might otherwise become harder to certify on the original schedule.
- Communities exposed to motor-vehicle pollution, especially people living or working near high-traffic corridors, are also affected because the proposal delays some planned emissions reductions.
- Commenters with useful evidence include automakers, suppliers, fleet operators, public-health groups, state air agencies, and residents or local groups concerned about near-road pollution.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

- Issue: EPA’s central rationale depends heavily on lower projected BEV sales and short lead-time problems for conventional vehicle upgrades.
Why it matters: That is the main reason EPA gives for delaying the standards. If the forecast or feasibility story is weaker than EPA says, the case for a two-year nationwide delay becomes weaker too.
Question a commenter can ask: Can EPA show a clearer side-by-side comparison of how manufacturers could comply under the current Tier 4 schedule using BEVs, ABT flexibility, already-certified Tier 4 vehicles, and targeted hardware upgrades?

- Issue: EPA presents a large cost-savings estimate, but it also says it modeled only one cost case and did not monetize some potential noncompliance and market-disruption effects.
Why it matters: A cost estimate can still be useful even when uncertain, but commenters should know whether the headline savings figure is robust or just one central scenario.
Question a commenter can ask: Can EPA provide sensitivity ranges for different BEV sales assumptions, different GPF deployment rates, and different assumptions about sunk Tier 4 investments?

- Issue: EPA did not do new air-quality modeling even though the proposal would increase emissions compared with the current rule.
Why it matters: EPA says the increases are small nationally, but national averages can hide more concentrated local effects, especially near roads and freight corridors.
Question a commenter can ask: Why is national inventory screening enough here, and what evidence supports the conclusion that localized air-quality changes would still be small?

- Issue: EPA openly asks whether regulated parties have significant reliance interests in the original 2024 Tier 4 schedule.
Why it matters: Some manufacturers may already have spent real money on research, development, supplier contracts, or early certification work for model years 2027 and 2028. That matters because EPA is changing a recent prior position.
Question a commenter can ask: What evidence does EPA have about already-incurred Tier 4 investments, and how did it weigh those investments against the claimed feasibility concerns?

- Issue: The proposal asks about several narrower implementation options, but the visible record still centers on a broad two-year delay.
Why it matters: Commenters may be able to show that a shorter delay, class-specific relief, or narrower flexibilities would solve the real problem with fewer emissions consequences.
Question a commenter can ask: Why is a blanket two-year delay better than alternatives such as a one-year delay, targeted relief, or additional near-term compliance flexibility?

## Evidence Quotes

- “The EPA is proposing to delay the phase-in of the Tier 4 criteria pollutant standards ... to address changed circumstances and feasibility concerns.”
- “The EPA now projects that the share of new light-duty BEVs sold in the market will be significantly lower in MYs 2027 and 2028 than the Agency previously estimated.”
- “This proposal projects a cost reduction to manufacturers of approximately $1.66 billion to $1.77 billion in present value terms.”
- “The EPA did not conduct air quality modeling to determine how these emissions increases could change the ambient concentrations of air pollutants.”
- “The EPA requests comment on whether regulated parties have any significant reliance interests with respect to MYs 2027 and 2028 Tier 4 criteria pollutant standards.”
