## What This Document Does
- Proposes a split classification for blood irradiators based on intended use.
- Blood irradiators used to prevent transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease, or TA-GVHD, would move into class II with special controls and 510(k) review.
- Blood irradiators used to prevent metastasis would move into class III and face premarket approval, or PMA, through a separate proposed order issued alongside this rule.
- FDA says the split reflects different evidence and risk profiles for the two uses.

## Why It Matters
- This decides which uses stay in a class-II pathway and which move into the most demanding FDA device-review pathway.
- For TA-GVHD devices, FDA says there is enough information to use special controls such as performance testing, labeling requirements, software verification and validation, and electrical/electromagnetic compatibility testing.
- For metastasis devices, FDA says the evidence is too thin for class II. The available record says the technique does not appear to be widely used, and the 2023 panel record emphasizes that there is not enough information to show general and special controls would be sufficient.
- FDA's own PRIA says quantified annualized benefits are small while annualized costs are much larger. The PRIA also says the rule would significantly affect small entities.
- That combination makes the rule highly commentable: FDA has a real analytical basis, but several key judgments still deserve clearer public explanation.

## Who Is Affected
- Manufacturers of blood irradiators, especially firms that market metastasis-related intended uses.
- Small device firms. FDA expressly says net annualized compliance costs would exceed 1 percent of average annual revenues for affected small entities.
- Hospitals, blood centers, and clinical settings that rely on blood irradiators for transfusion-related use cases.
- Patients could be affected indirectly through labeling changes, product availability, and how firms respond to PMA costs.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: FDA identifies lower-burden alternatives but does not compare them in much public detail
   - Why it matters: The PRIA says several alternatives would reduce burden, including keeping devices unclassified, using class I for TA-GVHD, using class II for metastasis devices, or delaying implementation by one year. The explanation for rejecting those options is concise.
   - Practical question to ask: Please provide a side-by-side explanation of why the proposed split classification is superior to the lower-burden alternatives identified in the PRIA.

2. Issue: The metastasis branch relies heavily on an evidence gap without fully explaining why class III is the only workable response
   - Why it matters: FDA says the technique is not widely used and that there is insufficient information for general and special controls to assure safety and effectiveness. Commenters still need to know why a narrower or phased class-II approach would not work.
   - Practical question to ask: What specific evidence gaps make class III necessary for metastasis devices, and why would a class-II-plus-evidence-generation approach be inadequate?

3. Issue: The TA-GVHD special-control package is concrete, but FDA could better explain why this set is complete
   - Why it matters: FDA lists testing, labeling, software, and compatibility controls, but the record also reflects dose, blood-quality, and operator-use questions. If the final control package is incomplete, class II may not perform as intended.
   - Practical question to ask: Why are the listed special controls sufficient for TA-GVHD devices, and why were other possible controls, such as tighter dose-homogeneity requirements, not needed?

4. Issue: Small-entity burden is acknowledged but not fully tied to mitigation choices
   - Why it matters: FDA says the proposal would significantly affect a substantial number of small entities. That makes it important to show how the agency balanced burden against safety gains and why the burden-reducing alternatives were not adopted.
   - Practical question to ask: How did FDA weigh the acknowledged small-entity burden against the rejected alternatives, and what implementation changes could reduce burden without sacrificing public-health protections?

5. Issue: Downstream availability effects are still uncertain
   - Why it matters: The PRIA mostly counts industry and FDA costs, but device availability, hospital workflow, and patient access could still change if firms drop the metastasis indication or delay new submissions.
   - Practical question to ask: What analysis has FDA done on likely effects on device availability for hospitals and transfusion settings if sponsors stop pursuing or maintaining the metastasis indication?

## Evidence Quotes
- “FDA is proposing to classify blood irradiator devices intended to prevent TA-GVHD... into class II (special controls).”
- “FDA agrees with the 2023 Panel that insufficient information exists to determine that general and special controls are sufficient... for blood irradiators intended to prevent metastasis...”
- “The proposed special controls include performance testing; labeling information... a warning that elevated levels of potassium have been reported in irradiated blood; software verification, validation, and hazard analysis; and electrical and electromagnetic compatibility testing.”
- “Based on the literature and device information that we have gathered, it does not appear that this technique... to prevent metastasis is widely used.”
- “Because net annualized compliance costs of the proposed rule are more than 1 percent of average annual revenues and unquantified effects are uncertain, we find that the proposed rule will have a significant economic impact on a substantial number of small entities.”
- “Specifically... regulating blood irradiators intended to prevent metastasis as class II devices... or delaying the implementation of the final rule by one year would reduce the burden on small entities.”
