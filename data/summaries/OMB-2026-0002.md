## What This Document Does
- Proposes a package of changes to the Cost Accounting Standards program for federal contracts.
- Would raise the regulatory threshold for full CAS coverage from $50 million to $100 million.
- Would raise the threshold for disclosure statements from $50 million to $100 million and remove an older carveout that applies when CAS-covered awards are under $10 million and under 30 percent of segment sales.
- Would implement a statutory change that raises the basic CAS applicability threshold to $35 million and eliminates the old $7.5 million trigger-contract concept.
- Would raise executive-agency-head waiver authority from $15 million to $100 million.
- Would clarify how CAS exemptions apply to indefinite delivery contracts: single-award vehicles would be judged at the contract level using the IDC's base-and-option value, while multiple-award vehicles would be judged order by order.

## Why It Matters
- This is a deregulatory proposal aimed at reducing compliance burdens and lowering barriers to entry for contractors, especially mid-size firms and firms that no longer qualify as small businesses.
- The legal footing for some changes is strong because Congress already acted. The move to a $35 million basic threshold comes from the 2026 NDAA, and the higher waiver threshold implements a change made in the 2017 NDAA.
- The more important public-comment questions are about the Board's discretionary choices. The notice says that $100 million would keep nearly all currently covered dollars while removing about 30 percent of affected entities, but the visible record in this run does not include the underlying FPDS tables or methodology.
- The proposal also matters because it changes how much accounting visibility the government keeps over mid-size contracts. If the threshold is too high, some firms may fall out of full coverage or disclosure-statement requirements even when the underlying contracts are still economically significant.
- The indefinite-delivery-contract section is another leverage point. The Board says multiple-award IDCs should be evaluated order by order, but single-award IDCs should use the contract ceiling. The supplied materials do not show enough detail to fully test that proxy.

## Who Is Affected
- Federal contractors and subcontractors subject to CAS, especially mid-size firms.
- Segments that currently file disclosure statements or would stop doing so under the higher threshold.
- Contracting agencies that use waiver authority or rely on single-award and multiple-award indefinite delivery contracts.
- Audit, pricing, compliance, and legal teams that have to determine when full CAS, modified CAS, or exemptions apply.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: The notice relies on summarized FPDS analysis without showing the underlying tables.
   - Why it matters: The Board's strongest quantitative justification is that $100 million keeps nearly 99 percent of covered dollars while removing nearly 30 percent of affected entities. That is a major claim, but the supplied record does not show the supporting calculations.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Please publish the FPDS tables, assumptions, and methodology used to estimate the effect of different threshold levels.

2. Issue: The Board assumes that a `UEI` represents a covered segment.
   - Why it matters: That assumption drives the entity counts used to justify the threshold increase and disclosure-statement changes. If the proxy is weak, the burden-reduction case may be overstated or distorted.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence supports using `UEI` as a segment proxy, and how were cases handled where a segment does not have its own `UEI`?

3. Issue: The choice of $100 million is only partly explained.
   - Why it matters: The notice says inflation from 2000 would place the threshold around $92 million, but it does not visibly compare $100 million against other plausible options.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Why is $100 million preferable to a lower or inflation-nearer threshold, and what would the burden and oversight tradeoffs look like at those alternatives?

4. Issue: The single-award-IDC rule may over-rely on ceiling value.
   - Why it matters: If ceilings are often set high but actual orders stay well below them, CAS could apply based on projected capacity instead of real ordering behavior.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Please provide the distribution of ceiling-to-order realization for single-award IDCs and explain why a hybrid or rebuttable rule was rejected.

5. Issue: The deregulatory framing is only lightly quantified.
   - Why it matters: The Board says the rule will reduce compliance costs and barriers to entry, but the supplied materials do not show quantified savings, transition costs, or oversight losses from reduced disclosure-statement coverage.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What compliance savings does the Board expect from each major change, and what offsetting costs or oversight tradeoffs did it consider?
