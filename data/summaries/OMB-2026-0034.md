## What This Document Does

- Proposes broad revisions to the government-wide rules for Federal financial assistance in 2 CFR.
- Aims to strengthen transparency, accountability, payment integrity, subrecipient oversight, program integrity, and alignment with law and Executive Branch policy.
- Would clarify the regulatory status of OMB's government-wide financial assistance requirements and make conforming changes for many grantmaking agencies.
- Includes major recipient-facing changes: eliminating fixed amount awards and subawards, expanding E-Verify duties to recipients and subrecipients, requiring payment justifications and pre-payment checks, tightening subaward reporting, clarifying termination and suspension authority, and adding new national-policy and foreign-collaboration restrictions.
- Provides a Regulatory Impact Analysis and an Initial Regulatory Flexibility Analysis. The available record includes both documents.

## Why It Matters

- This is not a narrow program rule. It could affect Federal agencies, States, local governments, Tribal entities, universities, nonprofits, research institutions, pass-through entities, subrecipients, and contractors working under Federal awards.
- OMB's oversight goals are clear, and the docket has a real analytical spine. The strongest public-comment issues are practical: cost, timing, scope, exceptions, and operational clarity.
- The proposed effective date is October 1, 2026. That may help fiscal-year uniformity, but it gives agencies and recipients a short runway for policy updates, systems changes, training, subaward clauses, and payment-control changes.
- The record often describes administrative costs as minimal, modest, or moderate. Commenters with real compliance experience can make the final rule stronger by supplying concrete estimates.

## Who Is Affected

- Federal agencies that issue or administer grants and cooperative agreements.
- Recipients and subrecipients, including nonprofits, universities, States, local governments, territories, and some research entities.
- Pass-through entities that manage subawards or related-organization funding flows.
- Small nonprofits and small governmental jurisdictions with limited administrative staff.
- Employees and contractors performing work in the United States under Federal awards, if the E-Verify provision is finalized.
- Research and international-program recipients affected by foreign-collaboration restrictions.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. Elimination of fixed amount awards and subawards
   Why it matters: OMB says these award types can reduce financial visibility, but they can also lower burden for predictable, low-risk work.
   Practical question: Should OMB allow risk-based fixed amount awards with enhanced closeout or milestone reporting instead of eliminating them categorically?

2. E-Verify scope and implementation
   Why it matters: The proposal would apply E-Verify duties to recipients and subrecipients, but mixed-funded staffing and contractor arrangements can be complicated.
   Practical question: How should OMB define employees and contractors "performing work" under a Federal award, and should small entities receive phased implementation?

3. Payment checks and payment justifications
   Why it matters: Pre-payment verification can reduce improper payments, but poor data matching can delay legitimate disbursements.
   Practical question: What safe harbors, correction procedures, and minimum data standards should apply to State pre-payment checks and payment justifications?

4. Termination and suspension standards
   Why it matters: Broad discretionary termination language may affect multi-year project planning, hiring, subawards, and recipient reliance interests.
   Practical question: Should OMB require written reasons, cure opportunities, transition funding, closeout rules, or review procedures before termination or suspension?

5. Small-entity and cumulative burden analysis
   Why it matters: The RFA analysis recognizes administrative costs but concludes they are not significant. Small recipients may experience cumulative burdens from several provisions at once.
   Practical question: What staff-hour, system, training, or legal-review costs should OMB account for before finalizing the rule?

6. October 1, 2026 effective date
   Why it matters: A single fiscal-year effective date may be orderly for agencies, but recipients need time to update policies, systems, contracts, and subaward terms.
   Practical question: Should OMB phase in the most operationally demanding requirements by entity size, award type, or new-award cycle?
