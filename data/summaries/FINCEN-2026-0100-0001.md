## What This Document Does

- This proposal would make permitted payment stablecoin issuers a defined Bank Secrecy Act-regulated financial institution category and layer in both FinCEN AML/CFT duties and OFAC sanctions-program duties.
- FinCEN would require PPSIs to maintain a risk-based AML/CFT program, conduct customer due diligence, collect beneficial-ownership information for legal-entity customers, file required reports, keep records, perform independent testing, train staff, and designate a U.S.-based responsible officer.
- OFAC would require a sanctions compliance program built around five minimum elements: senior-management commitment, risk assessment, internal controls, testing/auditing, and training.
- The proposal distinguishes between “primary market” activity, where the issuer directly interacts with a customer, and “secondary market” activity, where the issuer is not directly a party except through smart-contract infrastructure.
- Treasury also proposes technical-capability obligations tied to blocking, freezing, rejecting, and complying with lawful orders.

## Why It Matters

- This is a foundational federal compliance rule for the post-GENIUS Act stablecoin market.
- The proposal matters not just because it imposes obligations, but because it chooses where not to impose them. Most importantly, FinCEN preliminarily declines to require PPSIs to file SARs on secondary-market transactions, saying the burden may outweigh the benefit.
- The agencies are also trying to balance two themes that can pull in opposite directions: they say the framework should be tailored to issuer size and complexity, but they also reject one obvious small-issuer accommodation, arguing that lighter AML/CFT obligations could attract illicit actors and that compliance technology is scalable.
- The record is substantial, with a preamble, regulatory impact analysis, flexibility analysis, and policy alternatives. But several practical judgments still rest on summarized assumptions rather than clearly visible supporting studies in the packet.

## Who Is Affected

- Permitted payment stablecoin issuers are the direct targets of the rule.
- Existing large stablecoin issuers appear especially affected because Treasury's own analysis suggests many likely future PPSIs are current market participants with at least some onboarding and AML-related infrastructure already in place.
- Smaller or newer issuers may be affected disproportionately if the agencies are underestimating the cost of building lawful-order tooling, sanctions controls, and auditable compliance functions from a relatively thin baseline.
- Direct customers in the “primary market,” including legal-entity customers, would be affected through identification, due-diligence, and information-collection requirements.
- Law-enforcement and national-security agencies are also part of the intended beneficiary set. Treasury grounds the rule in concerns about money laundering, sanctions evasion, terrorism finance, and fraud.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

- **Issue:** The primary-market versus secondary-market line does a lot of work, but the NPRM gives only a limited operational explanation of where that line sits in edge cases.
  **Why it matters:** Issuer wallet controls, freezing, burning, and reissuance can blur the line between direct and indirect involvement.
  **Question a commenter can ask:** What examples or factors will Treasury use to determine when a PPSI is sufficiently involved in an activity for direct AML/CFT or sanctions obligations to attach?

- **Issue:** Treasury preliminarily excludes issuer SAR duties for secondary-market activity without showing much visible quantitative support for the burden-benefit balance.
  **Why it matters:** The proposal treats stablecoin misuse as serious and growing, but one of the broadest potential reporting duties is withheld on a preliminary assumption.
  **Question a commenter can ask:** Should Treasury adopt a narrower trigger for secondary-market SAR duties when a PPSI has actual knowledge or technical control over relevant wallets or transactions?

- **Issue:** The agencies repeatedly say the rule is tailored to issuer size and complexity, but FinCEN rejects a more explicit small-issuer accommodation benchmark.
  **Why it matters:** “Tailored” is one of the proposal's central framing choices. If the final rule is effectively one-size-fits-most, commenters can ask Treasury to justify that more clearly.
  **Question a commenter can ask:** What evidence supports the claim that AML/CFT technology and related controls are scalable enough that smaller PPSIs do not need more explicit burden accommodations or phased implementation?

- **Issue:** The proposal keeps major technical-control requirements flexible, especially around blocking, freezing, rejecting, and lawful-order capability.
  **Why it matters:** Flexibility can help innovation, but too much abstraction can create uneven examination and uncertain compliance expectations.
  **Question a commenter can ask:** Can Treasury provide examples, safe harbors, or implementation factors showing what counts as adequate technological capability for different PPSI models?
