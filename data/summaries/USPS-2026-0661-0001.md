## What This Document Does
- USPS proposes to rewrite its firearm-mailing standards in Publication 52 so they align with a January 15, 2026 Office of Legal Counsel opinion about 18 U.S.C. 1715, the federal statute restricting mailing of concealable firearms.
- The biggest practical change is that lawful handguns would become mailable under roughly the same general framework now used for lawful rifles and shotguns.
- The proposal would define “Mailable Firearms” broadly to include lawful pistols, revolvers, shotguns, and rifles. It would define “Nonmailable Firearms” to include firearms that are generally unlawful to possess, such as certain National Firearms Act items and certain undetectable firearms.
- USPS would still require firearms to be unloaded, securely packaged, and not externally marked to show their contents.
- Most mailable firearms would need to be sent using a USPS product or extra service that provides tracking and signature capture at delivery.
- The proposal also keeps specialized rules for some otherwise nonmailable handguns, government shipments, curios and relics, museums, air guns, and indemnity claims.

## Why It Matters
- This is a more significant operational change than the short preamble suggests. USPS is not only conforming to OLC’s constitutional view. It is also deciding how ordinary handgun shipments should work in the mailstream, what safeguards are enough, and how much compliance responsibility should fall on mailers.
- The available record is thin. The docket metadata for this document shows no supporting studies or technical attachments. The notice cites the OLC memorandum, but it does not provide visible analysis of expected shipment volume, theft or diversion risk, employee-handling implications, or how much added workload retail acceptance staff may face.
- That does not automatically make the proposal unlawful. But it does create useful comment opportunities. USPS itself says comments on “clarity and understandability for users of the mail” are welcome, and the proposal leaves several practical questions unanswered.
- A major concern is that USPS appears to assume lawful handguns can be mailed under nearly the same framework as rifles and shotguns without explaining why the risks are comparable. Handguns may raise different concealability, theft, and enforcement issues.
- Another concern is clarity. The rule tells mailers they must comply with federal, state, and local law, but it offers little practical guidance about how ordinary non-FFL mailers are supposed to determine legality in real transactions.
- There is also at least one concrete drafting issue in the proposed text. Section 432.3(g) refers readers to `431.1.f` for air guns, but `431.1.f` is the “Curio or Relic” definition. The actual air-gun definition appears in `431.1.g`. That kind of error is exactly the sort of thing commenters should flag before finalization.

## Who Is Affected
- Non-FFL firearm owners who may want to mail lawful firearms under the new rules.
- Federal firearms licensees, including dealers, manufacturers, importers, gunsmiths, and curio-and-relic collectors.
- USPS retail clerks, acceptance personnel, inspectors, and claims staff who may have to apply the revised standards.
- State and local actors indirectly affected by the way the proposal pushes legality determinations onto mailers.
- Any communities concerned with package theft, diversion, or the practical handling of newly mailable handguns.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: The notice gives very little operational analysis for a major expansion in ordinary handgun mailability.
   - Why it matters: USPS is changing more than legal wording. It is changing how concealable firearms may move through the mailstream, but the public record does not show much discussion of likely volume, theft risk, or handling burden.
   - Practical question: What evidence did USPS consider on expected handgun shipment volume, mail theft or diversion risk, and employee-handling impacts before concluding the new framework is workable?

2. Issue: USPS does not explain why tracking and signature capture are the right default safeguards.
   - Why it matters: Those controls may help, but the notice does not explain why they are sufficient or whether stronger or narrower alternatives were considered.
   - Practical question: Why did USPS choose tracking and signature capture as the principal safeguards, and did it consider adult-signature, retail-only acceptance, stronger shipper attestations, or phased implementation?

3. Issue: The proposal may be hard for ordinary mailers to apply.
   - Why it matters: The text repeatedly points to federal, state, and local law, but it gives little plain-language guidance for non-FFL users trying to decide whether a shipment is lawful and mailable.
   - Practical question: Will USPS provide examples or clearer instructions showing how non-FFL mailers should determine legality under the proposed framework?

4. Issue: USPS may be framing the scope of comments too narrowly.
   - Why it matters: Even if commenters do not challenge OLC’s constitutional reasoning, they can still ask USPS to justify how it chose to implement that reasoning in postal operations.
   - Practical question: Will USPS clarify that comments on the operational line-drawing choices in the proposed text remain in scope, even if commenters are not disputing OLC’s legal conclusion?

5. Issue: The proposed text includes at least one apparent drafting error and some structural ambiguity.
   - Why it matters: A wrong cross-reference or unclear relationship between general handgun mailability and special handgun exceptions can create confusion for clerks and mailers.
   - Practical question: Will USPS correct the apparent air-gun cross-reference error in `432.3(g)` and better explain how `431.3` interacts with the retained special rules in `432.2` through `432.25`?

## Evidence Quotes
- “The proposed revisions expand the scope of mailable firearms compared to the existing regulations by allowing lawful handguns to be mailed under the same terms and conditions as lawful rifles and shotguns.”
- “Lawful firearms, including pistols, revolvers, shotguns, and rifles, are mailable (‘Mailable Firearms’).”
- “Mailers must comply with the Gun Control Act of 1968, all provisions of postal law, and all other federal and state regulations and local ordinances affecting the movement of firearms.”
- “The Postal Service may require the mailer to open packages containing Mailable Firearms or give written certification that the weapon is unloaded.”
- “All Mailable Firearms must be mailed using a USPS product or Extra Service that provides tracking and signature capture at delivery...”
- “Comments regarding the merits of OLC's opinion, including the scope of firearms that are mailable and nonmailable under the Second Amendment, are therefore outside the scope of this rulemaking.”
