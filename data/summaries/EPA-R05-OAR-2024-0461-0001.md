## What This Document Does

- EPA proposes to approve Ohio's lead-attainment plan for the Canton nonattainment area, determine the area is now attaining the 2008 lead standard, approve Ohio's maintenance plan, approve the area's lead emissions inventory, and redesignate the area from nonattainment to attainment.
- The proposal centers on one main fact pattern: Republic Steel's Canton plant was the dominant historic lead source, the plant shut down permanently, the air permits were terminated, and monitored lead levels fell sharply afterward.
- EPA says the area's `2019-2021` design value was `0.40 μg/m3`, above the `0.15 μg/m3` standard, but the `2022-2024` design value now meets the standard.
- EPA also says Ohio's maintenance plan will keep the area in compliance through `2037` by relying on continued monitoring, future emissions-inventory comparisons, NSR rules for any new or modified source, and contingency measures tied to ambient trigger levels.

## Why It Matters

- A redesignation from nonattainment to attainment changes how the area is treated under the Clean Air Act and signals that EPA believes the original lead problem has been solved in a durable way.
- The proposal matters because the core compliance story depends less on new control technology than on permanent shutdown of the major source. That is plausible, but it makes the long-term maintenance explanation especially important.
- EPA's basic theory is straightforward: emissions dropped from `4.4224` tons per year in `2020` to `0.1508` tons per year in `2023`, measured concentrations dropped with them, and no comparable source can reopen or start operating without going through NSR requirements.
- The strongest public-comment opportunity is to ask whether EPA has explained the future-risk side of the story well enough, especially for demolition, cleanup, remediation, or redevelopment activity at the former Republic Steel site.
- EPA relies on contingency measures that trigger if monitoring shows lead concentrations of `0.75 μg/m3` on a daily average or `0.10 μg/m3` on a one-month average, but the explanation of why those trigger levels are sufficient is brief.

## Who Is Affected

- Residents near the former Republic Steel Canton plant and the broader Canton nonattainment area.
- Any future owner or operator of the Republic Steel site, because the contingency orders and dust-control obligations can carry forward to subsequent owners.
- Ohio EPA and local air officials, who will keep running the monitoring network and administering the maintenance plan.
- Businesses that might seek to build, reopen, modify, or reconstruct a lead-emitting source in the area, because EPA points to NSR as part of the continuing-protection framework.
- Community groups and local governments focused on cleanup risk and neighborhood protection.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. EPA should explain the `2037` maintenance-case assumptions more clearly.
Why it matters: The proposal says future emissions will stay at or below the `2024` attainment inventory and that modeling shows no violation through `2037`, but the public-facing description of those assumptions is thin.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What specific future source-mix, site-activity, and emissions assumptions were used to conclude the area will remain in attainment through `2037`?

2. EPA should justify the contingency trigger levels and response timing more concretely.
Why it matters: The plan uses specific ambient triggers and a `14`-day response framework, but the record does not say much about why those numbers are the right ones for cleanup or demolition-related lead risk.
Practical question a commenter can ask: Why are the `0.75 μg/m3` daily and `0.10 μg/m3` one-month triggers protective enough for likely future site conditions?

3. EPA should explain why no stronger demolition- or remediation-specific safeguards were needed.
Why it matters: The proposal recognizes future cleanup and deconstruction scenarios, but it does not do much public-facing analysis of whether those scenarios deserve more explicit federally described controls.
Practical question a commenter can ask: Did EPA consider requiring a more detailed demolition or remediation protocol in the approved maintenance plan, and if not, why not?

4. EPA should make the future inventory-verification system easier to evaluate.
Why it matters: Ohio commits to compare future lead inventories with `2020`, `2024`, and `2037` benchmarks, but the proposal is brief on how fast that system would reveal new risk.
Practical question a commenter can ask: How frequently will those inventory comparisons occur, and what happens if they show a trend inconsistent with continued attainment?

## Evidence Quotes

- "By the end of 2024, ambient air monitoring demonstrated that airborne Pb levels had declined to `0.00 μg/m 3` and that the three-year design value for 2022-2024 ... had achieved `0.15 μg/m 3`."
- "The EPA notes that Ohio fully demonstrated that these improvements in ambient air quality are due to permanent and enforceable reductions in emissions resulting from the permanent shutdown of all emission units requiring air permits and the termination of the associated air permits."
- "Ohio determined, and the EPA agrees, that no RACM exists that would appreciably reduce fugitive dust emissions ... or expedite attainment."
- "Ohio effectively showed that future emissions would not exceed the level of the `2024` attainment inventory and that modeling of its control strategy demonstrated the future mix of sources will not cause a violation ... through `2037`."
- "These contingency measures require Republic Steel or any subsequent owner or operator ... to take certain actions if the ... monitoring sites reach Pb daily average concentrations of `0.75 μg/m 3` or `1-month` average concentrations of `0.10 μg/m 3`."
