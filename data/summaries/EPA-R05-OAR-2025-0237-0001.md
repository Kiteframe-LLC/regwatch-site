## What This Document Does

- EPA proposes to approve Ohio's source-specific reasonably available control technology determinations for three facilities in the Cleveland ozone nonattainment area: Lubrizol, Henkel, and Cleveland-Cliffs Cleveland Works.
- The proposal covers both volatile organic compounds (`VOC`) and nitrogen oxides (`NOx`) for major non-CTG sources under the 2015 ozone standard.
- EPA says that, if finalized, this package would complete the Cleveland area's remaining **moderate** RACT obligations for the 2015 ozone standard, even though the area was later reclassified to `Serious`.
- For Lubrizol, EPA proposes to approve continued use of existing high-efficiency thermal oxidizers, submerged filling practices, and loading-rack work practices and permit terms.
- For Henkel, EPA proposes to treat existing condenser-based controls and low-VOC operating practices as RACT after rejecting stronger add-on controls as technically or economically unreasonable.
- For Cleveland-Cliffs VOC sources, EPA proposes work-practice controls, BAT limits, and oil-content limits instead of new add-on controls.
- For Cleveland-Cliffs NOx sources, EPA proposes a mix of existing limits, presumptive boiler limits, and a notable trigger: each reheat furnace would have to perform a new RACT study if emissions exceed `348.3` tons on a rolling 12-month basis.

## Why It Matters

- This is a classic example of how EPA and a state use source-specific studies to decide what counts as “reasonably available” control for older industrial facilities.
- The proposal matters because EPA is not just approving fixed emission limits. In several places it is approving permit terms, work practices, equipment specifications, and one future re-study trigger as the RACT outcome.
- That makes the notice important for two reasons:
- First, it affects real ozone precursor emissions at chemical manufacturing and steelmaking facilities in northeast Ohio.
- Second, it shows how far EPA is willing to go in treating existing controls and flexible compliance mechanisms as enough for SIP-approved RACT.
- The docket is not thin. The record includes supporting RACT studies with cost tables, technology screening, and public-notice history. But the NPRM itself is shorter than the underlying technical record, so some of the most important judgment calls are described only briefly.
- The biggest example is Cleveland-Cliffs' reheat furnaces. The underlying study says replacing burners can look cost-effective on a potential-to-emit basis, but not on an actual-emissions basis. EPA proposes to resolve that tension with a rolling trigger rather than requiring a tighter limit now.
- That may be defensible, but it is the kind of decision that deserves a clearer explanation in the final record.

## Who Is Affected

- The three named facilities and their operators, especially the units at Cleveland-Cliffs that rely on existing limits, work practices, or the reheat-furnace trigger.
- Ohio EPA and local air agencies that will administer and enforce the permit conditions EPA proposes to approve into the SIP.
- Residents and local groups in the Cleveland area who care about ozone precursor emissions and whether source-specific controls are genuinely enforceable.
- Other regulated facilities and practitioners who watch this kind of docket for precedent on how EPA evaluates cost-effectiveness and operational flexibility in RACT determinations.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. EPA should explain why the Cleveland-Cliffs reheat-furnace trigger counts as present RACT.
Why it matters: The notice says burner replacement looks cost-effective on one baseline and not on another. EPA then approves a `348.3`-ton rolling trigger that requires a new study only if exceeded. That is a significant policy choice, not just a technical footnote.
Practical question a commenter can ask: Why does EPA believe a future re-study trigger satisfies moderate-area RACT now, instead of requiring a presently enforceable numerical NOx limit for each reheat furnace?

2. EPA should describe its cost-effectiveness benchmark more clearly.
Why it matters: The docket cites several dollar-per-ton figures, including about `$29,000` to `$44,000` per ton for Henkel VOC controls and over `$30,000` per ton for some Lubrizol control options, but the notice does not say what framework EPA is using to decide those costs are too high for RACT.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What principles or comparison points did EPA use to conclude that the cited VOC and NOx control costs are not reasonably available for these sources?

3. EPA should identify the exact permit terms it is SIP-approving for Cleveland-Cliffs VOC sources.
Why it matters: The proposal relies heavily on “manufacturer's or permittee's specifications,” good engineering practices, BAT limits, and oil-content limits. Those can be meaningful, but only if the incorporated terms are specific enough to monitor and enforce.
Practical question a commenter can ask: Which exact permit provisions are being incorporated by reference for Cleveland-Cliffs VOC RACT, and how will regulators verify compliance with those provisions in practice?

4. EPA should explain more directly why this package fully completes the area's moderate RACT obligations.
Why it matters: EPA says finalizing this notice would fully satisfy the Cleveland area's moderate RACT requirements. That may be right, but the notice does not clearly walk readers through the remaining source universe and why these are the last relevant determinations.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What facilities or source categories remained outstanding before this action, and how does EPA know this package closes the moderate-area RACT record?
