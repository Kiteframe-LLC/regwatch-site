## What This Document Does
- This document is not an FTC proposed rule. It is a short FTC notice saying the agency received a petition from Andrew Gonzalez and is inviting public comments on whether the petition merits further action.
- The attached petition asks the FTC to require dealers using indirect auto financing to disclose three things before the consumer signs:
  - the lender's approved "buy rate,"
  - the consumer-facing "sell rate," and
  - the dealer reserve or profit tied to the markup between those rates.
- The FTC notice makes an important procedural point: publishing the petition does not mean the Commission agrees with it. The agency says it will consider the petition's merits only after comments close and may grant it, deny it, or decide it is too weak to justify a rulemaking.
- That means the real question at this stage is not whether a disclosure rule is already justified. The question is whether the petition identifies a real enough problem, a workable enough solution, and enough supporting evidence for the FTC to move toward a fuller rulemaking process.

## Why It Matters
- Indirect auto financing is common, and even modest interest-rate markups can become expensive over the life of a loan. So if dealers are routinely presenting marked-up rates as if they were the best available rate, that could matter to a large number of car buyers.
- The petition is stronger than a generic complaint because it asks for a specific remedy. It does not merely say "make financing fairer." It names a concrete disclosure package and argues that the hidden spread between the buy rate and sell rate creates incentives to raise rates without the consumer knowing it.
- The record is still thin. The FTC notice itself contains almost no substantive analysis. The real support in the available materials comes from the petition attachment, which cites a 2023 working paper and a CFPB-related research reference to argue that discretionary dealer markups are common and costly.
- That thinness matters because the petition's proposed disclosure rule may be harder to implement than it first appears. It assumes the buy rate is always clearly identifiable, the sell rate is always comparable in a simple way, and the dealer reserve can be estimated accurately enough before signing to require disclosure as a dollar amount over the life of the loan.
- It also matters because this is only a threshold petition stage. The FTC is deciding whether the issue deserves more formal attention, not defending a finished rule under a mature administrative record. That creates a good opening for commenters to ask for stronger evidence, clearer definitions, and a better explanation of how any future disclosure rule would work in practice.

## Who Is Affected
- Consumers financing vehicle purchases through dealerships rather than borrowing directly from a bank or credit union.
- Motor vehicle dealers and finance managers who negotiate or arrange indirect auto loans.
- Third-party lenders whose approved rates may be marked up before being presented to the borrower.
- State and federal consumer-protection enforcers if the FTC later tries to define a national disclosure baseline.
- Researchers, consumer advocates, and industry participants who can provide evidence about how dealer markups actually work in practice.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
- **The FTC should require stronger evidence on how common the alleged markup problem really is.**
Why it matters: The petition cites research and makes a serious claim, but the available record does not yet show how widespread the practice is across dealer types, lender types, borrower segments, or loan structures.
Question a commenter can ask: What broader market evidence would the FTC require before deciding that this issue is common enough to justify rulemaking rather than case-by-case enforcement?

- **The proposed dealer-reserve disclosure may be harder to calculate than the petition suggests.**
Why it matters: A life-of-loan reserve estimate may depend on assumptions about payment timing, prepayment, refinancing, and lender-dealer compensation structures.
Question a commenter can ask: How should the FTC handle cases where the dealer's expected reserve is uncertain or changes over the life of the loan?

- **The petition does not fully explain how its proposal fits with existing finance-disclosure rules.**
Why it matters: A new FTC disclosure rule could overlap awkwardly with other lending disclosures unless the agency explains what gap the new rule is filling.
Question a commenter can ask: What consumer-information problem remains unaddressed by current lending disclosures, and how would the FTC's requested disclosures solve that problem specifically?

- **Consumer understanding is not automatic just because more numbers are disclosed.**
Why it matters: Even accurate buy-rate and reserve disclosures may not help if buyers do not understand what those numbers mean or how to use them in a dealership setting.
Question a commenter can ask: What evidence shows that consumers would understand and benefit from buy-rate and reserve disclosures rather than simply receiving more complex paperwork?

- **The FTC should compare narrower alternatives before moving toward a full rulemaking.**
Why it matters: A targeted anti-misrepresentation rule, a simpler spread disclosure, or more focused enforcement guidance may solve part of the problem with less complexity.
Question a commenter can ask: What alternative transparency or enforcement approaches should the FTC study before deciding whether to grant the petition?

## Evidence Quotes
- "The Commission invites written comments concerning the petition."
- "Publication of this petition is pursuant to the Commission's Rules of Practice and Procedure and does not affect the legal status of the petition or its final disposition."
- "The Commission will not consider the petition's merits until after the comment period closes."
- "It may grant or deny the petition in whole or in part, and it may deem the petition insufficient to warrant commencement of a rulemaking proceeding."
- From the petition attachment: "It is an unfair or deceptive act or practice for any Motor Vehicle Dealer ... to fail to clearly and conspicuously disclose" the buy rate, sell rate, and dealer reserve before signing.
