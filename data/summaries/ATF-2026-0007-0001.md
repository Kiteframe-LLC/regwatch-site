## What This Document Does

- ATF proposes to remove the requirement that people filing certain National Firearms Act applications send a copy of those materials to a local chief law enforcement officer, usually called a `CLEO`.
- The change would affect both applications to make an NFA firearm and applications to transfer one, along with the responsible-person form used for trusts and other entities.
- This is not a proposal to eliminate ATF review, federal background checks, or the underlying NFA application process. It is a proposal to remove one local-notice step from that process.
- ATF would amend `27 CFR 479.62` and `27 CFR 479.84` by deleting the current CLEO-notification paragraphs.
- ATF says the notice once made more sense when local officials had a larger practical role, but now ATF handles the legal and background-check review itself.

## Why It Matters

- The rule is narrow, but it still matters because it removes a recurring paperwork step from a high-volume application process.
- ATF says the notice requirement does not meaningfully help criminal investigations or local law enforcement anymore. The notice points to the current federal background-check process and ATF's own state/local law review as the real screening mechanisms.
- ATF also says the post-`2016` notice system has not worked as intended. According to the NPRM, the agency is not aware of CLEOs doing independent background checks or filing objections in response to these notices.
- The proposal's main claimed benefit is cost savings. ATF says the old notice requirement cost applicants about `$5.8 million` annually when the `2016` rule was issued, and it now estimates roughly `$11.4 million` in annual savings from repeal because NFA application volume has grown.
- The most useful comment question is whether ATF has shown enough support for three factual claims:
- that CLEO notice really has little operational value,
- that the savings estimate rests on realistic burden assumptions,
- and that the agency has explained its policy reversal from the `2016` final rule well enough.
- ATF partly acknowledges that by explicitly inviting comments from CLEOs on whether the notices benefit them.

## Who Is Affected

- Individuals applying to make or transfer NFA firearms.
- Responsible persons associated with trusts or other legal entities that file these forms.
- Local law-enforcement offices that currently receive the notice copies.
- Businesses or service providers that help with NFA filing workflows, even if their economic stake is indirect.
- Commenters with direct evidence are especially relevant:
- CLEOs who can explain whether the notices are used or ignored.
- Applicants who can show the real burden of forwarding the forms.
- Analysts or service providers who can test ATF's assumptions about time, cost, and application growth.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. ATF's `no meaningful benefit` conclusion is important, but lightly documented.
- Why it matters: This is the main reason ATF gives for repeal, yet the NPRM does not visibly show much evidence behind it beyond the agency's own statement that it is not aware of useful CLEO activity.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: What data, tracking, outreach, or field experience did ATF review before concluding that CLEO notice no longer serves a significant purpose?

2. The policy reversal from the `2016` final rule deserves a fuller explanation.
- Why it matters: The earlier rule said notice gave CLEOs awareness and a chance to provide input. This NPRM says that rationale has not held up in practice.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: What specifically changed since `2016` that caused ATF to conclude the prior rationale no longer applies?

3. The `$11.4 million` annual savings estimate depends on several assumptions.
- Why it matters: ATF uses projected notification growth, a `15 minute` burden estimate, and a leisure-wage approach. If any of those assumptions are off, the headline number may move a lot.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: Can ATF publish a sensitivity analysis using different burden times, applicant behaviors, and application-growth scenarios?

4. The small-entity and downstream-administration discussion is brief.
- Why it matters: The rule is deregulatory for applicants, but some indirect effects on service providers or local administrative practices may still exist.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: What indirect effects, if any, does ATF expect for businesses or offices that currently help process or handle these notices?

## Evidence Quotes

- “ATF has no information that the CLEO notification meaningfully aids criminal investigations or serves a significant purpose for local law enforcement.”
- “During this time period, ATF is not aware of CLEOs performing independent background checks or filing objections to making or transferring a firearm.”
- “ATF estimates the savings from removing this requirement to be approximately $11.4 million annually.”
- “The CLEO notice is not in the statutory requirements for applying to make or transfer NFA firearms.”
- “This requirement, instead, has been upheld as an implied power based on ATF's ‘broad authority to promulgate regulations governing application forms.’”
- “ATF encourages public comments from CLEOs on whether these notices provide a benefit to them.”
