# Public Summary: ATF-2026-0336-0001

## What This Document Does
- Lets spouses jointly apply to make, transfer or receive, and register National Firearms Act firearms without first creating a trust.
- Revises `27 CFR part 479`, including the application and identity rules for `Form 1`, `Form 4`, `Form 5`, and `Form 23`, so a married couple can file as a joint applicant.
- Requires proof of a legal marriage, such as a certificate, license, or other state-recognized proof, and asks for comment on how common-law marriages should be verified.
- Keeps the existing background-check structure by requiring each spouse to submit individual responsible-person information.
- Explains how the joint-registration approach would interact with divorce, separation, death, and prohibited-person situations.

## Why It Matters
- This is a deregulatory proposal. ATF says many spouses currently use trusts mainly as a workaround so both people can lawfully possess the same NFA firearm. The agency argues that this adds cost, delay, and paperwork without enough public-safety value to justify it.
- ATF estimates the rule could save married couples about **$14.2 million over 10 years** by removing the need to create trusts for joint ownership. The notice uses a proxy for married-couple trust use, online trust-price examples, a notary-cost assumption, and a leisure-time wage estimate to reach that number.
- The main comment opportunities are not broad objections to ATF authority. They are narrower questions about whether the agency has fully justified:
  - why spouses are the only direct joint-registration category,
  - how common-law marriages will be verified consistently,
  - whether the savings estimate is strong enough to support the proposal's confident deregulatory framing.
- ATF also says the current trust route provides “little public safety benefit” for spouses because both spouses would still undergo background checks under the new system. That conclusion is important, but the visible record is more asserted than deeply proven.

## Who Is Affected
- Married couples who want both spouses to have lawful joint possession of NFA firearms.
- Existing individual registrants who may want to transfer a currently registered firearm into joint spousal registration.
- Current and future trust users whose main reason for using a trust is shared spousal possession rather than estate planning or broader multi-person ownership.
- Businesses that sell NFA trust services, including some small niche providers. ATF says the rule may indirectly reduce their revenue, but it does not quantify the effect.
- People in states with common-law marriage or less standardized proof of marriage, because they may face more uncertainty about what documents ATF will accept.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. ATF's savings estimate rests on rough proxies and assumptions.
- Why it matters: The notice's headline benefit claim depends on a chain of estimated inputs, including a `4.4 percent` proxy for married-couple trust applications, online trust-price sampling, and assumed time values. If those assumptions are weak, the `$14.2 million` ten-year savings figure becomes less persuasive.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: Please provide sensitivity analysis showing how the savings estimate changes if the married-couple share, trust price, or trust-preparation time differs from ATF's assumptions.

2. The spouse-only boundary may need a tighter explanation.
- Why it matters: ATF says marriage is different because it is legally documented and tied to shared property rights, while broader non-marital joint registration could invite pretextual ownership arrangements. That may be partly true, but the notice does not deeply compare other documented co-ownership structures.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence shows that other legally documented co-ownership arrangements present materially greater circumvention risk than marriage-based joint registration?

3. Common-law marriage verification is still underdeveloped.
- Why it matters: ATF expressly asks how common-law marriages can be “reliably verified.” If that standard stays vague, similarly situated applicants may face inconsistent treatment or longer review times.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: What exact documents or proof categories will ATF accept for common-law or otherwise non-certificate marriages, and how will examiners handle borderline cases?

4. The “little public safety benefit” conclusion could use more support.
- Why it matters: The proposal assumes direct spousal joint registration is as safe as the current trust route because both spouses still undergo background checks. That may be right, but the notice does not show much evidence about whether trust review currently catches issues that this shortcut would miss.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: Please explain what public-safety function current trust review serves for spousal applications and why removing that layer does not materially change risk.

5. Transition burdens for existing registrants may be understated.
- Why it matters: ATF says there are no additional direct costs because the same forms already exist, but people who voluntarily move existing individually registered firearms into joint registration still face real transfer steps, waiting time, and paperwork effort.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: Should ATF separately estimate one-time transition burden for existing registrants who choose to convert current registrations into joint spousal registrations?

## Evidence Quotes
- “ATF has determined that it is unnecessarily burdensome for spouses who desire to jointly register an NFA firearm to have to create a trust to do so.”
- “Permitting spouses to jointly register NFA firearms would relieve applicants and ATF of unnecessary burdens caused by the current roundabout process, which ATF has determined provides little public safety benefit.”
- “However, ATF also believes that it is reasonable to limit joint individual registration to spouses.”
- “ATF would therefore propose accepting common-law marriages for joint registration that can be reliably verified...”
- “ATF estimates that this proposed rule could save married couples who wish to jointly register $14.2 million over 10 years...”
