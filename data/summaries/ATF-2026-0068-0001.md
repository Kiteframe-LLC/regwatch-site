## What This Document Does

- ATF proposes to change how certain National Firearms Act firearms can be transported across state lines.
- For domestic short-term trips of `365` days or less, the proposal would stop requiring both notice to ATF and advance ATF approval.
- For domestic long-term trips of more than `365` days, and for permanent moves, the proposal would still require notice to ATF at least `14` days before travel, but the traveler would no longer have to wait for ATF to approve before moving.
- The proposal would also expressly allow “pass-through” travel across jurisdictions that otherwise prohibit the firearm, as long as the traveler complies with `18 U.S.C. 926A`.
- ATF would keep the current pre-approval process for temporary foreign transport. Permanent export rules would also stay in place.
- The proposed rule text would require the traveler to carry proof that the firearm is registered in the National Firearms Registration and Transfer Record, and would require common or contract carriers to receive a copy of that proof.

## Why It Matters

- This is a deregulatory proposal. ATF says the current Form 20 approval system has become an unnecessary barrier for lawful domestic travel with certain registered firearms.
- The strongest evidence ATF offers is practical and numerical. The notice says that from January `2020` through May `2025`, ATF received about `96,865` domestic transport requests and denied only `516` of them because the destination state's law barred possession of that firearm type.
- ATF also says the system often denies requests for technical paperwork mistakes rather than real safety problems, and that current processing still averages about `3` days for eForms and `9` days for paper forms.
- ATF estimates the proposal would save affected owners about `$122,525` per year in paperwork time, or about `$1.23 million` over ten years. The proposal also says the main benefits are qualitative: fewer delays, less trip-planning uncertainty, and less repeated paperwork.
- The main analytical tension is that ATF wants to reduce burden without fully explaining the legal and practical risks of removing pre-approval. The notice acknowledges that travelers may have to determine for themselves whether a destination is lawful under state, local, or tribal law.
- The proposal changes where the compliance burden sits. Instead of ATF screening domestic legality in advance, more of that work would fall on the individual traveler and, in some situations, carriers.

## Who Is Affected

- Individuals who lawfully possess registered machine guns, short-barreled rifles, short-barreled shotguns, or destructive devices and want to transport them across state lines.
- Owners who make repeated short domestic trips, such as travel to competitions, shows, exhibitions, neighboring residences, or shooting ranges.
- Owners planning longer temporary moves or permanent relocations, because they would still have to notify ATF but would no longer wait for approval.
- Common and contract carriers handling these firearms in interstate transport.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. ATF should justify why `365` days is the right line between “short-term” and “long-term” transport.
Why it matters: That single cutoff controls whether a traveler must notify ATF at all, but the notice gives little explanation for why a full year is the right threshold.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence or historical practice led ATF to choose `365` days instead of a shorter or more conditional threshold?

2. ATF should show more of the data behind its claim that pre-approval adds little safety value.
Why it matters: A low formal denial rate does not necessarily prove the review system has little preventive value. It may also deter bad filings or catch issues before a denial is recorded.
Practical question a commenter can ask: Can ATF provide a fuller breakdown of denials, withdrawals, corrections, and other outcomes so the public can evaluate what pre-approval actually prevents?

3. ATF should explain how travelers are expected to navigate state, local, and tribal law after pre-approval is removed.
Why it matters: The proposal openly says individuals may need to make these judgments themselves, which can create legal risk for less sophisticated travelers.
Practical question a commenter can ask: Would ATF consider publishing a compliance aid or safe-harbor guidance to reduce avoidable mistakes about destination legality?

4. ATF should clarify what happens if authorization is rescinded after a long-term move has already started.
Why it matters: The notice says a traveler may have to stop transporting the firearm mid-trip, but it gives little practical guidance about routing, storage, return travel, or post-arrival handling.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What operational steps would ATF expect a traveler to take if ATF rescinds authorization after travel begins or after the traveler reaches the destination?

## Evidence Quotes

- “Only `516 (0.5 percent)` of these Form 20 applications were denied on the basis that the destination state's law prohibits a person from possessing that kind of firearm in that state.”
- “Of the `10,532` Forms 20 that were denied from January `2020` to May `2025`, the most common reason ATF denied them was for a technical error.”
- “The Director authorizes such short-term transportation without notifying ATF.”
- “The individual must notify ATF ... fourteen days prior to the move and is authorized to initiate the move `14` days thereafter.”
- “If ATF rescinds authorization after travel has begun, the person would have to stop transporting the firearm.”
