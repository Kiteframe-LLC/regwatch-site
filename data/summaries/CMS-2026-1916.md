## What This Document Does

- CMS proposes to replace the current `average commercial rate` ceiling for many Medicaid `state directed payments` with payment limits tied to Medicare or, if Medicare has no published rate, the Medicaid `State plan` rate.
- For the four services specifically addressed by the 2025 `WFTC` legislation, the proposed cap would be `100 percent` of Medicare in expansion States and `110 percent` in non-expansion States.
- The proposal also reaches beyond those four services by applying similar Medicare-based payment-limit logic to additional managed-care arrangements and to certain `targeted Medicaid practitioner payments` in fee-for-service.
- CMS would also revise which payment arrangements still need written prior approval through the SDP `preprint` process.
- Some questions, especially how to define `provider class`, are not fully resolved in this rule. CMS explicitly asks for comment on those issues and says future rulemaking may follow.

## Why It Matters

- This is a significant Medicaid payment-policy proposal, not a minor cleanup. It would change how States, managed-care plans, hospitals, nursing facilities, AMCs, and practitioners think about supplemental payment ceilings.
- CMS says SDP spending has grown quickly, citing projections of about `$97.8 billion` in FY 2024, `$124.3 billion` in FY 2025, and `$144.6 billion` in FY 2026 absent further change. The agency frames the rule as a fiscal-integrity response to that growth.
- The strongest agency argument is administrative consistency: Medicare rates are public, standardized, and easier to audit than commercial-rate benchmarks.
- The main weakness is that CMS also admits it cannot reliably disaggregate some downstream effects on providers and small entities. That means the rule's practical consequences may be less certain than the fiscal-integrity framing suggests.
- The proposal matters most where Medicaid payments are being used to support provider participation or access. If the new ceilings are too blunt, they may cut arrangements CMS dislikes and arrangements that some States see as access-supporting.

## Who Is Affected

- State Medicaid agencies that design and submit `SDP preprints`.
- Medicaid managed-care organizations, because State-directed payments affect contract pricing and actuarial soundness.
- Hospitals, academic medical centers, nursing facilities, and physicians or other practitioners that receive supplemental or targeted Medicaid payments.
- Non-expansion States in particular, because the proposal preserves a `110 percent` Medicare cap for certain services there while expansion States face `100 percent`.
- Commenters with direct knowledge of provider access problems, Medicaid payment design, safety-net financing, rural delivery, or practitioner reimbursement are especially well positioned to add useful evidence.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

- CMS relies heavily on Medicare as the new benchmark.
Why it matters: Medicare is easier to administer, but it may not reflect Medicaid access needs for every service or provider setting.
Question a commenter can ask: For which services or provider classes is Medicare a poor proxy for the payment level needed to maintain Medicaid participation?

- The rule uses broad spending-growth figures to justify major policy changes.
Why it matters: Aggregate SDP growth does not automatically prove that every high-payment arrangement is wasteful or misaligned with access goals.
Question a commenter can ask: Can CMS separate growth driven by suspected financing distortions from growth tied to legitimate access or workforce support?

- CMS narrows some prior-approval pathways while leaving parts of the oversight design unsettled.
Why it matters: Less ex ante review may make it harder to catch problematic one-provider or narrowly tailored arrangements before they take effect.
Question a commenter can ask: What monitoring, audit, or retrospective review tools will replace the loss of prior approval in higher-risk cases?

- Provider-class issues are still unresolved.
Why it matters: CMS says provider-class design matters, but it is not actually proposing a final definition in this rule.
Question a commenter can ask: What specific provider-class definition would avoid both overbreadth and loopholes, and what evidence supports it?

- The downstream impact analysis is incomplete.
Why it matters: CMS says it cannot reliably attribute or disaggregate some provider and small-entity effects, yet the rule could still shift real money away from hospitals, AMCs, nursing facilities, and practitioners.
Question a commenter can ask: Which provider types, State categories, or service lines face the largest projected reductions, and what does CMS expect that to mean for access?

## Evidence Quotes

- "we estimate that absent any changes to existing regulations and before accounting for the impact of the WFTC legislation, SDP spending is projected to be $97.8 billion in fiscal year (FY) 2024 ... $124.3 billion ... and $144.6 billion for FY 2026."
- "The total payment rate limit ... is 100 percent of the total published Medicare payment rate for an expansion State, or 110 percent ... for a non-expansion State."
- "we interpret section 71116(a)(1) and (2) of the WFTC legislation to require that the exact rate(s) approved under the Medicaid State plan serve as the payment limit in the absence of a total published Medicare payment rate."
- "We solicit comments on these possible approaches to defining 'provider class' ... We emphasize that we are not proposing a definition of the term 'provider class' in this rule."
- "we do not have data that would allow us to reliably attribute or disaggregate these impacts to specific provider types or to small entities"
