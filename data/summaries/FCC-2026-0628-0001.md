## What This Document Does

- The FCC is proposing two targeted updates to its `6 GHz` unlicensed-device rules.
- First, it would let automated frequency coordination (`AFC`) systems account for up to `6 dB` of building entry loss (`BEL`) when they calculate channel and power availability for composite indoor/standard-power access points. In plain terms, the FCC wants certain indoor devices to get more usable power because walls and building materials can reduce the chance that signals will reach and interfere with licensed users outside.
- Second, it would allow low-power indoor (`LPI`) access points to operate on cruise ships, even though the current rules generally prohibit these devices on boats.
- The FCC says both changes could improve indoor Wi-Fi performance while keeping harmful interference risk low. For the BEL proposal, the FCC relies heavily on prior waiver practice by its Office of Engineering and Technology. For the cruise-ship proposal, it relies on two main ideas: cruise ships are limited in number, and their thick metal structures may significantly attenuate signals.
- The notice also asks broader questions about whether the FCC should update any other `6 GHz` unlicensed rules in light of technological and business developments since `2020`.

## Why It Matters

- This proposal matters because `6 GHz` Wi-Fi is increasingly important for high-capacity indoor connectivity. If the FCC is right, the changes could improve performance in homes, buildings, and dense passenger environments like cruise ships.
- But the most important comment opportunities are not just pro- or anti-Wi-Fi expansion. They are about whether the FCC has shown enough support for the exact thresholds and boundaries it chose.
- The `BEL` proposal depends on several linked assumptions: that AFC systems can reliably identify eligible composite devices, that `6 dB` is an appropriate attenuation value for rulewide use, and that real-world indoor deployments will behave roughly the way the FCC expects.
- The cruise-ship proposal raises a similar issue. The FCC may be right that cruise ships are different from smaller boats, but the available notice text only partly explains why the proposed carveout should stop exactly there.
- The economic discussion is also worth attention. The FCC estimates a one-time `\$4,800` savings from avoiding additional waiver work and an annual `\$35.6 million` benefit from improved connectivity on cruise ships. At the same time, it says the proposal would not impose substantial costs because many implementation choices would be voluntary. That framing may undercount real costs that operators, equipment makers, or smaller entities would still face in practice.
- This is still an `NPRM`, so some incompleteness is normal. The FCC is explicitly asking for evidence. That means the best public comments are likely to be technical, comparative, and practical rather than rhetorical.

## Who Is Affected

- AFC operators that would need to implement, validate, and defend BEL-based calculations.
- Device manufacturers seeking certification for composite indoor/standard-power access points.
- Licensed incumbent users in the `6 GHz` band, including parties concerned about aggregate interference risk.
- Cruise ship operators and onboard network providers that may install or expand `6 GHz` Wi-Fi systems.
- Smaller entities and suppliers that may face testing, certification, or implementation costs that the notice does not quantify in much detail.
- Passengers and building users who could benefit if the FCC's interference assumptions are correct and the resulting indoor coverage gains are real.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. The FCC should show more of the engineering basis for the `6 dB` BEL threshold.
Why it matters: `6 dB` is central to the proposal, but the public-facing notice relies heavily on prior waiver experience rather than fully surfacing the technical basis in this record.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What data, modeling assumptions, or sensitivity analysis show that `6 dB` is protective across realistic indoor building conditions?

2. The FCC should explain how it will verify that BEL-adjusted AFC treatment is used only for eligible indoor composite devices.
Why it matters: The interference case depends not just on indoor attenuation, but also on whether AFC systems can accurately identify and constrain the right devices.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What audit, enforcement, or misuse-detection mechanisms would confirm that devices benefiting from BEL treatment are actually certified and operating as assumed?

3. The FCC should better justify the cruise-ship-only carveout.
Why it matters: The notice gives reasons for treating cruise ships differently, but the line between cruise ships and other vessels may look arbitrary if the final record does not compare attenuation, passenger density, and interference risk more carefully.
Practical question a commenter can ask: Why is the proposed `33 CFR 101.105` cruise-ship definition a better fit than a more engineering-based standard tied to vessel construction and expected signal attenuation?

4. The FCC should provide a fuller accounting of implementation costs.
Why it matters: The notice quantifies benefits more readily than costs and treats several likely expenses as non-substantial because adoption is voluntary.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What real costs would AFC operators, cruise operators, device makers, and smaller entities face for system changes, testing, installation, and compliance if these rules are adopted?

5. The FCC should commit to stronger post-adoption monitoring.
Why it matters: If the proposal is built on engineering assumptions about attenuation and low interference risk, the final rule should include a way to detect whether those assumptions hold up in practice.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What reporting, field validation, or review triggers would let the FCC revisit the rule if unexpected interference patterns emerge?

## Evidence Quotes

- “This will provide stronger signals and better coverage inside homes and buildings, without increasing the risk of harmful interference to licensed services that share the 6 GHz band.”
- “OET only permitted the AFC systems to incorporate building entry loss up to and including 6 dB in their predictive propagation model calculations...”
- “These devices were previously banned on boats, but the Commission believes the risk of harmful interference occurring from this application is low because cruise ships are few in number and their thick metal walls block signals from escaping.”
- “The Commission proposes limiting this exception to its rules to cruise ships for two reasons.”
- “The Commission tentatively concludes that the proposed rules will yield modest benefits, including a one-time cost savings of $4,800 ... and a recurring annual benefit of $35.6 million.”
- “Therefore, the Commission tentatively concludes that the proposed rules will not incur any substantial costs.”
