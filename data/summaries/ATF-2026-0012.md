## What This Document Does

- ATF proposes to add three definitions to its permanent-import regulations under the Arms Export Control Act: `accessories and attachments`, `component`, and `part`.
- Those definitions are borrowed from the State Department's export-control rules in the International Traffic in Arms Regulations, or `ITAR`.
- The proposal is not just a copy-and-paste exercise. ATF also wants to amend the firearms section of the U.S. Munitions Import List so that the phrase `components and parts for such firearms` does not accidentally sweep in unassembled elements of accessories or attachments.
- In plain terms, ATF says it wants its own import rule text to use the same vocabulary it already relies on in practice, while making sure that borrowing the ITAR definition of `part` does not unintentionally widen firearm-import coverage.
- The operative changes are to `27 CFR 447.11` and `27 CFR 447.21`.

## Why It Matters

- This is a narrow NPRM, but it still matters because definitions often control who needs permits, how products are classified, and how disputes are resolved.
- ATF says its current regulations refer to `components`, `parts`, `accessories`, and `attachments` without defining them, even though ATF already relies on State Department determinations or ITAR-based technical classification when reviewing import applications.
- The agency's main claim is that codifying these definitions will make decisions more consistent and easier for importers to understand.
- The most important complication is the definition of `part`. ATF says that if it adopted the ITAR definition with no adjustment, an odd result would follow in the firearms category: some unassembled elements of accessories or attachments could become regulated even though complete accessories or attachments are not.
- That is why the NPRM adds a firearm-specific clarification. ATF says the clarification preserves the current scope of Category I rather than expanding it.
- The strongest comment question is whether ATF has shown enough evidence for that assurance. The notice explains the logic, but it does not give many concrete before-and-after examples showing how actual import classifications would work under current practice versus the proposed text.
- ATF also says the rule imposes no costs and will not significantly affect small entities. That may be directionally true, but it is still worth asking whether importers, especially smaller firms, will have to review product lines, internal compliance procedures, or prior assumptions once these terms are formally codified.

## Who Is Affected

- Importers of defense articles subject to ATF's permanent-import regulations.
- Firearm importers and related businesses dealing with Category I items on the U.S. Munitions Import List.
- Compliance professionals and trade counsel who advise on whether an item is a `component`, `part`, `accessory`, or `attachment`.
- Small importers with limited in-house legal capacity may be especially affected if they need new guidance to apply the codified definitions confidently.
- Commenters with the most useful evidence would include:
- importers who have seen uncertainty or inconsistency under the current undefined terms,
- firms that can show whether the proposal would change any real classification outcomes,
- and smaller businesses that can speak to transition costs, if any.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. ATF says the rule preserves current firearm-import scope, but the visible record is light on examples.
- Why it matters: The firearm-specific carveout is the most substantive part of the proposal. If it does more than ATF says, commenters should surface that now.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: Can ATF provide concrete examples showing how borderline firearm-related items are classified today and how they would be classified under the proposed text?

2. The `no costs` conclusion is plausible but thinly supported.
- Why it matters: Even a small definitional rule can create transition work if businesses need to review inventory, guidance, or permit assumptions.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: What transition steps, if any, does ATF expect importers to take when these definitions are formally added to the regulations?

3. The proposal assumes industry already uses these definitions consistently.
- Why it matters: That assumption underlies both the small-entity certification and the claim that the rule is mainly clarifying.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence supports ATF's statement that these definitions are already used by industry and ATF in a way that makes the rule effectively costless?

4. The rule could benefit from interpretive examples, not just regulatory text.
- Why it matters: Definitions alone may not resolve uncertainty for mixed-use or borderline items that do not fit cleanly into one label.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: Will ATF provide examples or guidance for items that combine component-like and accessory-like features?

## Evidence Quotes

- “ATF proposes to add the compositional terms defined in 22 CFR 120.40—‘accessories and attachments,’ ‘component,’ and ‘part’—to its AECA regulations so that ATF's regulations include definitions for these terms and the definitions align with those in the ITAR.”
- “Fully applying the ITAR definition of ‘part’ to the USMIL Category I—Firearms would consequently lead to the result that unassembled accessories or attachments ... would be regulated while complete accessories or attachments would not.”
- “Category I—Firearms would thus include components of regulated firearms as well as unassembled elements of a component, but it would exclude assembled or unassembled attachments and accessories.”
- “The changes would also ensure that ATF and the Department of State align in how they use these compositional terms with respect to the USMIL and USML.”
- “It imposes no costs and is also not significant because it incorporates definitions from the ITAR for consistency between agencies without changing importer requirements.”
