## What This Document Does
- Proposes new FSOC interpretive guidance for deciding how to identify and respond to risks from nonbank financial companies and financial activities.
- Would replace the 2023 interpretive guidance and rescind the separate 2023 analytic framework, while leaving the underlying 2012 procedural rule in place.
- Moves the Council back toward a more 2019-style structure by putting an activities-based approach first and treating firm-specific designation as a later step when broader tools are not enough.
- Says FSOC would consider "economic growth" and "economic security" when identifying potential risks to U.S. financial stability.
- Says FSOC would use a cost-benefit test before making a section 113 designation and would use a higher threshold for what counts as a threat to U.S. financial stability.
- Adds a process where FSOC can first ask an existing regulator to respond in writing about how it plans to address a risk.

## Why It Matters
- This is a process rule in practical effect, but it matters because process choices shape when and how FSOC can act against systemic risk.
- The notice would make designation harder to reach in at least three ways: it prioritizes activity-based responses first, it says designation should occur only when expected benefits justify expected costs, and it interprets a qualifying threat as one that could cause "severe damage on the broader U.S. economy."
- FSOC says these changes would improve rigor, transparency, and engagement with firms and regulators. That is a serious claim, but the notice is stronger on announcing the new framework than on demonstrating why the 2023 framework was inadequate in practice.
- The notice also introduces "economic security" as part of the risk-identification picture. That may become important in future cases, but the proposal does not do much yet to define its limits or show how it will be applied.
- The result is a record with a real, commentable tension: the Council wants more disciplined and explainable decisionmaking, but some of the proposal's most important new ideas are themselves only partly explained.

## Who Is Affected
- Large nonbank financial companies that could face future FSOC review or designation.
- Primary financial regulators that may be asked to respond first before FSOC moves toward a company-specific step.
- Market participants, investors, and the public who depend on the Council to identify and address emerging systemic risks before they become crises.
- Firms and advocates trying to comment on future designations, because this guidance affects what evidence and arguments may matter most.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. The new "economic security" concept is broad but not well bounded.
- Why it matters: Broad framing can increase flexibility, but it can also make future decisionmaking harder to predict and harder to test.
- Practical question: What limiting principles, examples, and excluded considerations would FSOC use when deciding whether an "economic security" concern is relevant to financial stability?

2. The notice says cost-benefit analysis will gate designation, but gives limited methodology.
- Why it matters: Compliance costs may be easier to measure than crisis-prevention benefits, so the record should explain how FSOC will compare them fairly.
- Practical question: What categories of benefits and costs will FSOC include, and how will it evaluate uncertain but potentially very large systemic-risk benefits?

3. The higher "severe damage" threshold needs more practical explanation.
- Why it matters: A narrower threshold could improve discipline, but it could also make early preventive action harder unless the Council explains how the standard works in real cases.
- Practical question: How does the proposed "severe damage" interpretation differ in practice from the 2023 framework, and what examples show why the newer standard is preferable?

4. The activities-based-first sequence lacks clear escalation criteria.
- Why it matters: If the public cannot tell when an activities-based process is considered inadequate, the framework may become less predictable even while claiming more rigor.
- Practical question: What evidence or decision triggers would cause FSOC to conclude that activity-based measures cannot adequately address a risk and that entity-specific review is warranted?

5. The regulator-response step is useful in concept but thin on accountability details.
- Why it matters: Asking a primary regulator to respond first may improve coordination, but only if the notice explains timing, sufficiency, and next steps when the response is weak.
- Practical question: What minimum content, deadlines, and follow-up standards would FSOC apply to written regulator responses?

## Evidence Quotes
- "The Proposed Guidance would help ensure that the Council's work is clear, transparent, and analytically rigorous."
- "The Proposed Guidance explains that the Council would consider impediments to economic growth and economic security when identifying potential risks to U.S. financial stability."
- "The Council recognizes that economic growth provides the strongest foundation for financial stability, and that economic security, in turn, supports economic growth."
- "The Council would pursue entity-specific determinations under section 113 of the Dodd-Frank Act only if a potential risk to U.S. financial stability cannot be, or is not, adequately addressed through an activities-based approach."
- "The Council would make a determination under section 113 only if the expected benefits to financial stability from Federal Reserve supervision and prudential standards justify the expected costs that the determination would impose."
- "The Council would modify its interpretation of the term 'threat to the financial stability of the United States' ... to mean ... severe damage on the broader U.S. economy."
