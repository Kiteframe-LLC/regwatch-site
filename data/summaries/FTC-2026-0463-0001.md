## What This Document Does
- The FTC is not proposing final rule text yet. This document is an `ANPRM` asking whether it should write a trade regulation rule about unfair or deceptive fees and pricing practices on online food and grocery delivery platforms.
- The notice focuses on several recurring practices: menu-price markups compared with in-store or restaurant prices, mandatory fees disclosed late in checkout, "free delivery" advertising paired with other required charges, confusing "service fee" labels, and representations about tips or delivery-worker compensation.
- The Commission also points to prior FTC cases against Grubhub, Instacart, Amazon, and Walmart, plus State and local enforcement and pricing-transparency laws, as evidence that these issues are not hypothetical.
- A major theme of the notice is that delivery-platform pricing is often variable. Total price may depend on basket size, distance, timing, subscription status, and other user choices, which makes disclosure design more complicated than in a simple fixed-price transaction.
- The FTC asks whether these practices are common, which ones are most harmful, and whether a Federal rule should require upfront fee disclosures, itemized breakdowns, or other platform-specific transparency standards.

## Why It Matters
- Food and grocery delivery has become a normal part of household purchasing, so even modestly misleading price presentation can affect a very large number of transactions.
- The strongest part of the ANPRM is that it identifies concrete patterns instead of speaking only in general terms about "junk fees." It describes marked-up menu prices, late-stage mandatory fees, and potentially misleading "free delivery" offers as distinct issues.
- It also recognizes a real design problem: some delivery charges are variable, so the agency cannot simply copy a one-line total-price rule without thinking about when exact pricing is realistically knowable.
- The weaker part is that the FTC has not yet shown how it will distinguish between practices that are always deceptive and practices that depend on context, wording, or timing. The ANPRM cites many studies, settlements, and State laws, but the supplied record does not include a market-wide comparison showing which practices are most common or which regulatory models have worked best.
- That matters because a later NPRM could become too broad if it lumps together item-price markups, mandatory platform fees, and optional tips without clear boundaries. It could also become too vague if it tells platforms to disclose fees "upfront" without defining what that means for a transaction where the fee changes with distance, order size, or delivery speed.
- This stage is where commenters can ask the FTC to make those distinctions before it drafts binding regulatory text.

## Who Is Affected
- Consumers ordering meals or groceries through third-party delivery apps and websites.
- Restaurants, grocery stores, and other sellers whose goods appear on those platforms and may be subject to markups or fee-allocation decisions they do not fully control.
- Delivery platforms offering marketplace ordering, grocery fulfillment, subscriptions, or "free delivery" promotions.
- Delivery workers indirectly, especially where tip prompts or fee labels affect how consumers understand worker compensation.
- State and local regulators and courts, because a Federal rule could overlap with or reshape the practical importance of existing State pricing-transparency laws.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
- **The FTC should separate item-price markup issues from hidden-fee issues.**
Why it matters: A consumer seeing a marked-up burger price and a consumer being surprised by a late-added mandatory fee are facing related but different transparency problems.
Question a commenter can ask: Should the FTC regulate item-price markup disclosure and mandatory-fee disclosure through different standards or different parts of a future rule?

- **The agency needs a staged disclosure framework for variable-fee orders.**
Why it matters: Some delivery charges depend on order details, distance, and timing, so the rule should say what must be disclosed at search, cart, and checkout rather than relying on one vague "upfront" instruction.
Question a commenter can ask: What exact information should platforms have to show when the final fee cannot yet be known with precision?

- **The ANPRM does not yet show which platform practices are widespread versus platform-specific.**
Why it matters: A national rule should be based on recurring market conduct, not only on a set of notable cases or settlements.
Question a commenter can ask: Can the FTC publish or summarize complaint and prevalence data showing how common each cited practice is across major platforms?

- **The FTC should explain why a platform-specific rule is needed instead of relying only on general mandatory-fee principles.**
Why it matters: Delivery platforms have special intermediary and variable-pricing features, but the ANPRM has not yet clearly identified which of those features require bespoke Federal treatment.
Question a commenter can ask: Which consumer-protection problems are unique enough to online delivery platforms that they justify special rule text?

- **State-law models need closer comparison before they become the template for a Federal rule.**
Why it matters: The ANPRM cites multiple State statutes and settlements, but those laws differ in scope, definitions, and treatment of variable fees.
Question a commenter can ask: Which State-law approaches does the FTC view as most successful, especially for itemized breakdowns, maximum-fee disclosures, and "free delivery" claims?

## Evidence Quotes
- "The Commission proposes to commence a rulemaking proceeding to address certain unfair or deceptive acts or practices relating to fees and charges for food and grocery items ordered through online delivery platforms."
- "Often, the existence and cost of these fees are not disclosed until checkout, if at all, meaning that consumers frequently do not learn the true cost of getting their food delivered until the very end of the transaction."
- "These hidden fees force consumers to either abandon their order (thus wasting the time they spent preparing it) or pay unanticipated costs that sometimes exceed the amount they were prepared to spend."
- "Many food and grocery delivery platforms currently charge variable fees, and the total price of a transaction typically varies based on a consumer's selections, as well as on other factors such as the size of an order or the distance and time it will take to deliver the order."
- "Despite slight variations across jurisdictions, these approaches highlight that tailored pricing and disclosure requirements may be appropriate for third-party food and grocery delivery platforms given that such platforms typically serve as intermediaries between consumers and the primary seller or maker of the ordered goods."
