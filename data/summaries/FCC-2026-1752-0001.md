## What This Document Does

This FCC notice proposes the agency's annual regulatory fee schedule for fiscal year 2026. The FCC says it must collect `$416,112,000`, equal to its salaries-and-expenses appropriation for the year. The real choices are about how that amount gets spread across regulated sectors.

The FCC proposes to use its usual framework of tying fees to employee work allocation and category-specific payment units. The most important proposed change is a net shift of `59` staff positions into the pool treated as directly related to fee-paying industries. More specifically, the FCC proposes to move `61` indirect FTEs from the Office of Economics and Analytics, the Office of General Counsel, and the Public Safety and Homeland Security Bureau into direct allocations for core licensing bureaus, while moving `2` Media Bureau FTEs back to indirect status.

The notice also asks whether the FCC should keep two notable fee methodologies:

* population covered by a television station's contour for full-service broadcast TV fees
* NRUF assigned-number data for CMRS fee assessments

The document is therefore less about whether fees will be collected and more about whether the FCC is using fair and well-supported ways to divide those fees among broadcasters, carriers, space operators, cable entities, and other payors.

## Why It Matters

This matters because the total collection target is mandatory, but the distribution of burden is not automatic. When the FCC changes which staff time counts as directly tied to fee payors, or keeps one unit proxy instead of another, some sectors pay less and others pay more.

The strongest part of the notice is its legal baseline. The FCC says section `9` requires annual fee collection and that fee methodology should reflect FTE burden adjusted for benefits to payors.

The weaker part of the record is public-facing support for the agency's line-drawing. The FCC says it conducted a “high-level, yet comprehensive” staff analysis to support the FTE reallocations, but the visible record does not show much of the underlying work papers or criteria. The notice also asks whether to keep the current television and CMRS proxies, but the available text does not provide much side-by-side comparison of alternatives.

That creates practical comment leverage. The more useful path is to ask the FCC to show more of its reasoning for who bears the burden and why the chosen proxies still fit current market conditions.

## Who Is Affected

* Broadcasters, especially full-service television stations whose fees still depend on population-based contour calculations.
* CMRS providers if NRUF assigned-number data remains the basis for their fee assessments.
* Carriers and other fee payors in categories affected by the direct-versus-indirect FTE reallocations.
* Small regulated entities that may be above the de minimis threshold but still sensitive to modest fee changes.

Unknown from available text:

* which categories will see the largest fee increases or decreases from the FY 2026 reallocations
* how strongly alternative CMRS or television proxies would change final fee burdens
* how much uncertainty in payment-unit estimates could matter in individual categories

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. The FCC has not shown much public-facing support for the FTE reallocation judgments.
Why it matters: The proposed fee shifts depend on whether work in non-core bureaus really should be treated as directly benefiting fee-paying sectors.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What criteria, examples, or worksheets support the decision to reclassify the `61` identified FTEs as direct for FY 2026?

2. The television fee proxy may need a clearer defense.
Why it matters: Population covered by contour may be administrable, but the final rule is stronger if the FCC explains why that measure still tracks regulatory benefit better than available alternatives.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence shows the population-based contour method remains the best proxy for full-service broadcast television fees in FY 2026?

3. The CMRS fee proxy may deserve a side-by-side alternatives analysis.
Why it matters: Assigned-number data may not always reflect actual market activity or burden as well as other datasets.
Practical question a commenter can ask: How does NRUF compare with other plausible CMRS datasets in accuracy, timeliness, and fit with section `9`'s benefits rationale?

4. The notice is thin on category-specific small-entity impacts.
Why it matters: Generic references to waivers, installment plans, and the de minimis threshold do not show which smaller payors are likely to face heavier burdens from the proposed methodology.
Practical question a commenter can ask: Which small-entity sectors are expected to see meaningful fee increases or decreases under the FY 2026 proposal?

## Evidence Quotes

* “We propose and seek comment on the regulatory fees and methodology to assess and collect $416,112,000 ... as set forth in Tables 3 and 4.”
* “Based upon this analysis, we propose to reallocate a total of 59 FTEs as direct to the Commission's core licensing bureaus.”
* “We therefore propose to reallocate 61 FTEs from the Office of Economics and Analytics, the Office of General Counsel, and the Public Safety and Homeland Security Bureau as direct FTEs to core bureaus...”
* “We also seek comment on our proposal to continue to calculate television broadcaster regulatory fees using the Commission's methodology of population-based full-service broadcast television regulatory fees...”
* “Finally, we seek comment on whether to continue to use Numbering Resource Utilization Forecast (NRUF) assigned number data as the basis for assessing regulatory fees on Commercial Mobile Radio Service (CMRS) providers.”
