## What This Document Does
- Updates Medicare's FY 2027 hospital inpatient payment systems for acute-care hospitals and long-term care hospitals.
- Estimates about `$1.9 billion` in higher FY 2027 IPPS payments and about `$55 million` in higher FY 2027 LTCH PPS payments.
- Adds a wide set of policy changes beyond the annual rate update:
- New and modified hospital quality measures across several CMS programs.
- A new sepsis readmission measure for the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program.
- An anti-discrimination provision for graduate medical education and nursing/allied-health education programs.
- Changes to the mandatory TEAM bundled-payment model.
- A new nationwide mandatory `CJR-X` bundled-payment model for lower-extremity joint replacement episodes beginning October 1, 2027, with limited exclusions.
- Includes several requests for information on future LTCH quality concepts, future TEAM options, emergency-care access, sepsis mortality, birthing-friendly designation design, electronic prior authorization direction, and device-identifier policy.

## Why It Matters
- The routine part of the document is the annual hospital payment update. The more comment-worthy part is that CMS is using the same rule to make a large number of quality-program and payment-model changes at once.
- CMS says the annual payment effects are positive in the aggregate. But aggregate gains do not answer whether particular hospitals, especially rural, safety-net, teaching, low-volume, or formerly MDH hospitals, will experience the rule the same way.
- The quality-program changes matter because they can affect public reporting, reporting burden, and eventually payment consequences. CMS proposes to adopt the Advance Care Planning eCQM across multiple programs, adopt five modified mortality measures in Hospital IQR and later Hospital VBP, add a sepsis readmission measure to HRRP, and change reporting rules in other programs.
- Those changes may be sensible, but they are also the kind of policies where validation, case-mix adjustment, coding sensitivity, and implementation burden matter a great deal. A rule can be directionally reasonable while still needing stronger public explanation for why these exact measures and timelines are the right ones.
- TEAM and `CJR-X` matter because they use payment incentives to change hospital behavior. CMS says the TEAM proposals would not materially change the prior savings estimate, and it says `CJR-X` would save Medicare about `$725 million` over five performance years while preserving or enhancing quality. Those are consequential claims, especially for a nationwide mandatory model.
- The anti-discrimination provision matters for a different reason. CMS says it does not expect approved programs or accrediting bodies to keep engaging in unlawful discrimination as of October 1, 2026. That may be true as a legal baseline, but commenters can still ask whether the new regulatory text gives enough practical clarity for admissions, recruitment, and accreditation decisions.

## Who Is Affected
- Acute-care hospitals paid under IPPS.
- Long-term care hospitals paid under LTCH PPS.
- Teaching hospitals and institutions involved in graduate medical education and nursing/allied-health education programs.
- Hospitals participating in CMS quality-reporting and value-based-payment programs.
- Hospitals that would be drawn into the mandatory `CJR-X` model, especially those with unusual market conditions, patient populations, or episode patterns.
- Commenters focused on rural access, safety-net funding, measurement validity, hospital operations, or bundled-payment design.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Aggregate payment gains may hide important subgroup effects
   - Why it matters: The rule emphasizes total increases and savings, but hospitals do not all start from the same position. Rural, teaching, low-volume, and safety-net hospitals may face different operational burdens or different exposure to model incentives.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Can CMS provide a clearer breakdown of impacts by hospital type, including rural, safety-net, teaching, low-volume, and former-MDH hospitals?

2. Cross-program quality-measure alignment needs stronger visible validation
   - Why it matters: CMS is proposing multiple new or modified measures at once. Alignment across programs can reduce fragmentation, but only if the measures are reliable and fit for their intended uses.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What validation, risk-adjustment, and confounding analysis support these modified mortality, sepsis-readmission, and eCQM proposals for the timelines CMS has chosen?

3. The sepsis readmission proposal deserves extra scrutiny
   - Why it matters: Sepsis is a high-variation area where coding practices, patient complexity, and post-acute factors may affect measured performance.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: How has CMS tested whether the sepsis readmission measure distinguishes hospital performance from coding or case-mix differences?

4. Mandatory `CJR-X` expansion should come with more subgroup and sensitivity analysis
   - Why it matters: CMS says `CJR-X` would save about `$725 million` across five performance years, but nationwide mandatory models can produce uneven results across markets and hospital types.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What do the savings and quality projections look like under less favorable assumptions, including regional variation, outpatient migration, or atypical episode patterns?

5. The anti-discrimination provision may need more implementation clarity
   - Why it matters: Even if CMS expects lawful entities to need little change, a new regulatory text can still raise practical questions about what policies or practices are clearly acceptable.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Can CMS provide examples or guidance showing how the anti-discrimination provision applies to common admissions, recruitment, and accreditation scenarios?

6. Transfer effects and compliance costs should be presented more separately
   - Why it matters: The rule includes payment increases, burden increases, burden decreases, and model-based savings claims. Those are not all the same kind of economic effect.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Can CMS present a cleaner side-by-side breakdown separating payment transfers, administrative burden, implementation costs, and projected behavior-change savings?
