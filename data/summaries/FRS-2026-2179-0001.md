# Public Summary: FRS-2026-2179-0001

## What This Document Does
- The Federal Reserve Board proposes to amend Regulation A so that any institution holding a new `Payment Account` at a Federal Reserve Bank would be ineligible for discount window credit.
- The change would apply to all three standard discount window programs: primary credit, secondary credit, and seasonal credit.
- The proposal does not change discount window rates or the basic structure of those programs.
- It also does not change discount window eligibility for other depository institutions using ordinary master accounts or correspondent arrangements.
- This rule is part of a broader same-day package on proposed Payment Accounts, which are described as a new optional way for certain institutions to access Federal Reserve accounts and services.

## Why It Matters
- This is a boundary-setting rule. The Board is deciding that if it creates a new special-purpose Payment Account, holders of that account should not also receive one of the Federal Reserve's most important emergency liquidity backstops.
- The Board's stated reasons are prudential: likely Payment Account holders may be uninsured, may operate under varied insolvency regimes, may follow novel business models, and may be harder for Reserve Banks to monitor using existing supervisory information channels.
- At the same time, the Board admits there is another side to the issue. It says keeping current eligibility rules might help financial stability and monetary-policy implementation by allowing otherwise eligible Payment Account holders to obtain liquidity in stress periods.
- That makes this a useful comment target. The proposal is not obviously unlawful or procedurally irregular, but the record is thinner on why a full categorical ban is better than narrower alternatives.

## Who Is Affected
- Institutions that may seek a proposed Federal Reserve `Payment Account` rather than a traditional master account.
- Nontraditional or payments-focused firms that want direct Federal Reserve access for clearing and settlement activity.
- Smaller eligible institutions that may have to choose between the operational benefits of a Payment Account and continued discount window access.
- Reserve Banks, which would administer the new account category and the associated credit restriction.
- Commenters with expertise in payments, liquidity risk, banking supervision, insolvency, or emergency operations.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: The Board relies on a categorical risk rationale for a potentially diverse group of Payment Account holders.
   - Why it matters: A blanket exclusion may be overbroad if some likely account holders are lower-risk or could satisfy targeted safeguards.
   - Practical question to ask: What evidence shows that likely Payment Account holders as a class are too risky or too opaque for any form of discount window access?

2. Issue: The alternatives analysis is acknowledged but thin.
   - Why it matters: The NPRM itself asks whether narrower restrictions or access in limited circumstances would be better, but the preamble does not explain in much detail why those options lose.
   - Practical question to ask: Why did the Board reject options such as collateral-based eligibility, enhanced reporting, institution-type distinctions, or stress-only access?

3. Issue: The notice asserts information and insolvency concerns without much public support.
   - Why it matters: Those concerns may be real, but commenters need enough detail to test whether they justify a full exclusion rather than a more tailored design.
   - Practical question to ask: What specific supervisory-information gaps or insolvency risks does the Board expect to face, and why can those not be mitigated through account terms or credit conditions?

4. Issue: The Board acknowledges possible financial-stability benefits from access but does not weigh them deeply.
   - Why it matters: The strongest case for some access may arise during operational incidents or market stress, which is exactly when liquidity policy matters most.
   - Practical question to ask: Under what scenarios, if any, would limited or emergency access improve resilience more than it increases risk?

5. Issue: The small-entity analysis may be too formalistic.
   - Why it matters: Saying Payment Accounts are optional does not fully answer whether the proposed tradeoff changes real choices for smaller firms seeking direct Federal Reserve access.
   - Practical question to ask: How many smaller eligible institutions could realistically consider a Payment Account, and how would the loss of discount window access affect that decision?

## Evidence Quotes

- “not permitting discount window access for Payment Account holders would mitigate risk to the Reserve Banks”
- “Current credit risk monitoring at Reserve Banks relies mostly on supervisory information”
- “It is possible that permitting discount window access for Payment Account holders ... may support broader financial stability”
- “Should the Board instead consider a narrow restriction ... or permit access ... in limited circumstances”

This NPRM is comment-worthy because the Board has stated a clear policy preference but left room to challenge how broad that preference should be. The most useful comments will likely focus on comparative design: whether the Board has shown enough to justify a full ban, or whether conditional access, narrower exclusions, or stress-only access would better balance innovation, supervision, and liquidity risk.
