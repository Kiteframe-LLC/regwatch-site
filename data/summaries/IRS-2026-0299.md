## What This Document Does
- Proposes the IRS rules for how a child can receive the one-time `$1,000` Trump Accounts Contribution Pilot Program payment created by `26 U.S.C. 6434`.
- Says an authorized adult may make a pilot-program election for an eligible child who is a U.S. citizen, has a valid Social Security number, and was born in `2025` through `2028`.
- Uses a legal device called a "special taxable year" so that, once the election is processed, the child is treated as having made a `$1,000` tax payment that becomes a `$1,000` overpayment and is refunded directly into the child's Trump account.
- Requires the election to be made on an IRS-prescribed form or electronic application rather than through the return itself.
- Makes timing and processing order matter: the election can be made from the day the child becomes eligible until December 31 of the year the child turns 17, and only the first processed election counts.

## Why It Matters
- This proposal does not build the whole Trump-account system. It decides who can trigger the initial public contribution and when they can do it.
- The IRS argues that earlier elections can help children because money invested earlier has more time to grow. Its main quantitative example says the median difference at age 18 between investing `$1,000` at birth and investing it six months later was about `$300`.
- The record does not estimate how many children will benefit because elections can be filed outside the tax return, how often competing adults may file, or how the IRS will handle mistakes and disputes.
- The proposal also adopts a forward-looking eligibility standard. An adult can file based on expecting the child to be that adult's qualifying child for the year, and the election is not automatically undone if later facts show that expectation was wrong. That makes the rule more flexible, but also raises fairness and verification questions.

## Who Is Affected
- Eligible children born during `2025-2028` who meet the citizenship and Social Security number requirements.
- Parents, guardians, and other adults who may try to make the election for a child.
- Families with complicated caregiving or household arrangements, because the proposal uses anticipation of qualifying-child status and a first-processed-election rule.
- IRS administrators and account providers who will need to process elections, reject invalid ones, and ensure the contribution goes to the correct Trump account.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. The forward-looking qualifying-child rule may be too loose without better safeguards.
   - Why it matters: The NPRM allows an adult to file based on anticipating that a child will be that adult's qualifying child for the year. That helps early filing, but it can also create disputes if family circumstances are unsettled.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What verification, notice, or correction process will IRS use when more than one adult could plausibly claim the same child?

2. The first-processed-election rule could create race-to-file problems.
   - Why it matters: Once IRS processes the first election, later elections for the same child will not be processed. That makes processing order a substantive design choice.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Should there be a short challenge or cure period before a processed election becomes effectively final for the child?

3. The record does not show that a separate form or web page is the main reason participation will improve.
   - Why it matters: IRS argues that earlier filing helps investment growth, but the record does not show whether the real barriers are timing, account setup, awareness, or SSN timing.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence does IRS have that filing outside the tax return will materially increase timely uptake, rather than just change the filing channel?

4. The age-17 deadline is explained, but not fully justified against other options.
   - Why it matters: A long filing window increases flexibility, but may also increase administrative complexity and weaken incentives for timely enrollment.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Why is age 17 the best balance between accessibility and administrability, rather than a shorter default window with targeted relief for late cases?

## Evidence Quotes
- "Proposed § 301.6434-1(b)(1)(i) permits the child's eligibility as long as a pilot program-electing individual anticipates the eligible child will be that individual's qualifying child under section 152(c) for the taxable year in which the election is made."
- "The pilot program election will not be rendered ineffective solely on the basis that it is later determined that the eligible child does not meet the definition of a qualifying child of the individual for the taxable year in which the pilot program election is made."
- "The proposed regulations would require a pilot program election to be made on a form, electronic application, or web page."
- "Among birth cohorts from June 1926 to May 2007, the median difference in account value at age 18 between $1,000 invested at birth and $1,000 invested six months after birth was $300."
- "A pilot program election must be made ... no later than December 31 of the calendar year in which the eligible child attains age 17."
- "Only the first pilot program election processed by the Secretary with respect to an eligible child will result in a $1,000 overpayment being refunded as a $1,000 pilot program contribution."
