## What This Document Does

This FCC notice proposes a broad set of rules meant to push more customer-service work for communications providers back into the United States. It is not limited to robocalls or scam calls. It reaches ordinary customer-service interactions and would apply to telecommunications carriers, CMRS providers, interconnected VoIP providers, cable operators, DBS providers, and some affiliated internet-access operations.

The proposal includes several different tools at once:

* a requirement that foreign call-center staff be proficient in spoken and written American Standard English
* a cap on the share of calls that may be handled outside the United States, with the actual percentage left open as `[XX]`
* a requirement to tell customers when a call is being handled abroad and to offer transfer to a U.S.-based representative on request
* a rule that sensitive interactions involving items like passwords, account information, bank details, or multifactor codes must be handled in the United States
* a prohibition on using call centers in “foreign adversary” nations
* tracking and reporting obligations
* a separate request for comment on bond-style financial deterrents for unlawful foreign-originated calls

The FCC also asks whether some of these protections should be extended beyond phone calls to email, texts, and online chat.

## Why It Matters

This is a significant policy move because it treats customer-service location itself as a consumer-protection and privacy issue. The FCC is not just asking for more disclosure. It is considering location-based operating rules that could raise labor costs, change vendor relationships, affect service quality, and reshape how providers route customer contacts.

The record suggests three overlapping rationales:

* customer-service quality
* protection of sensitive consumer information
* national-security concerns tied to certain foreign jurisdictions

The most important weakness in the current record is that some of the strongest proposed requirements are not yet tightly connected to quantified evidence. The FCC says a cap on foreign-handled calls would best address its concerns, but it does not identify the actual percentage in the proposed rule text beyond `[XX]`. It also recognizes that onshoring has costs and asks commenters for more data on current call-center location, staffing, and expenses.

That means this is a strong comment opportunity. The agency is plainly still deciding how hard to push and how to justify it.

## Who Is Affected

* Communications providers that use foreign call centers for customer-service or account-support work.
* Affiliates that handle internet-access-related customer communications.
* Call-center contractors and subcontractors serving those providers.
* Consumers whose service interactions involve billing, authentication, account access, or complaint resolution.
* Smaller providers that may have less capacity to move work quickly or build detailed compliance reporting systems.

Unknown from available text:

* the final percentage cap on foreign-handled calls
* the exact test for “American Standard English” proficiency
* the final reporting cadence
* whether the FCC will extend the rules to stand-alone internet-only or non-interconnected VoIP providers

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. The FCC has not justified the key cap it wants to impose.
Why it matters: The proposal treats a call-share cap as one of its main tools, but the percentage is still blank. That number will drive costs, operational disruption, and whether the rule is actually tailored to the problem.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence will the FCC use to choose the final cap, and why would a narrower transaction-based rule not address the same privacy and service concerns?

2. The English-proficiency rule is important but too subjective as written.
Why it matters: A rule that depends on “American Standard English” could become hard to measure, unevenly enforced, or used as a vague vendor-screening standard without clear compliance criteria.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What testing, auditing, or performance measures would the FCC require to show compliance with this proficiency standard?

3. The transfer-right and equal-wait-time rule needs operational detail.
Why it matters: A right to transfer is only meaningful if providers must preserve queue position, track wait times consistently, and document what counts as a customer-requested transfer.
Practical question a commenter can ask: How will the FCC define and verify transfer timing, queue treatment, and dropped-call reporting so this right works in practice?

4. The proposal’s legal scope is stronger for some provider categories than for others.
Why it matters: The FCC openly asks whether it has legal authority to extend the proposal to stand-alone internet-only and non-interconnected VoIP providers. If the agency expands too far without a stronger explanation, the final rule becomes more vulnerable.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What is the service-by-service statutory basis for each proposed requirement, especially for providers outside the clearest core communications categories?

5. The foreign-originated-call bond concept is still underdeveloped.
Why it matters: A bond requirement could become expensive and complex, yet the NPRM still asks basic questions about who would pay, who would administer it, how bond draws would work, and what statutory barriers may exist.
Practical question a commenter can ask: Should the FCC defer any bond proposal until it publishes a fuller design covering triggers, liable parties, disputes, and statutory authority?

## Evidence Quotes

* “We propose to require providers that use offshore call centers to ensure that all calling staff at those call centers are proficient in both written and spoken American Standard English.”
* “We believe that such a cap would encourage movement of call center operations back to the U.S. and thus best address our communication and data privacy concerns.”
* “We propose to prohibit providers from using call centers located in ‘foreign adversary’ nations.”
* “We seek comment on whether we should apply our proposals to ‘stand-alone’ providers of non-interconnected VoIP and other internet-only providers, including providers that provide only stand-alone internet access service, to the extent that we have legal authority to do so.”
* “The data suggest that customer service representatives are paid significantly more in the United States compared to India, the Philippines, and Mexico.”
