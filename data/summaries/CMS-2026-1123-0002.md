## What This Document Does

- CMS proposes its annual FY 2027 update for the Inpatient Psychiatric Facilities Prospective Payment System, or IPF PPS.
- The routine part of the rule updates the base rate, ECT payment, labor-related share, wage-index factors, and fixed-loss threshold for outlier payments.
- The less routine part proposes a new facility-level cap on outlier payments. CMS says an IPF could receive outlier payments only up to 20 percent of its total IPF PPS payments in a year. If a facility crosses that line, it would still receive normal per diem payments but would stop receiving outlier payments for additional high-outlier cases.
- The rule also proposes quality-reporting changes. CMS would implement a standardized IPF patient assessment instrument, the IPF-PAI, under statutory direction from the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023.
- At the same time, CMS would remove two existing IPF Quality Reporting Program measures: the alcohol brief-intervention measure `SUB-2/2a` and the tobacco-treatment-at-discharge measure `TOB-3/3a`.
- CMS presents the package as a mix of annual payment maintenance and targeted policy refinement. The notice says the payment update would increase FY 2027 payments to IPFs by about `$50 million`, while the quality-reporting proposals would create a net facility cost increase of `$7,223,725`.

## Why It Matters

- Medicare payment updates for psychiatric facilities matter because even modest changes in the PPS formula can affect service availability, staffing decisions, and the willingness of hospitals and units to continue serving complex psychiatric patients.
- The 20 percent outlier cap is the biggest comment target in this document. That is not just a mechanical inflation update. It is a policy choice about how much unusually costly care Medicare should continue to reimburse through outlier payments before CMS cuts the stream off for the rest of the year.
- The quality-reporting changes also matter because they trade one kind of burden for another. CMS proposes a new standardized assessment instrument that may improve data collection, but it also proposes removing focused measures on alcohol and nicotine-related intervention before the replacement pathway is fully visible in the public-facing record.
- The visible record gives a plausible policy story. CMS has clear legal authority to update rates annually and to implement standardized patient assessment. It also gives some rationale for the quality-measure removals and explicitly asks for comment on alternative cap percentages and alternative nicotine-related data collection.
- The weaker part of the record is calibration. The notice does not appear to show much facility-level data on why `20 percent` is the right cap, how many IPFs would be affected, or whether those facilities treat unusually complex populations. It also does not publicly unpack the burden assumptions behind the `$7.2 million` quality-reporting estimate in much detail.

## Who Is Affected

- Inpatient psychiatric hospitals and psychiatric units paid under the IPF PPS.
- Medicare beneficiaries receiving inpatient psychiatric care, especially patients whose cases are unusually costly or complex.
- Facilities with high outlier-payment shares, because they would be most directly affected by the new cap.
- IPF administrators, coding and billing staff, quality-reporting teams, and IT staff who would have to implement the IPF-PAI.
- Behavioral-health clinicians and quality teams that currently work with alcohol- and tobacco-related discharge measures.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

- Issue: The notice does not fully show why `20 percent` is the right outlier-cap threshold.
Why it matters: This is the most consequential new payment-policy choice in the rule, and the public-facing record only partly explains why this line is better than another percentage or a more tailored design.
One practical question a commenter can ask: How many facilities would exceed the proposed cap, what are their case-mix characteristics, and how would results change under alternative percentages?

- Issue: The cap may affect facilities serving unusually complex psychiatric populations.
Why it matters: If a facility's high outlier share reflects legitimate patient complexity rather than billing anomalies, the cap could reduce payment for medically demanding cases and create access pressure.
One practical question a commenter can ask: Did CMS analyze whether facilities most likely to exceed the cap serve distinct patient populations or face atypical case-severity patterns?

- Issue: The `$7,223,725` quality-reporting cost estimate is not fully unpacked in the visible record.
Why it matters: Facilities need to know whether the estimate includes realistic assumptions for staff training, workflow redesign, IT changes, validation, and transition costs tied to the IPF-PAI.
One practical question a commenter can ask: What cost categories and implementation assumptions produce the estimated net increase in IPF Quality Reporting Program costs?

- Issue: The record only partly explains the transition away from `SUB-2/2a` and `TOB-3/3a`.
Why it matters: Removing targeted alcohol and tobacco intervention measures before the substitute collection pathway is fully shown could create a temporary gap in accountability or visibility.
One practical question a commenter can ask: What exact information would be lost by removing these measures, and when would the IPF-PAI or another tool fully replace that information?

- Issue: The proposal relies on forecast inputs that CMS may later update.
Why it matters: CMS says it may use newer data for the final rule, which means commenters may be assessing a proposal whose key numeric inputs could still move.
One practical question a commenter can ask: Can CMS provide sensitivity analysis showing how newer market-basket, productivity, or labor-share data could change the final payment update?
