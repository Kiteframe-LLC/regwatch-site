## What This Document Does

- The FCC is considering stronger `Know Your Customer` (`KYC`) rules for originating phone providers as part of its anti-robocall program.
- The notice asks whether providers should be required to collect baseline information from new and renewing customers before giving them service, including items such as name, physical address, government-issued identification number, and an alternate phone number.
- It also asks whether higher-risk customers, such as high-volume, business, or foreign customers, should have to provide more information, such as intended use of service and call-origin IP address.
- The FCC is also considering rules on verifying customer information, keeping records, re-verifying information over time, and using risk-based controls for prepaid and other service models.
- One especially concrete piece of the proposal is an enforcement change: the FCC proposes to add a `$2,500` per-call forfeiture amount for KYC violations.

## Why It Matters

- The FCC is trying to stop illegal calls earlier in the process by focusing on the providers that first let callers onto the network.
- The basic theory is easy to follow: if providers know more about who they are serving, bad actors may have a harder time getting access, and enforcement agencies may find it easier to trace them later.
- The main weakness in the visible record is not that the FCC has no reason to act. It is that the notice is much more developed on direction than on calibration.
- The notice asks many good questions, but it does not yet show much empirical support for why this exact set of customer data should be required, how `high-volume` or `higher-risk` customers should be defined, or how much burden the rules would impose on different kinds of providers.
- The FCC also says many providers already take KYC measures and therefore “tentatively conclude[s]” that added compliance costs will be minimal. That may be true for some larger providers, but the notice does not visibly break out costs for smaller providers, prepaid services, VoIP firms, or providers that do not already run sophisticated document-verification systems.
- The per-call penalty proposal raises the stakes further. If the FCC finalizes a strong penalty before clearly defining what counts as compliance, providers may respond by over-collecting personal data or screening legitimate customers too aggressively.

## Who Is Affected

- Originating voice providers, including carriers and other providers that onboard customers who can place calls onto the network.
- Smaller providers and prepaid-service providers that may have fewer compliance systems and fewer staff for identity verification and data retention.
- Business, foreign, and potentially high-volume customers who could face enhanced KYC review.
- Consumers more broadly, because stricter KYC may help reduce illegal calls but may also increase collection and storage of personal information.
- Commenters with operational experience in onboarding, fraud prevention, privacy compliance, prepaid services, or small-provider economics are especially well positioned to improve the record.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. The FCC should justify the minimum required data set more clearly.
- Why it matters: The notice lists several pieces of customer information but does not show why each one is necessary or how much each field helps deter or trace illegal callers.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: What evidence supports requiring each proposed data element, and which elements are optional or low-value?

2. The proposal needs objective thresholds for `high-volume` and other risk tiers.
- Why it matters: If risk categories are vague, providers may screen similar customers differently or over-collect information to avoid liability.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: What numeric, behavioral, or account-based criteria would trigger enhanced KYC review?

3. The FCC's cost discussion is still thin.
- Why it matters: The notice tentatively says incremental costs will be minimal, but it does not show that assumption across different provider sizes and service models.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: How would implementation costs differ for nationwide carriers, smaller VoIP providers, MVNOs, and prepaid-focused businesses?

4. Privacy and retention limits need more development.
- Why it matters: Stronger KYC can mean more collection and storage of personally identifiable information, which creates its own risks.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: What retention periods, access controls, and data-minimization rules would apply to newly collected customer information?

5. Per-call penalties should be tied to clear compliance rules.
- Why it matters: A `$2,500` per-call penalty can escalate quickly, so the underlying duty needs to be clear enough that providers know what conduct creates liability.
- Practical question a commenter can ask: Will the FCC define safe harbors, cure periods, or examples showing how per-call KYC violations would be counted?

## Evidence Quotes

- “We believe that requiring originating providers to gather this basic identification information will have two benefits.”
- “Periodic review or ongoing re-verification of KYC information is essential”
- “We anticipate that many originating providers already take KYC compliance measures and therefore tentatively conclude that any incremental compliance costs will be minimal.”
- “We expect that any changes to the rules will eliminate confusion and provide clear guidance to originating providers where ambiguity exists.”
- “we propose to codify the forfeiture amount for KYC violations on a per call basis”
