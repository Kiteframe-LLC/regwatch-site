## What This Document Does
- Proposes a new `12 CFR part 706` to implement part of the GENIUS Act for credit-union-affiliated payment stablecoin issuers.
- Sets up the licensing process for a permitted payment stablecoin issuer (`PPSI`) that is a subsidiary of a federally insured credit union (`FICU`).
- Uses a joint-application model in which the subsidiary applies together with information and certifications tied to parent companies, directors, officers, and some principal shareholders.
- Creates procedures for investigations, application review, denials, hearings, reapplication, AML/sanctions certification, and change-in-control notices.
- Would prohibit a FICU from investing in a payment stablecoin issuer unless that issuer is licensed by the NCUA.
- Does **not** yet propose the full substantive operating standards for PPSIs; the notice says a later proposal will address those requirements.

## Why It Matters
- This is the front door for stablecoin activity in the credit-union sector. Even though it is framed as a licensing rule, it can affect who enters the market and how quickly.
- The proposal makes an important structural choice: instead of requiring every investing credit union to file its own application, the NCUA prefers a joint application by the subsidiary and related parent-company parties.
- The investment limitation is also a major policy choice. If only NCUA-licensed PPSIs may receive FICU investment, market access may depend heavily on how many licenses are granted and how quickly the process works.
- The record is thinner than the stakes suggest on burden and access. The rule is a “significant regulatory action” under Executive Order 12866, but the main public burden discussion is a short PRA estimate of 440 annual hours and an RFA certification for small credit unions.
- Because a later proposal will address substantive PPSI standards, commenters are being asked to evaluate the licensing architecture before the complete operating rule is visible.

## Who Is Affected
- Federally insured credit unions that may want to invest in or organize stablecoin-issuing subsidiaries.
- Credit union service organizations and other subsidiaries that may seek PPSI licenses.
- Directors, officers, parent companies, and some principal shareholders that may need to supply information, certifications, and background materials.
- State-chartered insured credit unions whose subsidiaries would still need NCUA licensure.
- Members and service providers that may eventually rely on credit-union-linked stablecoin products or infrastructure.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: The joint-application structure may be underexplained.
   - Why it matters: This is the proposal's central burden-allocation choice and determines how ownership and control will be screened.
   - Question to ask: What comparative analysis led the NCUA to choose a joint-application model rather than a tiered notice system or investor-by-investor filing structure?

2. Issue: The investment limitation could become a practical bottleneck.
   - Why it matters: If few NCUA-licensed PPSIs are approved quickly, the rule could reduce participation options rather than simply improve prudential oversight.
   - Question to ask: What evidence supports the Board's statement that limiting FICU investment to NCUA-licensed PPSIs “should not pose a barrier” to industry participation?

3. Issue: The burden estimate may be understated.
   - Why it matters: Novel licensing applications involving ownership mapping, background checks, AML certification, and legal review may require more than the public estimate suggests.
   - Question to ask: Please disclose the assumptions behind the estimate of 10 respondents and 440 total annual burden hours, including any expected follow-up requests or remediation work.

4. Issue: The small-entity analysis may rely on a narrow baseline.
   - Why it matters: Using current CUSO investment rates to support the RFA certification may understate future effects on smaller credit unions that might want to enter this new market.
   - Question to ask: Why is existing CUSO participation the right proxy for future impact on small credit unions interested in stablecoin-related activity?

5. Issue: The record is incomplete on how this rule interacts with the later substantive PPSI proposal.
   - Why it matters: A licensing framework can create path dependence before the public sees the full compliance regime.
   - Question to ask: How does the NCUA expect this licensing proposal to interact with the forthcoming standards-and-restrictions proposal, and will applicants receive any transition or safe-harbor protections?

## Evidence Quotes
- “The Board has preliminarily determined that it is preferrable for FICU subsidiaries themselves to submit the required applications to be an NCUA-licensed PPSI jointly with their FICU Parent Company(ies) ... rather than having every single FICU investing in them submit an application.”
- “The Board's proposed approach would also require the applying FICU subsidiary, and any of its FICU Parent Companies and Principal Shareholders, to provide written certification that any filing or supporting material submitted to the NCUA contains no material misrepresentations or omissions.”
- “The Board believes this limitation is consistent with the definition of FICU subsidiary in the GENIUS Act and should not pose a barrier to the credit union industry's ability to facilitate payment stablecoin services for their members.”
- “OMB has determined that this proposed rule is a ‘significant regulatory action’ as defined in section 3(f) of Executive Order 12866.”
- “Accordingly, the NCUA certifies the proposed rule would not have a significant economic impact on a substantial number of small credit unions.”
- “The proposed rule contains information collection reporting requirements that would impose PRA burden governing the application and licensing of permitted payment stablecoin issuers (PPSIs). The NCUA estimates a total annual burden of 440 hours...”
