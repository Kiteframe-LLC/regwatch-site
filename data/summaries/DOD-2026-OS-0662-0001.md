## What This Document Does

* DoD proposes to amend its Privacy Act rule for `CIG-04`, the Office of Inspector General criminal-investigation records system.
* The proposal would keep an existing exemption under `5 U.S.C. 552a(j)(2)` and add exemptions under `5 U.S.C. 552a(k)(1)` and `(k)(2)`.
* In plain terms, that means DoD wants broader authority to withhold some notice, access, amendment, disclosure-accounting, and related Privacy Act rights for records in this system.
* DoD says the reasons are national security, criminal-investigation needs, source protection, and avoidance of interference with ongoing investigations.
* The rule also updates the system name from the older `Case Control System` label to `Inspector General Criminal Investigation Records`.

## Why It Matters

* This is not a major cost rule, but it still matters because it affects when people can learn about, inspect, or seek correction of records the government keeps about them.
* DoD's basic legal theory is conventional. The Privacy Act does allow agencies to claim exemptions for properly classified records and certain law-enforcement investigatory material.
* The more useful question for commenters is whether DoD has explained the proposal with enough specificity. The notice relies heavily on general statements that disclosure could harm national security, expose sources, or tip off investigative subjects.
* Those concerns are plausible, but the visible record is thin on what the new exemptions add beyond the exemption DoD already has under `5 U.S.C. 552a(j)(2)`.
* The notice does include one important limiting sentence: records are exempt only to the extent the reason for the exemption actually applies to the record. That is helpful, but it remains abstract unless DoD explains how it will make that determination in practice.
* This creates a solid comment opportunity. A constructive commenter can accept that some investigative secrecy is legitimate while still asking DoD to show why the added exemption authorities are necessary and how the agency will avoid overbroad use.

## Who Is Affected

* People whose information may appear in DoD OIG criminal-investigation records, including investigative subjects and possibly witnesses, informants, complainants, or other related individuals.
* DoD OIG personnel who manage investigations and process Privacy Act requests.
* Transparency, civil-liberties, due-process, and records-governance commenters who track how agencies limit access and correction rights.
* Lawyers, advocates, journalists, and watchdog groups interested in how investigative systems balance secrecy with accountability.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

* Issue: DoD says the existing `j(2)` exemption will remain in place, but the notice does not clearly explain what practical work the new `k(1)` and `k(2)` exemptions add.
Why it matters: If the older exemption already covers most of the sensitive records, commenters can reasonably ask why broader or overlapping exemptions are needed.
Question to ask: What categories of records are expected to fall within `k(1)` or `k(2)` that are not already adequately addressed by the continuing `j(2)` exemption?

* Issue: The justifications are mostly generic and do not provide many system-specific examples.
Why it matters: General statements about harm are common in exemption rules, but the administrative record is stronger when the agency explains why this particular system presents those risks.
Question to ask: Can DoD provide high-level examples of record types or scenarios showing why access, amendment, or disclosure accounting would create the harms described?

* Issue: The notice says records are exempt only when the exemption rationale pertains to the record, but it does not explain how that tailoring works operationally.
Why it matters: Without clearer limiting principles, the public cannot easily evaluate whether mixed files, closed investigations, or segregable non-sensitive material may still be accessible.
Question to ask: How will DoD determine when a record is only partly exempt, no longer exempt, or suitable for partial disclosure?

* Issue: The proposal says little about the practical rights impact on individuals.
Why it matters: Even if the rule does not impose classic business compliance costs, it can still reduce the ability of affected people to inspect or challenge inaccurate records.
Question to ask: What safeguards exist to prevent stale, irrelevant, or inaccurate information from remaining effectively unreviewable in this system?

* Issue: The notice mentions a modified system-of-records notice published elsewhere in the same Federal Register issue, but does not explain in detail whether that companion change is what makes the new exemptions necessary.
Why it matters: If something material about the system changed, commenters should be able to see that connection in the rulemaking record.
Question to ask: What specific changes in the companion system notice, if any, created the need for these additional exemptions now?

## Evidence Quotes

* “The existing exemption under 5 U.S.C. 552a(j)(2) will continue to be claimed for this system.”
* “This rulemaking seeks public comment on the addition of exemptions under 5 U.S.C. 552a(k)(1) and (k)(2).”
* “The DoD OIG proposes this exemption because certain records may contain classified national security information.”
* “The DoD OIG also proposes to exempt this system of records because these records support the conduct of criminal law enforcement activities.”
* “Records in this system of records are only exempt from the Privacy Act to the extent that the purposes underlying the exemption pertain to the record.”
