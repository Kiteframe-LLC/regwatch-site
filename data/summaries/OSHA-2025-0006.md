# Public Summary: OSHA-2025-0006-0013

_Source file: `summaries/OSHA-2025-0006-0013.md`_  
_Detected type: `NPRM`_

## What This Document Does
- Proposes to remove OSHA's initial medical-evaluation requirement for workers required to use filtering facepiece respirators (FFRs) and loose-fitting powered air-purifying respirators (PAPRs).
- Keeps the rest of the Respiratory Protection Standard in place, including hazard assessment, respirator selection, fit testing where required, training, and maintenance.
- Keeps medical-evaluation requirements for other respirator types, so this is a targeted carveout rather than a full rollback of OSHA's respirator program.
- Bases the proposal on OSHA's view that the public evidence does not clearly show that mandatory medical screening meaningfully prevents health impairment for these two lower-burden respirator categories.

## Why It Matters
- This removes a long-standing screening checkpoint for a large worker population while leaving other respirator-program duties in place.
- OSHA's argument depends on three ideas: the evidence for screening benefit is weak, very few workers are restricted after screening, and widespread post-COVID use did not surface clear evidence of harm from the lack of screening.
- That creates a central comment question: does uncertainty about benefit justify deleting the requirement, or does it justify a narrower, more tailored revision instead?
- The issue matters most for workers with underlying respiratory or cardiovascular conditions and workers in hot or high-exertion settings.
- The reopened record also matters because OSHA later disclosed that ACCSH voted 5-4 to recommend that the agency not proceed and instead continue developing best practices.

## Who Is Affected
- Workers required to use FFRs, including many healthcare, industrial, and dusty-task users.
- Workers required to use loose-fitting PAPRs.
- Employers that now pay for questionnaires, follow-up exams, and related recordkeeping.
- Occupational-health providers who review questionnaires or conduct follow-up exams.
- Small and dispersed workplaces that may benefit from lower compliance costs but may also have weaker internal monitoring.
- Construction and maritime employers too, because OSHA says the change would flow through standards that incorporate the general Respiratory Protection Standard.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Issue: OSHA relies heavily on lack of proven screening benefit, but does not clearly show that this justifies a full carveout
   - Why it matters: A weak evidence base for a requirement can support refinement, but it does not automatically support eliminating the requirement for all users of these respirator types.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Why did OSHA choose a categorical exemption instead of narrower options such as duration limits, high-exertion triggers, or symptom-risk screening for workers with known cardiopulmonary concerns?

2. Issue: The proposal uses the absence of surfaced COVID-era adverse-event data as a major support point
   - Why it matters: If OSHA did not identify the actual surveillance systems or datasets it reviewed, commenters cannot tell whether the agency found "no problem" or merely lacked a strong way to detect one.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: What specific adverse-event, injury, illness, or surveillance datasets did OSHA review when concluding it was not aware of harmful outcomes from workers using these respirators without medical evaluations?

3. Issue: The line OSHA draws between exempt respirators and non-exempt respirators may be underexplained
   - Why it matters: The record shows that burden varies by respirator type, but also by exertion, duration, heat, and worker health status. A type-based line may be too blunt.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Please explain why respirator type is the right legal and practical boundary instead of use duration, workload, temperature, or employee medical-risk factors.

4. Issue: The cost-savings estimate depends on dated and assumption-heavy inputs
   - Why it matters: OSHA's public economic case uses a 2001 NIOSH respirator-use survey adjusted to later employment figures, plus assumptions about turnover and noncompliance correction.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: Please publish updated sensitivity analyses using higher and lower respirator-use prevalence, different turnover assumptions, and alternative assumptions about how much baseline noncompliance would ever actually be corrected.

5. Issue: The rule does not visibly answer ACCSH's recommendation not to proceed
   - Why it matters: The reopened record now includes an advisory-committee recommendation against the proposal. If OSHA finalizes the rule, it should squarely explain why it rejected that advice.
   - Practical question a commenter can ask: How does OSHA respond to ACCSH's recommendation that the agency not proceed and instead focus on best practices for employers?

## Evidence Quotes
- "OSHA concludes that the data available for health effects are lacking and insufficient to establish that medical evaluations meaningfully reduce material impairment caused by wearing an FFR or a loose fitting PAPR."
- "Despite this sudden increased and widespread usage of these respirators, often without a medical evaluation, the agency is not aware of any data identifying adverse outcomes from individuals with underlying medical conditions during the COVID-19 pandemic."
- "In practice, few workers have their respirator use limited."
- "Only those employees using either a filtering facepiece respirator (FFR) or loose-fitting powered air purifying respirator (PAPR) will no longer have to complete a medical questionnaire or receive a medical evaluation."
- "OSHA estimates the annualized cost savings would be approximately $81 million at a 3 percent discount rate, or $83 million at a 7 percent discount rate."
- "ACCSH recommends that OSHA not proceed with the proposal and continue to look for best practices and provide those to employers."
