## What This Document Does

- Extends the public comment period for EPA's April 29, 2026 proposal on Maryland's municipal waste combustor NOx rules, but does not materially change that underlying proposal.
- The substantive NPRM would approve Maryland air-plan revisions that set reasonably available control technology, or RACT, limits for nitrogen oxides from the state's two large municipal waste combustors.
- The action combines pieces of two Maryland SIP submissions. One older submission (`#18-04`) supplies definitions and related provisions. The later submission (`#20-10`) supplies the stronger large-facility limits.
- For normal operation, the proposal relies on concentration-based NOx limits, including 24-hour block averages and tighter 30-day rolling averages.
- For non-normal operation, the proposal also approves mass-based limits that apply during startup, shutdown, and warm-up so that a limit applies during all hours of operation.
- EPA says this package satisfies Maryland's RACT duties for the 2008 and 2015 ozone standards.

## Why It Matters

- The selected document is procedural, but the extension matters because it preserves the chance to challenge or improve the underlying SIP approval record before EPA finalizes it.
- This is a narrow state-plan approval, but it still matters because the main issue is whether EPA has shown that Maryland closed a real regulatory gap. Older rules did not fully cover some startup and shutdown conditions with continuously applicable limits.
- EPA's theory is that municipal waste combustors cannot always be judged fairly by the same concentration-based metric during warm-up or startup because extra air changes the oxygen-correction math. So Maryland switched to mass-based limits for those modes.
- If EPA is right, the final SIP would be stronger and more enforceable than before because there would be a continuous NOx standard for all operating modes.
- If EPA has not explained that switch clearly enough, commenters have a useful opening. The biggest question is not broad anti-regulatory politics. It is whether the mode-specific limits are truly equivalent, transparent, and enforceable.

## Who Is Affected

- The direct regulated parties are Maryland's two large municipal waste combustors:
- Montgomery County Resource Recovery Facility.
- Wheelabrator Baltimore.
- Maryland environmental regulators are also affected because they will administer the approved SIP provisions and receive the quarterly compliance reports.
- Nearby communities, air-quality advocates, and local governments may care because the rule concerns NOx controls tied to ozone planning.
- Commenters with technical knowledge of combustor operations, CEMS monitoring, startup/shutdown procedures, or SIP enforceability are especially well positioned to add useful evidence.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

- Issue: EPA relies heavily on a technical support document to say the startup, shutdown, and warm-up limits are equivalent to the normal-operation limits.
Why it matters: That equivalency claim is the main bridge between the old SIP defect and EPA's proposed approval. The visible NPRM itself does not walk readers through the calculations in a simple way.
Question a commenter can ask: Can EPA add a clear table showing how each mass-based alternative limit was derived from the normal-operation NOx limits and facility-specific flow assumptions?

- Issue: The proposal says warm-up, startup, and shutdown are covered, but the record is thinner on how those modes are identified and checked in practice.
Why it matters: Different limits apply in different modes. If the transition between warm-up and startup is hard to verify, enforceability gets weaker even if the numbers themselves are sound.
Question a commenter can ask: What specific data fields in quarterly reports will show when a unit moved from warm-up to startup and whether the correct limit was applied?

- Issue: EPA cites other Ozone Transport Region states to support Maryland's stringency, but the comparison is brief.
Why it matters: Interstate comparisons can be useful, but only if the facilities and averaging periods are comparable enough to support the conclusion.
Question a commenter can ask: Why are the cited Connecticut, New Jersey, New York, and Massachusetts limits technically comparable benchmarks for Maryland's two large combustors?

- Issue: The record gives limited public-facing information on how significant non-normal operating periods are in real life.
Why it matters: Warm-up may last from three to sixteen hours, while startup and shutdown are capped at three hours per occurrence. If those modes happen often, the alternative limits matter much more.
Question a commenter can ask: How often do warm-up, startup, and shutdown periods occur at each facility, and what share of annual operating time do they represent?
