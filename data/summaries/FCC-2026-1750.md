## What This Document Does

This FCC notice proposes to stop treating certain higher-risk entities as automatically authorized to provide domestic interstate telecommunications service. Today, the FCC's blanket domestic section `214` rule generally allows carriers to enter the market without first filing an individual application. The FCC now proposes to carve out entities on the federal `Covered List`, which is the list of communications equipment and services found to pose unacceptable national-security risk.

The main proposal is straightforward: entities on the Covered List would no longer get blanket domestic section `214` authority under `47 CFR 63.01(a)`. Instead, they would have to seek individual FCC approval if they want to provide domestic interstate telecommunications service.

The notice also asks several related questions that could make the rule broader:

* whether the exclusion should cover all Covered List entities or only those whose services, rather than equipment, are listed
* whether current providers using blanket authority should have that authority revoked
* whether the FCC should also exclude entities owned by, controlled by, or subject to the direction of a foreign adversary
* whether other carriers should be barred from interconnecting with excluded entities
* whether the FCC should extend similar restrictions into related areas, including some unlicensed wireless contexts

The FCC is therefore not just making a narrow text edit. It is testing a larger shift in how national-security concerns should affect domestic telecom entry and operation.

## Why It Matters

This matters because it would move some firms out of the default permission structure and into case-by-case review. That is a major policy change even if the number of directly affected firms is small. It also matters because the proposal could affect more than the listed firms themselves. Interconnected carriers, customers, resellers, and firms with complicated affiliate relationships could all feel the effects depending on how broadly the FCC writes the final rule.

The strongest part of the notice is its high-level rationale. The FCC can point to existing national-security determinations and say that automatic domestic operating authority may no longer be appropriate for entities already identified as posing unacceptable risk. That logic is easy to understand.

The weaker part of the record is implementation and fit. The notice does not provide much concrete evidence on how many domestic providers are affected, what customer switching costs might look like, how affiliate status will be identified, or how quickly interconnection consequences could be imposed without disruption. The FCC recognizes that these issues exist and asks for comment on several of them, but commenters still have room to ask for a more disciplined final record.

## Who Is Affected

* Entities on the Covered List that may want to provide domestic interstate telecommunications service under blanket authority.
* Current providers, if the FCC later decides to revoke existing blanket domestic section `214` authority rather than only changing future eligibility.
* Corporate affiliates, subsidiaries, assignees, and transferees connected to listed entities.
* Other telecommunications carriers that currently interconnect with, acquire from, or transfer assets with affected firms.
* Customers who could face service transitions if revocation or interconnection restrictions move faster than migration planning.
* Smaller providers and resellers that may have less capacity to absorb compliance ambiguity or rapid network changes.

Unknown from available text:

* how many current domestic carriers are directly implicated
* how many customers would need to switch providers under a revocation scenario
* what transition periods, if any, the FCC would adopt
* how aggressively the FCC would define “affiliate,” “subsidiary,” or foreign-adversary control

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

1. The FCC has not yet shown the practical fit between Covered List status and every proposed domestic-service consequence.
Why it matters: The Covered List is a real national-security signal, but the final rule still needs to explain why that signal justifies each separate consequence, especially for domestic carrier authority, affiliates, and future assignees.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What specific findings connect existing Covered List determinations to the proposed exclusion from blanket domestic section `214` authority for each category of affected entity?

2. The affiliate and control boundaries need much more clarity.
Why it matters: A rule that reaches current and future affiliates, subsidiaries, and entities included by reference can become hard to administer if the trigger conditions are not precise.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What ownership, control, or reference criteria will the FCC use to determine when an entity is covered, and what notice process will apply when status changes?

3. The individualized application path needs defined standards to be a real safeguard.
Why it matters: The FCC presents individual applications as the main alternative to blanket authority, but the notice does not yet say much about timing, evidentiary standards, or how the process works in practice.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What application contents, review deadlines, and decision criteria would govern an excluded entity seeking domestic section `214` approval?

4. Interconnection restrictions could create third-party customer harms if adopted without transition rules.
Why it matters: Other carriers and their customers may be affected even if they are not themselves the target of the national-security concern.
Practical question a commenter can ask: If the FCC bars interconnection, what transition periods, safe harbors, or customer-protection steps will it require before existing arrangements must end?

5. The proposal asks for cost information but does not provide much of it.
Why it matters: The FCC acknowledges that customers, excluded carriers, and interconnected carriers may face real costs, yet the record currently leaves many of those burdens unquantified.
Practical question a commenter can ask: What data will the FCC use to estimate switching costs, refiling burdens, and service-disruption risk before adopting a final rule?

## Evidence Quotes

* “We propose to amend § 63.01 ... to exclude, on a prospective basis, any entities identified on the Covered List ... from being authorized to provide interstate telecommunications services pursuant to blanket domestic section 214 authority.”
* “We tentatively conclude that the present and future public interest, convenience, and necessity would no longer be served by the grant of the Covered List entities' blanket domestic section 214 authority.”
* “We tentatively conclude that a broad exclusion of all entities identified on the Covered List is appropriate, and seek comment on that conclusion.”
* “We further propose that any entity so excluded should be able to submit an individual application seeking affirmative approval for domestic section 214 authority.”
* “We tentatively conclude that section 201(b) provides direct authority for the Commission to prohibit telecommunications carriers from interconnecting with entities on the Covered List...”
