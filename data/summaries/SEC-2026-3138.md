## What This Document Does

- This SEC proposal would reorganize public-company filer status into two main buckets: `large accelerated filers` and `non-accelerated filers`.
- It would sharply narrow who counts as a large accelerated filer. The notice asks whether a proposed `public float` threshold of `$2 billion` is appropriate, and it would also lengthen the `seasoning period` for large accelerated filer status from `12 months` to `60 months`.
- The proposal would eliminate `accelerated filer` and `smaller reporting company` as separate filer-status categories, while extending many scaled-disclosure accommodations now associated with smaller reporting companies and some emerging growth company relief to all non-accelerated filers.
- It would also make non-accelerated filers newly exempt from the `section 404(b)` auditor attestation requirement for internal control over financial reporting.
- The proposal creates a new subcategory of the smallest non-accelerated filers, using a `$35 million` total-assets threshold, and would give that group more time to file annual and quarterly reports.
- It also updates SEC small-entity definitions for Regulatory Flexibility Act purposes.

## Why It Matters

- This is a substantial burden-reduction proposal for public companies, especially smaller and newer ones. It is not just a technical cleanup.
- The SEC argues that the current framework is layered, confusing, and too costly for many issuers. The notice points to a decline in domestic public-company counts over time and repeated concerns from small-business forums about quarterly reporting, three years of audited financial statements, and the cost of `404(b)` compliance.
- The proposal's basic theory is that lighter reporting burdens may help more companies go public or remain public, while the largest and most market-followed firms would still carry the most demanding disclosure and timing obligations.
- The main caution is that the economic case is mixed in tone. The SEC says many benefits and costs are hard to quantify and that public data are limited, but it still presents large monetized benefit estimates. That gap between admitted uncertainty and exact-looking numbers is one of the best places for public comments.
- The other major issue is calibration. A `$2 billion` large-accelerated-filer threshold and a `60-month` seasoning period are big design choices. Those lines determine which firms lose faster filing deadlines, fuller disclosure requirements, and `404(b)` auditor attestation.

## Who Is Affected

- Public companies that currently fall into `large accelerated filer`, `accelerated filer`, `non-accelerated filer`, `smaller reporting company`, or `emerging growth company` frameworks.
- Companies near the proposed large-accelerated-filer threshold, because their disclosure and attestation obligations could change materially.
- Smaller public companies that may gain scaled disclosures, more time for periodic reports, and relief from `404(b)` auditor attestation.
- Investors, analysts, auditors, and underwriters, because the proposal may change the timing, amount, and assurance level of information available for a large group of issuers.
- Commenters with experience in IPO planning, ongoing SEC reporting, internal controls, audit fees, or small-company capital access are especially well positioned to add useful evidence.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

- The proposal uses a very high threshold for large accelerated filer status.
Why it matters: A `$2 billion` public-float threshold and `60-month` seasoning period could move a large number of companies out of the most demanding regime.
Question a commenter can ask: Why is `$2 billion` the right threshold, and what would investors gain or lose under lower alternatives or a shorter seasoning period?

- The notice predicts major savings from relief from `404(b)` auditor attestation.
Why it matters: The SEC estimates `1,596` registrants would become newly exempt, representing about `60 percent` of firms now subject to that requirement.
Question a commenter can ask: How much of the projected savings depends on issuers actually dropping attestation rather than keeping it voluntarily, and what investor-protection costs might follow?

- The monetized benefits look very precise even though the SEC says the data are limited.
Why it matters: The notice says many effects are difficult to quantify, yet it reports annualized monetized benefits of about `$1.87 billion` and annualized monetized costs of only about `$73,960`.
Question a commenter can ask: What assumptions, ranges, and omitted costs sit behind those figures, and how sensitive are they to different issuer and investor responses?

- The smallest-filer accommodation may involve arbitrary line drawing.
Why it matters: Using `$35 million` in total assets to define the smallest non-accelerated filers may help some firms while excluding similarly burdened firms just above the cutoff.
Question a commenter can ask: Why is total assets the best metric here, and did the SEC compare it against revenue, public float, or mixed thresholds?

- The proposal may reduce information available to investors.
Why it matters: Scaled disclosures, slower filings for the smallest firms, and reduced attestation can save money for issuers but may also reduce comparability and assurance.
Question a commenter can ask: What investor costs does the SEC expect from these disclosure reductions, and how should those costs be weighed against issuer savings?

## Evidence Quotes

- "The Securities and Exchange Commission (“Commission”) proposes amendments to streamline filer statuses for Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (“Exchange Act”) reporting companies into two primary categories: large accelerated filers and non-accelerated filers."
- "Many of the benefits and costs are difficult to quantify or estimate with any degree of certainty."
- "These difficulties are exacerbated by the limited public data that would inform predictions about how market participants may respond to the proposed rules."
- "We estimate that the proposed amendments would result in 1,596 registrants (26.7 percent of all registrants) being newly exempt from the ICFR auditor attestation requirement under section 404(b) of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act."
- "We estimate that annualized total monetized benefits are $1,874,534,145 per year using both a three percent discount rate and a seven percent discount rate."
