## What This Document Does
- Proposes to reduce the IRS portion of the enrolled agent special enrollment examination fee from $99 per part to $66 per part.
- Leaves the contractor-administered portion of the exam price outside this rule, even though the record says that contractor charge will be $251 per part for the coming testing period.
- Uses the same-day interim final rule as the main source of the cost methodology, while this NPRM invites public comment before a final rule is issued.
- Grounds the fee amount in the Independent Offices Appropriation Act and OMB Circular A-25, which require user fees to recover the government's full cost of providing the service.

## Why It Matters
- This is a narrow fee-setting proposal, but it matters to anyone seeking enrolled-agent status through the written exam.
- The IRS-side story is simple: the agency says its own cost of overseeing each exam part has fallen, so it should lower its fee to match that lower cost.
- The more important practical story is that candidates do not experience the IRS fee in isolation. The record says the contractor fee rises from $168 per part in the prior testing period to $251 per part in the coming one. So even though the IRS is proposing a lower federal fee, the total amount a candidate pays per exam part still appears likely to be much higher.
- The rule therefore creates a useful comment opportunity. The strongest questions are not about whether IRS may charge a fee at all. The stronger questions are whether the record explains the new $66 figure clearly enough, and whether it gives enough attention to the real-world effect of the combined price candidates must pay.
- The analytical record is decent but not exhaustive. The interim final rule includes a cost table, an overhead rate, and a projected exam volume. That is more than a bare assertion. But the record says the lower fee is driven mainly by a "change in timekeeping methodology" without explaining that change in much detail. That is the clearest place where commenters can ask the agency to show more work.

## Who Is Affected
- Individuals who want to become enrolled agents by taking the three-part special enrollment examination.
- Tax professionals, bookkeepers, payroll practitioners, and career changers deciding whether to pursue the credential.
- Small tax firms or solo practices that reimburse or encourage employees to obtain enrolled-agent status.
- IRS and Treasury only indirectly address firm-level effects because the notice treats the fee as primarily affecting individual test takers rather than businesses.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. The record says the fee reduction is mainly due to a changed timekeeping methodology, but it does not explain that methodological change in much detail.
- Why it matters: If the new timekeeping approach materially changes how staff effort is counted, commenters should be able to understand why the new method better reflects full cost.
- Practical question to ask: What specifically changed in the timekeeping methodology, and how did that change lower the estimated cost per exam part?

2. The notice focuses on the IRS fee reduction without fully discussing the much higher total price candidates will face once the contractor fee is included.
- Why it matters: A candidate decides whether to sit for the exam based on the total price, not only the federal share of that price.
- Practical question to ask: Please discuss the expected effect of the combined IRS and contractor charges on exam participation and access to the enrolled-agent credential.

3. The cost-per-part estimate relies on historical exam volume, but the record does not say much about whether those volumes will hold after the contractor price increase.
- Why it matters: If exam participation drops, the denominator used to justify the $66 fee may no longer reflect actual program volume.
- Practical question to ask: Did the agency test whether the projected exam-part volume remains reasonable after the contractor fee increase for the May 2026 to February 2027 testing period?

4. The small-entity discussion may be too narrow if small firms often support employees seeking the credential.
- Why it matters: Even if only individuals can become enrolled agents, small employers may still bear reimbursement or training costs.
- Practical question to ask: Did the agency evaluate how higher all-in exam costs affect small firms that sponsor employee credentialing?
