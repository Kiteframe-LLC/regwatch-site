## What This Document Does

- EPA finalized a one-time TSCA section 8(d) reporting rule for 16 chemicals.
- The rule requires manufacturers, including importers, to submit certain unpublished health and safety studies and study lists to EPA.
- EPA says the studies will support later chemical work under TSCA section 6, including prioritization, risk evaluation, and risk management.
- The rule is broad. It reaches firms that made or proposed to make the listed chemicals within the prior 10 years, not just current manufacturers.
- It also reaches some studies involving mixtures, articles, and impurity contexts, as long as the listed chemical is specifically identified in the study.
- EPA requires OECD Harmonized Template formatting for certain health and environmental effects information when the submission includes confidential-business-information claims and a template exists.

## Why It Matters

- This is an information-forcing rule. EPA is not banning these chemicals here; it is trying to collect unpublished studies that may change how the agency evaluates them later.
- That means the value of the rule depends on two things at once: EPA needs the studies badly enough to justify the reporting burden, and the reporting scope needs to be tailored well enough that firms do not spend large amounts of time searching for low-value material.
- EPA says the first-year burden is about `$5.884 million` and `70,630` hours. It also says `35` small businesses are affected, with `2` potentially above the `1% of revenue` impact level.
- The record looks real, not improvised. The export includes EPA's final-rule economic analysis and related support documents, so this is not a bare assertion-only rule.
- The better criticism is narrower: EPA explains why it wants more data more clearly than it explains why some of its broadest scope choices are necessary.

## Who Is Affected

- Manufacturers and importers of the 16 listed chemicals.
- Firms that handled those chemicals in the prior 10 years and may hold older unpublished studies.
- Companies that may need to search records across multiple sites, review studies for confidentiality, and prepare electronic submissions.
- Businesses dealing with these chemicals as impurities or within mixtures or articles, where reporting scope may be harder to interpret.
- EPA staff and outside stakeholders who will later rely on the submitted studies for TSCA decisions.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)

- Issue: EPA kept reporting scope broad by covering impurity-only manufacture and any chemical grade or purity level.
Why it matters: Those choices may capture useful risk information, but they can also create expensive search obligations in low-value contexts.
Question to ask: What evidence shows that impurity-only and any-purity reporting will produce enough high-value studies to justify the added burden?

- Issue: EPA says only studies in which the listed chemical is specifically identified must be reported, but the line between responsive and non-responsive studies may still be hard to apply in mixtures and articles.
Why it matters: Ambiguity here can increase compliance cost and lead to uneven reporting across firms.
Question to ask: Will EPA provide clearer implementation guidance or examples for mixture, article, and impurity scenarios?

- Issue: EPA requires OECD Harmonized Template formatting for covered CBI studies, but not for non-CBI studies.
Why it matters: That reduces burden for some submitters, but it may also leave EPA with less standardized non-CBI data that are harder to compare and use.
Question to ask: Did EPA consider a lighter standardized format for non-CBI studies that would improve comparability without imposing full OHT burden?

- Issue: EPA added 6PPD-quinone based on a citizen petition and aquatic-harm concerns, but the visible record is thinner on expected respondent numbers and study inventories for that chemical.
Why it matters: If the burden estimate for that chemical is especially uncertain, the overall forecast may be less reliable than it looks.
Question to ask: How did EPA estimate the likely reporting universe and expected study volume for 6PPD-quinone?

- Issue: The burden analysis relies heavily on prior reporting-cycle assumptions and analogies.
Why it matters: If actual reporting rates or study counts are higher than EPA expects, small-entity and workload impacts could be understated.
Question to ask: What post-implementation monitoring will EPA use to determine whether actual burdens materially exceed the forecast?
