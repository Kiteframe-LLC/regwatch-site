# Public Summary: CMS-2026-1255-0001

_Source file: `summaries/CMS-2026-1255-0001.md`_

## What This Document Does
- Proposes to expand federal interoperability and prior authorization requirements so affected payers must support electronic prior authorization for drugs.
- Uses two standards tracks:
  - HL7 FHIR and Da Vinci implementation guides for drugs covered under a medical benefit.
  - NCPDP SCRIPT, Formulary & Benefit, and Real-Time Prescription Benefit standards for drugs covered under a pharmacy benefit.
- Would apply to Medicare Advantage organizations, state Medicaid and CHIP fee-for-service programs, Medicaid managed care plans, CHIP managed care entities, and Federally-facilitated Exchange QHP issuers, including small group FF-SHOP issuers.
- Also proposes API endpoint reporting, API usage metrics, prior authorization denial-reason communication, shortened QHP prior authorization decision timeframes, Open Payments audit-access penalties, HIPAA transaction-standard updates, and ONC health IT standards adoption.
- Includes RFIs on event notifications, health care resiliency, payer API implementation, step therapy, laboratory tests, and durable medical equipment/prosthetics/orthotics/supplies.

## Why It Matters
- Drug prior authorization is a major source of patient and provider burden. This proposal attempts to make drug prior authorization more electronic, consistent, and measurable.
- The rule would affect both payer compliance systems and provider-facing workflows. If implemented well, it could reduce manual portals, unclear documentation demands, and delays.
- The hardest implementation issue is routing: the rule depends on whether a drug is covered under a medical benefit or a pharmacy benefit. Some drugs and plan designs may not fit neatly into one category.
- The proposal is economically significant under Executive Order 12866. CMS/HHS includes a substantial RIA, but many important estimates depend on assumptions that commenters can test.
- API usage metrics could make the policy more accountable, but only if they measure actual successful use, errors, response times, and provider burden rather than just API availability.

## Who Is Affected
- Medicare Advantage organizations and MA-PD plans.
- State Medicaid and CHIP fee-for-service programs.
- Medicaid managed care plans and CHIP managed care entities.
- QHP issuers on the Federally-facilitated Exchanges, including small group market QHP issuers on FF-SHOPs.
- Health IT vendors, EHR developers, pharmacy systems, providers, prescribers, pharmacies, patients, and enrollees affected by drug prior authorization workflows.
- Applicable manufacturers and group purchasing organizations under Open Payments, for the proposed "failure to report" audit-access CMP definition.

## Issues Worth Commenting On (0-6)
1. Standards routing for ambiguous drugs.
   - Why it matters: The proposal depends on whether a drug is handled through a medical-benefit FHIR workflow or pharmacy-benefit NCPDP workflow. Ambiguous products could cause failed transactions or inconsistent payer behavior.
   - Practical question to ask: Will CMS provide a decision tree, examples, and fallback rules for drugs or plan designs that do not fit cleanly into either category?

2. API usage metrics.
   - Why it matters: Metrics can help CMS evaluate whether the rule reduces burden, but vague metrics may only show call volume or API availability.
   - Practical question to ask: Will CMS require metrics for successful completion, failed transactions, response time, denial-reason completeness, and provider-facing burden?

3. Implementation timing.
   - Why it matters: October 1, 2027 may be workable for some payers but difficult for state programs, smaller issuers, and vendor-dependent systems facing multiple standards changes.
   - Practical question to ask: Should CMS add testing milestones, phased compliance, or extension criteria tied to documented implementation barriers?

4. Cost assumptions and sensitivity analysis.
   - Why it matters: The RIA includes quantified costs, but several assumptions, including estimates for adding drug functionality to existing APIs, may vary widely across organizations.
   - Practical question to ask: What data supports the agency's implementation-cost assumptions, and how do costs change for small payers, state programs, and vendors?

5. Versioning and interoperability.
   - Why it matters: Allowing any unexpired version of incorporated standards may ease transition but could create compatibility issues if trading partners choose different versions.
   - Practical question to ask: How will CMS and ONC prevent version drift from undermining interoperability across payers, providers, and vendors?

6. Small-entity and provider-facing effects.
   - Why it matters: The IRFA addresses small entities, but workflow burdens may fall unevenly on small plans, rural providers, safety-net providers, and smaller technology vendors.
   - Practical question to ask: Will CMS provide more granular analysis of how implementation costs and workflow changes affect small or under-resourced entities?

## Evidence Quotes
- "We are proposing to require that impacted payers support electronic prior authorization for all drugs that require prior authorization."
- "The FHIR standard, including the IGs, and the three NCPDP standards, all facilitate electronic prior authorization for drugs; which standards set is applicable depends on whether the drug is covered under the payer's medical benefit or its pharmacy benefit."
- "We propose that impacted payers be required to support electronic prior authorization for all drugs through the Prior Authorization API and NCPDP standards beginning on October 1, 2027."
- "To help assess the impact of our policies, we are proposing to collect API usage metrics."
- "Based on our estimates, the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (OIRA) has determined this proposed rulemaking to be significant per section 3(f)(1) of Executive Order 12866."
